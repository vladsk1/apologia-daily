The Cambrian Explosion

The deep dive tells you what the argument is. This track makes you able to use it — reconstruct it from memory, answer its hardest objections, and hold the line in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

The Cambrian explosion refers to a remarkable interval in the fossil record, roughly 538 million years ago, when most of the major animal body plans (phyla) appear over a geologically short window without obvious ancestral forms in the layers beneath. Charles Darwin himself recognised the pattern as a difficulty for his theory, and the discovery of fossil sites such as the Burgess Shale and Chengjiang has sharpened rather than dissolved the puzzle. The argument from design draws on this abrupt arrival of complex, information-rich life and asks whether a gradual, unguided process is the best explanation.

The deeper point concerns biological information. On the design argument, building new body plans requires new proteins and new gene regulatory networks, and functional protein sequences appear to be rare within the vast space of possible sequences. Some design proponents cite Douglas Axe's estimates here — though it is important to be candid that those figures are contested by a number of biochemists, and the debate is live. The argument does not claim certainty; it claims that the geologically rapid origin of so much specified complexity is, on the design view, better explained by an intelligent cause than by blind search alone.

Mainstream biologists offer serious replies: soft-bodied precursors that fossilise poorly, the Ediacaran biota, developmental-genetic toolkits, and rising oxygen levels. These are genuine responses worth weighing carefully. Working biologists do not regard the Cambrian explosion as evidence against evolution. Design is presented here as an inference to the best explanation — a conclusion drawn from the evidence, not a scientific consensus. Note the scope: this argument reaches an intelligent cause — not yet the Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Identifying that being with the God revealed in Christ is the work of the other arguments and of revelation. The fuller case for the Christian God rests above all on the historical evidence for the resurrection (see The Minimal Facts Argument). This is a design inference, not a doctrinal test: Christians who hold young-earth, old-earth and evolutionary-creation views all confess the same creeds, and this site does not adjudicate between them.

Before you start · key terms

Eight words this argument leans on

Learn these first — the objections below turn on them, and most weak versions of the argument come from blurring one of them.

The Cambrian explosionThe geologically rapid appearance of most major animal body plans, beginning around 538.8 million years ago and unfolding over roughly the first 20 to 25 million years of the Cambrian, with a denser pulse of body-plan innovation inside that window. It is a real landmark event, but slower and more layered than the word suggests: it was preceded by the Ediacaran biota, the small shelly fauna and complex trace fossils.
Phylum (body plan)A body plan at the highest level of architectural difference — the distinction between an insect and a starfish and a fish. The striking thing is not merely that the Cambrian animals are complex but that the deep, top-level differences among them appear early and close together, rather than accumulating slowly over hundreds of millions of years.
Specified information and dGRNsMeyer's argument is about information, not gaps in the fossil record (he grants the record is incomplete). Building a new body plan, he contends, requires new, functionally specified genetic information — new genes coding for new proteins, and, more demandingly, new developmental gene regulatory networks (dGRNs) that switch genes on and off in the right cells at the right times to assemble an embryo.
Inference to the best explanationThe argument's form: design is offered as the best current explanation of the Cambrian information, not as a deductive proof. The entire weight rests on the two premises, and it is there that the real debate lives — inference to the best explanation is how Darwin himself argued, and the god-of-the-gaps charge contests the form of the inference rather than the data.
Axe's rarity estimate (contested)The design biochemist Douglas Axe estimated, by extrapolating from experiments on a β-lactamase enzyme domain, that sequences performing a specific function by any domain-sized fold might be as rare as 1 in 10^77 — which, if correct, would make a blind mutational search prohibitively unlikely in the time available. It is genuinely contested: Arthur Hunt argues the experiment does not measure the global density of all functional sequences, and that functional proteins may be far commoner. It is one disputed estimate in a live technical dispute, and the case cannot lean on it as settled.
Evo-devo and regulatory rewiringThe mainstream developmental answer to Premise 2: evolutionary developmental biology showed that large morphological change need not require large amounts of brand-new information, because much body-plan diversity arises from rewiring an ancient, shared genetic toolkit (the Hox genes and other regulators), changing where and when existing genes are expressed. Marshall's central rebuttal is that building Cambrian animals may have required little new genetic information and much rearrangement of connections among genes already present.
God-of-the-gaps (and the positive reply)The charge, pressed by Marshall, that the inference reads design off what evolution has not yet explained; the phrase was popularized by the Christian mathematician and chemist Charles Coulson (1955), and evolutionary creationists press it today. The design reply is that the inference is meant to be positive — intelligence is the only known cause of large amounts of functionally specified information — but its force still depends on whether the naturalistic options are genuinely inadequate, which is the contested half.
Data conceded, design inferredThe data of the Cambrian explosion are not in serious dispute, and on the data the design movement and mainstream paleontology largely agree: complex animal body plans appear over a geologically short interval, and the full causal story of how their developmental programs originated is not yet known. What is contested is the inference to a mind; mainstream biology treats the event as a partly open chapter within evolutionary history, not as evidence against common descent.
The argument, formally

Three lines. The whole weight rests on the two premises.

P1

The geologically rapid Cambrian appearance of most major animal body plans, without the long graded series of intermediate architectures strict gradualism predicts, demands an adequate cause for that burst of biological information.

P2

Unguided mutation and selection are not an adequate cause of that information in the available time, whereas intelligent agency is a known and adequate cause of specified information.

∴ C

Therefore, the best current explanation of the Cambrian Explosion is an intelligent cause capable of producing functional biological information.

This is an inference to the best explanation, not a deductive proof — so the entire weight rests on the two premises, and it is there that the real debate lives; the god-of-the-gaps charge, by contrast, contests the form of the inference itself. Knowing what an objection targets is half the battle.

Then the conceptual step — what does the cause have to deliver?

Reasoning from the conclusion: building a new animal phylum isn't a tweak to an existing form — it requires coordinated new tissues, organs, and the genetic regulatory networks to assemble them in the right order. On the design argument, that is the kind of specified information whose only known cause, in our uniform experience, is intelligence—the step most biologists contest, holding that much body-plan diversity comes from rewiring an ancient, shared genetic toolkit.

Defending Premise 1

The geologically rapid Cambrian appearance of most major animal body plans, without the long graded series of intermediate architectures strict gradualism predicts, demands an adequate cause for that burst of biological information

Precision check: the logic is an inference to the best explanation, not a deductive proof; the entire weight rests on the two premises, and it is there that the real debate lives. The data of the Cambrian explosion are not in serious dispute; what is contested is the inference. Premise 1 is the empirical premise — and it is softer than the rhetoric of “sudden” suggests.

The fossil record itself The data · not in serious dispute

The Cambrian Period opens around 538.8 million years ago. Within roughly the first 20 to 25 million years of it—and with a particularly dense pulse of body-plan innovation in a narrower window—the fossil record fills with animals representing most of the major animal phyla that have a substantial fossil record: arthropods, mollusks, brachiopods, echinoderms, and our own group, the chordates. Two fossil sites anchor the picture: the Burgess Shale (Canada, c. 508 Mya) and the slightly older Chengjiang biota (Yunnan, China, c. 518 Mya) are Lagerstätten—sites of exceptional preservation where soft tissues, not just hard shells, were fossilized. They confirm that the Cambrian seas held real, anatomically sophisticated animals, not preservational illusions.

Honest limit: the word “explosion” is relative to geological time: 20 million years is a long while by any human reckoning. The mainstream paleontologists Douglas Erwin and James Valentine describe the event as a real and still incompletely explained episode of rapid morphological innovation — that a design proponent and the leading textbook authors agree the explosion is real and partly unexplained means the fossil pattern behind Premise 1 is not a fringe claim; but the further claim that the pattern required vast amounts of brand-new information is exactly what those authors, and the mainstream reply, dispute.

The “top-down” pattern and the missing roots Contested reading

The disparity (the major body-plan differences) appears at the start, with diversity (species variation) filling in later — on the design reading, the reverse of the gradual branching tree Darwin expected. Darwin himself called the absence of the expected ancestral forms beneath the Cambrian a serious difficulty for his theory: “The case at present must remain inexplicable; and may be truly urged as a valid argument against the views here entertained.” The Precambrian fossils found since (the Ediacaran biota) do not supply clear lineages for most Cambrian phyla.

Honest limit: the empirical premises are softer than the rhetoric of “sudden” suggests: a 20-million-year diversification, preceded by Ediacaran organisms, small shellies, complex trace fossils, and molecular-clock signals of deeper divergence, is a slower and more textured event than the word “explosion” conveys to a lay reader. The honest defender concedes this.

The information claim Meyer’s inference · disputed

Meyer’s case is not primarily about gaps in the fossil record; he grants that fossils are incomplete. His argument is about information. Building a new animal body plan, he contends, requires vast quantities of new, functionally specified genetic information—new genes coding for new proteins, and, more demandingly, new developmental gene regulatory networks (dGRNs) that switch those genes on and off in the right cells at the right times to assemble an embryo.

Honest limit: it depends for its force on empirical claims (the rarity of functional proteins, the rigidity of developmental networks, the adequacy of the available time) that remain disputed among qualified researchers.

Objection — the window was longer, and the fuse was lit earlier Hardest

Critics argue that the “explosion” is real but slower and more layered than the design framing suggests. The Cambrian diversification unfolds in stages over roughly 20–25 million years, and it was preceded by the Ediacaran biota (c. 575–539 Mya)—enigmatic soft-bodied organisms—and by the “small shelly fauna,” tiny skeletal fragments that appear in the earliest Cambrian before the larger animals. Trace fossils (burrows and tracks) record increasingly complex animal behavior across the Ediacaran–Cambrian boundary, implying mobile, bilaterally symmetric animals were already present before they left good body fossils. The geologist Donald Prothero has been especially pointed, arguing that what is called an explosion is better described as a protracted, stepwise diversification, and that Meyer underweights this earlier record.

Reply

The honest defender concedes that the empirical premises are softer than the rhetoric of “sudden” suggests. But against the roughly three billion years of microbial life that preceded it, the burst of animal architecture is abrupt enough that even its most committed naturalistic students call it, without embarrassment, an explosion. On the data the design movement and mainstream paleontology largely agree: complex animal body plans appear over a geologically short interval, and the full causal story of how their developmental programs originated is not yet known.

Objection — molecular clocks and preservation bias

Molecular phylogenetics—estimating divergence times from differences in DNA and protein sequences—generally dates the genetic splits among the major animal lineages well before the Cambrian, deep in the Ediacaran or earlier. On this view what “exploded” in the Cambrian was not the lineages themselves but their acquisition of fossilizable hard parts and larger size. Soft-bodied animals also fossilize only under rare conditions, so the absence of obvious Precambrian ancestors may reflect a thin record rather than the absence of ancestors. In his Science review the Berkeley paleontologist Charles Marshall argued that Meyer omits relevant fossils and “misunderstands the nuances of molecular phylogenetics,” both of which, Marshall wrote, “cause him to exaggerate the apparent suddenness of the Cambrian explosion.” Marshall also charged that Meyer’s handling of sources was selective: a passage Meyer quotes from one of Marshall’s own review papers, Marshall says, was stitched together from two separate sections, omitting its central conclusion that new genes were not the principal driver of the Cambrian radiation.

Reply

Meyer and the Discovery Institute disputed Marshall’s characterization of how Meyer handled his sources, replying that the quotation fairly conveyed Marshall’s point; readers can weigh the exchange for themselves. Even Marshall, in a later radio exchange, granted that the fossil-record portion of Meyer’s book reflects “good scholarship”—though this was a limited concession about the descriptive paleontology, not his overall verdict, which was sharply negative; his Science review is titled “When Prior Belief Trumps Scholarship.” The honest defender concedes this: molecular clocks place many genetic divergences deeper in the Precambrian.

The room: Meyer (Darwin’s Doubt, 2013) presses the information argument; Erwin and Valentine (The Cambrian Explosion, 2013) agree the explosion is real and partly unexplained; Prothero and Marshall press the window and the molecular clocks. The overwhelming majority of paleontologists and evolutionary biologists do not accept the design argument, and their reasons are substantial, not reflexive.
Defending Premise 2

Unguided mutation and selection are not an adequate cause of that information in the available time, whereas intelligent agency is a known and adequate cause of specified information

Precision check: this is where Meyer’s distinctive argument lives, and it is an inference to the best explanation, not a deductive proof. This inference is not a scientific consensus; its key supporting estimates (Axe’s in particular) are disputed by qualified specialists, and its central premise about the inadequacy of natural mechanisms is rejected by the working scientists who study the event most closely. To imply otherwise would misrepresent the field.

The rarity of functional proteins Contested estimate

The design biochemist Douglas Axe estimated, by extrapolating from experiments on a β-lactamase enzyme domain, that sequences performing a specific function by any domain-sized fold might be as rare as one in 1077—a vanishingly small fraction of all possible amino-acid sequences—which, if correct, would make a blind mutational search for new protein folds prohibitively unlikely in the time the Cambrian allows.

Honest limit: Axe’s rarity figure—load-bearing for the time-budget claim—is genuinely contested. Biochemists such as Arthur Hunt have argued that Axe’s experimental design does not measure what the argument needs it to measure (the global density of all functional sequences), and that functional proteins may be far less rare than 1 in 1077. If the targets are commoner, the time-budget objection loses much of its sting. A responsible presentation cannot lean on Axe’s number as though it were settled; it is one disputed estimate in a live technical dispute.

The developmental constraint Contested reading

Drawing on the work of the developmental biologist Eric Davidson (no design advocate), Meyer argues that the gene regulatory networks that govern early body-plan formation appear to be remarkably resistant to change, because the early, body-plan-defining steps are precisely the ones whose disruption is lethal. If the mutations capable of rewiring a body plan are the mutations that kill the embryo, then the mechanism seems to lack a viable route to new architectures.

Honest limit: this reading of developmental rigidity is itself contested: many developmental biologists hold that the conservation of regulatory “kernels” is compatible with substantial evolutionary change in the more peripheral, plastic parts of the networks.

The positive inference: what minds are known to do Inference to the best explanation

The inference is meant to be positive, not negative: it rests on the claim that intelligence is the only known cause of large amounts of functionally specified information, so that design is an argument from what minds are known to do, not merely from biology’s present ignorance. This is a real and respectable form of reasoning—inference to the best explanation is how Darwin himself argued.

Honest limit: its persuasiveness depends on both halves of the comparison—what minds are known to do, and whether the naturalistic options are genuinely inadequate—and the second half is precisely the empirical question the mainstream answers differently. The argument cannot escape the verdict of the science; it depends on it.

The origin of the developmental programs Open problem

The origin of the developmental programs that build animals remains a deep open problem. The defender is entitled to say that the naturalistic mechanisms on offer—oxygen, ecology, regulatory rewiring—describe enabling conditions and machinery far better than they demonstrate a step-by-step pathway by which the first dGRNs were assembled.

Honest limit: the consensus judgment of the relevant scientific community today is that the naturalistic resources are not exhausted—that this is an unsolved problem within evolutionary biology, not a refutation of it.

Objection — evo-devo: the information problem has a developmental answer Hardest

Mainstream biologists deny Premise 2 directly. The rise of evolutionary developmental biology (“evo-devo”) showed that large morphological changes need not require large amounts of brand-new genetic information: a great deal of body-plan diversity arises from rewiring the regulation of an ancient, shared genetic toolkit (the Hox genes and other regulators), changing where and when existing genes are expressed. Marshall’s central scientific rebuttal is precisely this: building Cambrian animals may have required relatively little new genetic information and much rearrangement of regulatory connections among genes already present. Add ecological triggers (the advent of predation and an evolutionary “arms race”) and a rise in atmospheric oxygen permitting larger, more active bodies, and the mainstream account offers a multi-causal, naturalistic story for why complex animals appeared when they did.

Reply

The defender adds that regulatory rewiring presupposes the very developmental logic whose origin is in question: rearranging an existing gene-regulatory network is not the same as explaining how that network first arose—and it is the origin of the dGRNs, not their later redeployment, that the argument presses. Whether the naturalistic options are genuinely inadequate is precisely the empirical question the mainstream answers differently. The argument cannot escape the verdict of the science; it depends on it.

Objection — “God of the gaps”

The charge is that the inference is a “god-of-the-gaps”—that it infers design from what evolution has not yet explained. Marshall presses exactly this. The warning is not only a critic’s: the phrase “God of the gaps” was popularized by the Christian mathematician and chemist Charles Coulson (Science and Christian Belief, 1955), and evolutionary creationists press the objection today.

Reply

The design reply is that the inference is meant to be positive, not negative: it rests on the claim that intelligence is the only known cause of large amounts of functionally specified information. But its persuasiveness depends on both halves of the comparison, and the second half is precisely the empirical question the mainstream answers differently. The consensus judgment of the relevant scientific community today is that this is an unsolved problem within evolutionary biology, not a refutation of it.

The room: Meyer and Axe defend the inference; Hunt presses the rarity figure, Marshall the evo-devo answer, Coulson’s phrase the god-of-the-gaps charge. Apologia Daily takes no institutional position binding the Christian faith to intelligent design as science, nor to any particular reading of the timescales of Genesis; faithful Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant believers—old-earth, young-earth, and evolutionary-creationist alike—hold the Nicene faith in common while differing on these natural-historical questions. The argument establishes far less than its strongest advocates sometimes suggest. It does not show that evolution is false, that the explosion was instantaneous, or that scientists agree the Cambrian animals were designed—they do not. Scope: even at its strongest the argument reaches a purposing designer, not yet the Triune God of the creeds.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

This is what separates someone who knows the Cambrian argument from someone who can defend it. Each gets its best form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged just below.

The one that matters most: that the window is 20–25 million years with real Ediacaran precursors, and evo-devo denies the developmental premise. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"It wasn't an explosion — it took tens of millions of years" Common

Strongest form

Calling it an "explosion" is rhetoric. The Cambrian and the Ediacaran before it span tens of millions of years — ample time for gradual evolution. The fuse was long even if the burst looks sharp. Trace fossils imply mobile bilaterians before good body fossils, and Donald Prothero argues it was a protracted, stepwise diversification.

Reply

Concede what is true: a 20-million-year diversification (Erwin and Valentine), preceded by Ediacaran organisms, small shellies, complex trace fossils, and molecular-clock signals of deeper divergence, is a slower and more textured event than the word “explosion” conveys. But against roughly three billion years of microbial life, the burst is abrupt enough that its most committed naturalistic students still call it an explosion—and the argument was never chiefly about the clock. It is about the origin of the developmental programs that build animals, which mainstream paleontologists themselves count a deep open problem. A longer fuse makes that question less dramatic; it does not answer it.

"The ancestors existed but weren't fossilized" Trickier

Strongest form

Soft-bodied Precambrian ancestors simply didn't fossilize well. Absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence — the lineages were there; we just can't see them.

Reply

This was reasonable in Darwin's day, but the Precambrian record is no longer blank: we have exquisite microfossils, embryos (the Doushantuo formation), and the Ediacaran biota — soft tissues do preserve when conditions allow. We find the small soft things; what we don't find is the graded series of intermediate body plans the gradual story predicts. But concede what is true: the Doushantuo embryos show delicate organisms can preserve, but only patchily, which makes a sparse ancestral record unsurprising on the mainstream view. So the design case should not rest chiefly on missing fossils—Meyer himself grants the record is incomplete; its weight is on the information question.

"Molecular clocks show the lineages diverged much earlier" Trickier

Strongest form

Genetic comparisons (molecular phylogenetics) date the splits among animal groups well back into the Precambrian. So the divergences happened gradually and deep in time; only the hard parts appeared suddenly.

Reply

Molecular clocks are calibrated against the fossil record and carry large uncertainties that depend on calibration and model assumptions (dos Reis et al., 2015) — they're a model output, not a direct observation. More importantly, even if genes diverged earlier, the argument is about the origin of the morphological information for new body plans, which still has to be built. Pushing back a sequence divergence date doesn't supply a mechanism for assembling a new animal architecture.

"New mechanisms — evo-devo, gene regulation, oxygen — explain it naturally" Hard

Strongest form

Modern biology has moved beyond mutation-plus-selection. Evolutionary developmental biology, changes to gene regulatory networks (GRNs), ecological triggers, and a rise in atmospheric oxygen together provide a rich, sufficient natural explanation. The argument attacks an outdated version of the theory. Charles Marshall’s central rebuttal: building Cambrian animals may have required relatively little new genetic information and much rearrangement of regulatory connections among genes already present.

Reply

These are real and important — but they describe enabling conditions and machinery far better than they demonstrate a step-by-step pathway to the origin of the information. Oxygen and ecology may explain why complex animals could be sustained, not how their genetic instructions arose. And the defender adds that regulatory rewiring presupposes the very developmental logic whose origin is in question: rearranging an existing gene-regulatory network is not the same as explaining how that network first arose—and it is the origin of the developmental gene regulatory networks, not their later redeployment, that the argument presses. Here the argument cannot escape the verdict of the science; it depends on it. Naming the machinery doesn't yet name a cause that can rewrite it constructively.

"This is a god-of-the-gaps — design isn't science" Hard

Strongest form

Inferring a designer from what we can't yet explain is the god-of-the-gaps fallacy. Science fills gaps with natural causes over time; invoking intelligence halts inquiry and isn't testable.

Reply

The inference isn't from ignorance but from positive knowledge: in every case where we know the cause of large amounts of functional, specified information — software, language, codes — that cause is a mind. Design is offered as the best explanation given the only kind of cause known to produce the effect. That is a real and respectable form of reasoning—inference to the best explanation is how Darwin himself argued. But its persuasiveness depends on both halves of the comparison—what minds are known to do, and whether the naturalistic options are genuinely inadequate—and the second half is precisely the empirical question the mainstream answers differently. It doesn't halt science any more than inferring a human cause halts archaeology.

The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

The data of the Cambrian explosion are not in serious dispute, and on the data the design movement and mainstream paleontology largely agree: complex animal body plans appear over a geologically short interval, and the full causal story of how their developmental programs originated is not yet known. What is contested is the inference. Stephen Meyer (Darwin's Doubt, 2013) presses the design argument from the origin of biological information; the mainstream paleontologists Douglas Erwin and James Valentine (The Cambrian Explosion, 2013) agree the event is real and partly unexplained while disputing the information claim; the geologist Donald Prothero argues the radiation was protracted and stepwise and that Meyer underweights the earlier record; the Berkeley paleontologist Charles Marshall, in his Science review "When Prior Belief Trumps Scholarship," argues Meyer omits relevant fossils and "misunderstands the nuances of molecular phylogenetics," and that much of the diversity came from rewiring genes already present; the biochemist Arthur Hunt contests Douglas Axe's rarity figure. The overwhelming majority of paleontologists and evolutionary biologists do not accept the design inference, and their reasons are substantial, not reflexive.

A fairness most defenders skip: Honest defenders concede the observations. The empirical premises are softer than the rhetoric of "sudden" suggests: a 20-to-25-million-year diversification, preceded by Ediacaran organisms, small shellies, complex trace fossils and molecular-clock signals of deeper divergence, is slower and more textured than "explosion" conveys. Douglas Axe's rarity figure — load-bearing for the time-budget claim — is genuinely contested, and a responsible presentation cannot lean on it as though it were settled. The developmental answer (evo-devo), rising oxygen and ecological triggers are real responses worth weighing. But the inference does not follow from the concessions: against roughly three billion years of microbial life the burst is still abrupt; regulatory rewiring presupposes the very developmental logic whose origin is in question; and the origin of the developmental programs that build animals remains, by mainstream admission, a deep open problem. The concession is about the tempo and about one disputed number, not about the hard question at the centre — and the hard question is no proof of design either.

The bounded verdict: The Cambrian explosion is a real and remarkable event, and the origin of the developmental information that builds animal body plans is, by the admission of mainstream paleontologists, a genuine and partly open scientific problem. But the argument establishes far less than its strongest advocates sometimes suggest: it does not show that evolution is false, that the explosion was instantaneous, or that scientists agree the Cambrian animals were designed — they do not. At most it raises a serious philosophical challenge about the origin of biological information, one whose force depends on empirical claims (the rarity of functional proteins, the rigidity of developmental networks, the adequacy of the available time) that remain disputed among qualified researchers. It is best held as a live and interesting inference, offered with intellectual humility and without overstatement — and even at its strongest it reaches a purposing designer, not yet the Triune God of the creeds. The fuller case for the Christian God rests on revelation and, above all, on the historical evidence for the resurrection; this site takes no position binding the faith to intelligent design or to any reading of the timescales of Genesis.

    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    The in-house debate is real, and it should be stated accurately — but it is the opposite of what the design literature often makes of it. This is the "extended evolutionary synthesis," and the additions it proposes — developmental plasticity, niche construction, epigenetic inheritance, self-organization — are every one of them natural mechanisms, not design, and emphatically not a challenge to common descent, which its proponents affirm. Several have publicly repudiated the design movement's use of their work: the scientists of the "Third Way of Evolution" state that their website "is wrongly being referenced by proponents of Intelligent Design and creationist ideas" and that they reject any appeal to "supernatural intervention." So the move cuts the other way: these researchers are calling for more natural mechanisms folded into evolutionary biology, not for a mind, and not against common descent. The bounded verdict follows exactly: that a mechanism-debate is live is not evidence for design; it is evidence that the naturalistic research programme is very much alive.
