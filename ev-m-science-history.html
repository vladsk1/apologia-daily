Did Christianity Give Us Science?

The deep dive tells you what the argument is. This track makes you able to use it — reconstruct it from memory, answer its hardest objections, and hold the line in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

Almost everyone knows the story before they know any of the facts: faith and science as ancient enemies, the church against Galileo, dogma dragging its heels while reason advances. It is one of the most confident assumptions a modern person carries. And it has a curious feature — the men who actually founded modern science (Copernicus the cathedral canon, Kepler the would-be minister, Galileo the devout Catholic, Newton writing theology by candlelight) would not have recognised the war they are supposed to have fought. Most of them believed they were studying nature because it was the handiwork of God.

The “conflict thesis” is not ancient; it has a birthday. Two nineteenth-century bestsellers — Draper (1874) and White (1896) — cast Western history as a running war between faith and reason, and historians (many with no religious stake) have since retired that picture as bad history. In its place stands a more careful, contested claim: that certain Christian convictions — a free Creator who made a contingent world you must go and look at, an intelligible ordered creation, and a fallen mind that must be checked — supplied fertile preconditions for the scientific enterprise. This is not “no Christianity, no science,” and it takes nothing from the Greek and Islamic learning the enterprise also stands on.

The Galileo affair is a genuine scar, and this track owns it without flinching: the Roman Catholic authorities of that moment were wrong to condemn him and wrong to bind Scripture to one astronomy. But one tragedy is not a three-century war. And the honest bound holds — that a worldview proved intellectually fruitful is not proof that it is true; good and true are different things. What the history dissolves is a barrier, not a proof. The case that Christianity is true rests on the resurrection, argued on its own evidence elsewhere in the Library.

Before you start · key terms

Eight words this argument leans on

Learn these first — the objections below turn on them, and most weak versions of the argument come from blurring one of them.

The conflict (“warfare”) thesisThe confident modern assumption that faith and science are old enemies, locked in a long war — the church against Galileo, superstition against the telescope, dogma dragging its heels while reason advances. It is not ancient: it has a birthday, an author, and a sequel. Over the last half-century, historians of science — including many with no religious stake at all — have largely abandoned the “warfare” picture as bad history.
Draper and WhiteThe two nineteenth-century bestsellers that built the conflict thesis: John William Draper’s History of the Conflict between Religion and Science (1874) and Andrew Dickson White’s A History of the Warfare of Science with Theology in Christendom (1896). They were enormously influential — and, as history, largely wrong, as specialists now say bluntly. The imagery is older still (Jefferson, 1820, wrote that the priests “dread the advance of science as witches do the approach of day-light”).
The preconditions thesisThe careful, contested claim that certain Christian theological convictions supplied intellectual preconditions that made the scientific enterprise thinkable (Hooykaas, Harrison). It is not that Christianity single-handedly manufactured science — the strong slogan “no Christianity, no science” overreaches badly — but that its picture of a rational, contingent, intelligible creation was fertile soil in which the enterprise could take root and last.
A free Creator — so you must go and lookThe first of the three convictions. If God made the world by choice rather than necessity, then the world is contingent — it could have been otherwise — and you cannot deduce its workings by pure reason from your armchair, as the Greeks so often tried. You have to go and look. The other two convictions are an intelligible, ordered creation open to minds made in God’s image, and human fallibility — a fallen mind that must be checked, replicated, and disciplined against self-deception.

The argument, formally

Three lines. The whole weight rests on the two premises.

P1

The supposed war between science and Christian faith is a modern invention — the founders of modern science were overwhelmingly Christians who studied nature because they believed it was God’s handiwork, and modern specialists, many of them secular, have retired the “conflict thesis” as bad history.

P2

Certain Christian theological convictions — a free Creator who made a contingent world you must go and look at, an intelligible ordered creation, and human fallibility that must be checked — supplied intellectual preconditions in which the enterprise could take root and last (the careful thesis, not “no Christianity, no science”; Greek and Islamic learning were indispensable too).

∴ C

Therefore the assumption that a thinking person must choose between the laboratory and the church is false — modern science was born in a Christian context, not against it. This dissolves a barrier; it does not prove the faith is true, because fruitful and true are different things.

This is a historical inference, and its conclusion is deliberately bounded: it dissolves the “ancient enemies” assumption, and it stops there. It is not an argument that Christianity is true — a worldview can be fruitful without being correct.

Then the honest step — what fruitfulness does and does not show

That a worldview grew science shows it was intellectually fertile; it never shows its claims are true. Keep the two apart.

Defending Premise 1

The supposed war between science and Christian faith is a modern invention — the founders of modern science were overwhelmingly Christians who studied nature because they believed it was God’s handiwork, and modern specialists, many of them secular, have retired the “conflict thesis” as bad history.

Precision check: this is a historical claim, made carefully. It is not that the church never erred — the Galileo affair “is a genuine scar, and it should be owned without flinching” — and not that every founder was orthodox. It is that “one scar is not a war.”

The conflict thesis has a birthday, an author, and a sequel Strong

In 1874 the American chemist John William Draper published History of the Conflict between Religion and Science, a bestseller that cast the whole of Western history as a running battle between free inquiry and (chiefly Roman Catholic) faith. Two decades later Andrew Dickson White gave the thesis its monument: A History of the Warfare of Science with Theology in Christendom (1896). These books were enormously influential. They are also, as history, largely wrong — and specialists now say so bluntly.

Honest limit: the thesis’s imagery is older than either book (Jefferson, in 1820, wrote that the priests “dread the advance of science as witches do the approach of day-light”).

The correction is mainstream, not apologetics Strong

The correction is not a fringe Christian complaint; it is the mainstream of the discipline. The historian Ronald Numbers — himself no apologist for religion — edited Galileo Goes to Jail and Other Myths about Science and Religion, in which twenty-five scholars dismantle the set-piece stories one by one, from the flat-earth legend to the claim that the church banned human dissection. A more recent volume, The Warfare between Science and Religion: The Idea That Wouldn’t Die, edited by Jeff Hardin, Numbers, and Ronald Binzley, traces how a thesis the experts reject has survived so stubbornly in the popular mind. The title says it all: the warfare model persists not because the evidence supports it but because it is a useful story.

Honest limit: historians are wary of every tidy slogan here. John Hedley Brooke’s framework, dubbed the “complexity thesis”, holds that there is no single relationship between science and religion to be told as conflict or as harmony, only many particular entanglements, each to be studied in its own setting — a verdict that tells as much against a tidy Christian success story as against the warfare myth. That wariness is exactly what makes their verdict on the warfare thesis worth trusting.

The founders’ own faith Illustrative · not a proof

Nicolaus Copernicus was a canon of Frombork cathedral, and he dedicated On the Revolutions of the Heavenly Spheres (1543) to Pope Paul III. Johannes Kepler, who had trained for the Lutheran ministry, understood his astronomy as an act of worship. Galileo, a believing Catholic to the end, argued in his 1615 Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina that God had written two books, Scripture and nature, and that they could not finally conflict because both had the same Author. And Isaac Newton closed the Principia not with an equation but with a doxology: “This most beautiful system of the sun, planets, and comets could only proceed from the counsel and dominion of an intelligent and powerful Being.” The people who built modern science were not straining against their religion. Most of them were expressing it.

Honest limit: Newton’s private theology was in fact heterodox — he privately rejected the Trinity — a reminder that these were individuals, not a party line, and that the argument here needs no one to have been orthodox. The point is not that their faith proved their physics, nor that they were all orthodox; it is simply that the caricature is false.

Objection — “The Galileo affair proves the church was at war with science”

If the warfare thesis has one battle it can always name, it is Galileo. In 1616 the Roman Inquisition’s theologians judged the claim that the sun stands still to be “foolish and absurd in philosophy, and formally heretical,” and Copernicus’s book was suspended on the Index; in 1633 Galileo was tried, made to abjure his view, and confined to house arrest for the rest of his life.

Reply

Concede it first: this was a real injustice and a real error. The Roman Catholic authorities were wrong to condemn him, wrong to bind the interpretation of Scripture to a particular astronomy, and wrong to silence a man for being, as it turned out, right. No Christian account worth reading pretends otherwise. But the clean fable of enlightened science versus benighted faith does not survive contact with the details. In 1616 the evidence did not yet favour Copernicus. His 1543 system still used perfect circles and a thicket of epicycles, and it predicted the planets no better than the old Ptolemaic model; and Tycho Brahe’s geo-heliocentric system fit the same observations — including the phases of Venus — while keeping the earth still. Even the physical proof of the earth’s motion that Galileo set out in his 1632 Dialogue, an account of the tides, was mistaken. In 1616 the church sided with the scientific majority of the day — which is not a defence of the verdict, but it does explode the idea that faith was simply refusing an obvious fact. The conflict, as the leading Galileo scholar Maurice Finocchiaro observes, ran across both institutions: progressives and conservatives in the church and in the academy alike, not a tidy front of religion against reason. Jesuit astronomers at the Collegio Romano confirmed Galileo’s telescopic observations; his fiercest opponents included secular Aristotelian professors. That does not exonerate the church: its error was not caution but jurisdiction — it converted a legitimate scientific reservation into a charge of heresy and bound the reading of Scripture to it. The other name the legend invokes, Giordano Bruno, burned in 1600, does not change the picture: Bruno was condemned chiefly for a cluster of charges the Inquisition judged heretical — among them denying the Trinity, the divinity of Christ, and transubstantiation — not for Copernicanism as such; his cosmology of innumerable worlds did figure among the accusations, and how much weight it carried is debated, but Bruno was no working astronomer and his trial was not about evidence for a moving earth.

Nor was resistance to a moving earth a Roman peculiarity: Philip Melanchthon, Luther’s colleague at Wittenberg, rejected it as contrary to Scripture in his 1549 textbook of physics, even as his own circle put Copernicus’s mathematics to work — Rheticus had urged the book into print, and Erasmus Reinhold built his Prutenic Tables (1551) on it. The episode is double-edged: it rebukes a faith that overreaches its competence and clings to a scientific claim as though the gospel depended on it, and it rebukes the warfare myth that turns one Italian tragedy into a law of history. One thing it is not is a weapon for one branch of the church against another: the error was the Roman authorities’ of that moment, and the honest response is neither anti-Catholic point-scoring nor Protestant self-congratulation, but a shared caution against ever again mistaking the science of the hour for the word of God.

Objection — “They were all believers only because everyone was”

In a Europe where nearly everyone was baptised, the founders of science being members of the church would prove little on its own.

Reply

That is granted: it would prove little on its own. What matters is that so many of them said, in their own writings, that faith was their reason for looking.

The room: Ronald Numbers, no apologist for religion, edited the volume that dismantles the set-piece myths, and with Hardin and Binzley traced the thesis’s survival. John Hedley Brooke’s “complexity thesis” keeps every side honest. Maurice Finocchiaro reads the Galileo conflict as running across church and academy alike; the Orthodox writer David Bentley Hart calls it less a war of science against faith than a collision of egos, personality, and politics. On Giordano Bruno, Alberto Martinez argues his cosmology weighed more heavily in the condemnation.

You have to go and look. An intelligible, ordered creation Scholarly thesis · contested

The second conviction is an intelligible, ordered creation, made by a rational God and open to the minds of creatures made in his image.

Honest limit: this is a historical and theological precondition, not a proof of design — the deeper puzzle of why the cosmos is mathematically intelligible at all belongs to the argument from the laws of nature, taken up elsewhere.

Human fallibility — knowledge must be checked Scholarly thesis · contested

If the fall reaches the reasoning mind, then the individual thinker is not to be trusted alone; knowledge must be checked, replicated, and disciplined against self-deception. Harrison has traced how the doctrine of the fall shaped the experimental method itself, in Francis Bacon and the early Royal Society, as a remedy for a corrupted intellect.

A medieval lineage, and a witness from outside orthodoxy Supporting background

None of this sprang from nowhere in 1600. The universities — Bologna, Paris, Oxford — were a Christian invention with no exact precedent, and they institutionalised exactly the culture of open, rule-governed argument that science needs. John Philoponus in the sixth century criticised Aristotle’s physics and anticipated the idea of impetus; Jean Buridan and Nicole Oresme in the fourteenth pressed it toward inertia and even entertained a rotating earth; Robert Grosseteste and Roger Bacon pioneered the study of optics and experiment. The philosopher Alfred North Whitehead — no orthodox believer — called the very “faith in the possibility of science” an “unconscious derivative from medieval theology” and its insistence on the rationality of God.

Objection — “Science was really a Greek and Islamic achievement, not a Christian one”

Modern science stands on Greek mathematics and logic, and on the Islamic Golden Age, when scholars in Baghdad, Cairo, and Córdoba preserved and advanced Greek learning, founded algebra as a discipline, and — in Ibn al-Haytham’s optics — pioneered controlled experiment centuries before Europe.

Reply

All true: science was never a solely Christian achievement, and the case here neither needs nor claims a Christian monopoly. The thesis is not that Christianity single-handedly manufactured science, but that its picture of a rational, contingent, intelligible creation was fertile soil in which the enterprise could take root and last.

The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

Two honest limits keep this argument where it belongs. First, the thesis that Christian theology supplied the preconditions of science is a claim about history, and a contested one; historians rightly resist any monocausal story, and Numbers’s own Galileo Goes to Jail counts “that Christianity gave birth to modern science” among its myths — a caution against replacing the old warfare story with a triumphalist inverse. Take it as the careful historians offer it: not that Christianity single-handedly manufactured science, but that its picture of a rational, contingent, intelligible creation was fertile soil in which the enterprise could take root and last. Second, science was never a solely Christian achievement — it stands on Greek mathematics and logic, and on the Islamic Golden Age.

A fairness most defenders skip: The cautions here come from within the discipline, not from opponents of the faith. Ronald Numbers, no apologist for religion, counts “that Christianity gave birth to modern science” among the myths his own volume dismantles; John Hedley Brooke’s “complexity thesis” resists every tidy slogan — a Christian success story as much as the warfare myth; and Peter Harrison, one of the preconditions thesis’s own defenders, is also its sharpest critic on voluntarism. On the Galileo affair, Maurice Finocchiaro reads the conflict as running across church and academy alike; the Orthodox writer David Bentley Hart calls it less a war of science against faith than a collision of egos, personality, and politics; and on Giordano Bruno, Alberto Martinez argues his cosmology weighed more heavily in the condemnation than the standard account allows.

The bounded verdict: So the war is the modern invention. It is not a fact discovered by historians but a story popularised by two nineteenth-century writers, embraced because it flattered an age, and quietly retired by the scholars who checked it. In its place stands a scientific revolution midwifed in a Christian civilisation, by men who thought that in reading the book of nature they were reading a second scripture from the same hand — and whose convictions about a rational, contingent, intelligible creation were fertile soil for the enterprise. The Galileo affair is a genuine scar and should be owned without flinching; but one scar is not a war, and the case claims no Christian monopoly over learning that also runs through Greece and the Islamic Golden Age. And — this matters most — none of it shows Christianity is true. Fruitful and true are different things; the history dissolves a barrier, it does not prove the faith. That case rests on the resurrection, argued on its own evidence elsewhere.

    Drill 3 · The common objection (worked example)

    Someone hits you with the most common pushback

    Start here: draft your reply, then reveal a model answer to compare. This is the worked example — Drill 4 then makes you do it cold across the whole objection set.

    SK
    Skeptic
    "The church jailed Galileo for saying the earth moves. Science and religion have always been enemies — the church fights every discovery until it loses."
    One strong way to answer — in a 1 Peter 3:15 tone

    “I’d concede the hard part first: in the Galileo affair the Roman Catholic authorities got it badly wrong — wrong to condemn him, wrong to bind Scripture to one astronomy, wrong to silence a man who turned out right. No honest Christian account excuses that. But ‘always been enemies’ is a story two nineteenth-century writers popularised, not something historians find in the record. The men who actually built modern science — Copernicus a cathedral canon, Kepler trained for ministry, Galileo a devout Catholic, Newton ending the Principia with a doxology — thought they were studying nature because it was God’s handiwork. And in 1616 the evidence did not yet favour Copernicus: his system used circles and epicycles and predicted no better than Ptolemy, and Tycho’s system kept the earth still and fit the same data. The dispute cut across the church and the academy both. One Italian tragedy isn’t a three-century war — and, to be clear, none of this proves Christianity is true. That case rests on the resurrection, not on the history of science.”

    Gold gate · the unseen objection

    One you weren’t coached on

    Everything above rehearsed objections the page showed you. Here is the real test of mastery: an objection we deliberately did not drill. Reason it out cold from what the argument established, then reveal how it actually handles it. An honest “nailed it” is the first half of Gold.

    SK
    Skeptic
    "Christianity is structurally anti-science. It teaches that the Bible is the final authority on everything, so whenever Scripture and the world collide — Genesis against astronomy, against biology — faith is bound to override the evidence. The Galileo affair is just that principle doing what it always does."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    The mainstream Christian principle is the reverse of that, and it is far older than Galileo. Augustine — writing twelve centuries before the Galileo affair, in The Literal Meaning of Genesis — warned that Christians must not make the Bible contradict what reason and observation establish about the natural world, lest they discredit the faith before unbelievers. Galileo quoted that very principle; and, tellingly, both sides of the dispute could appeal to the same authority on how to read Scripture. So the lesson the episode actually teaches is double-edged, and it cuts against everyone: it rebukes a faith that overreaches its competence and clings to a scientific claim as though the gospel depended on it, and it rebukes the warfare myth that turns one Italian tragedy into a law of history. The error at Rome was not that Christianity is committed to Scripture trumping nature — it was a failure, in that one moment, to keep the ancient distinction Augustine had already drawn between what Scripture teaches and how a passing science reads the world.
