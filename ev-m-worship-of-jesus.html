Did the First Christians Worship Jesus as God?

The deep dive explains the case. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild from memory why the earliest Christians worshipped Jesus, meet the "it developed slowly over centuries" objection, and hold the answer together in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

It is one of the most repeated claims in the popular case against Christianity: that Jesus was revered as a great teacher and only slowly, over generations of Hellenistic drift, inflated into a god to be worshipped — the worship of Jesus a late development, not something the first Christians did. The New Testament scholar James Dunn put the historical question as a book title: Did the First Christians Worship Jesus? The evidence answers yes — and early, from the earliest layer we can reach.

To feel the force of it you have to see the line clearly. What Jewish monotheism guarded most jealously was not the posture of homage — Israelites bowed before a king as well as before God (1 Chronicles 29:20 uses one verb for both) — but the substance of worship: sacrifice, invocation, prayer, the hymn sung to a god, reserved for the one God alone. In the very books that record it, an angel twice refuses that worship for himself ("Worship God," Revelation 19:10; 22:9). Yet the earliest Christians gave exactly that to Jesus: they prayed to him (the Aramaic Marana tha, "Our Lord, come!", from the first Judean church; Stephen, Acts 7:59; "call upon the name of our Lord Jesus Christ," 1 Corinthians 1:2), sang to him (the Philippians hymn; and Pliny reports it from outside the church by c. AD 112), and worshipped him where they refused to worship an angel (Revelation 5:13–14; Hebrews 1:6).

And it was early, not a slow climb. Larry Hurtado found that a "binitarian" pattern of devotion emerged "amazingly early," within the first years, among Jews who counted worshipping any creature blasphemy; Marana tha is pre-Pauline Aramaic. The development was in the vocabulary the church later coined, not in the worship. Honest scope: this does not by itself prove the worship was warranted — that Jesus is in fact God — which is the separate work of the resurrection and Jesus's own words; nor does it hand us the fourth-century councils' metaphysics. What it establishes is enough to dissolve the "slow development" myth: the worship of Jesus is bedrock, and worship, in a Jew, is a confession about who God is.

Understand it

Not more prose — the load-bearing structure. See the skeleton, then learn to defend each bone.

The argument, formally

Worship, in a Jew, is a confession about who God is — and the first Christians offered it to Jesus, from the start.

P1

What Jewish monotheism reserved for the one God alone was not the posture of homage (Israelites bowed before kings too — 1 Chronicles 29:20 uses one verb for both) but the substance of cultic worship: sacrifice, invocation, prayer, the hymn to a god — so absolutely that even an angel refuses it ("Worship God," Revelation 19:10; 22:9). The earliest Christians gave exactly that to Jesus: they prayed to him (Marana tha; Stephen; "call upon the name," 1 Corinthians 1:2), sang to him (Philippians 2; Pliny, c. 112), and worshipped him where they refused to worship an angel (Revelation 5:13–14; Hebrews 1:6).

P2

And it was early, not a slow climb: Marana tha is pre-Pauline Aramaic from the Jerusalem church of the 30s, and Larry Hurtado dates the "binitarian" devotion "amazingly early," within the first years, among strict monotheists. The vocabulary the church later coined developed; the worship did not. And the one alternative — that Jesus was a supreme agent (the Jewish shaliach) — breaks at the altar: no agent was ever prayed to, or sung a hymn "as to a god."

∴ C

Therefore the first Christians worshipped Jesus, and did so from the start — not after centuries of drift. The worship of Jesus is bedrock; the councils later put it into words, they did not invent it.

This does not by itself prove the worship was warranted — that Jesus is in fact God — which is the separate work of the resurrection evidence and Jesus's own words. Its narrower, firmer point: the worship is early, offered by monotheistic Jews who confessed in the same breath that God is one. Nor does it hand us the fourth-century councils' metaphysics, which the honest case concedes came later, or adjudicate the intra-Christian disputes.

Why "the worship developed slowly" is the popular skeptic's move.

The claim's serious form comes from Bart Ehrman, who rejects the crude "Constantine invented it" story and grants that devotion to Jesus began strikingly early — but presses a subtler point: early reverence is not yet the later dogma; an "exaltation" devotion grew, over time, into full worship of Jesus as God. The reply grants what is true — the church's formal vocabulary really did develop later — and refuses only the inference that the worship itself was a late arrival.

Defending the premises

Why each premise is more plausible than its denial

Support for P1 — the line was cultic worship, and they crossed it with Jesus

The line was substance, not posture

It is easy to misplace the line. The mere posture of homage was not it: Israelites bowed low before a king as well as before God — 1 Chronicles 29:20 uses a single verb of prostration for both the LORD and King David in one breath. Bowing, kneeling, honouring could be offered to prophets, kings, and angels without blasphemy. What Jewish monotheism reserved for the one God, absolutely, was the substance of worship — sacrifice, invocation, prayer, the hymn sung to a god. The New Testament draws the line there itself: when John falls to worship the angel of the vision, the angel recoils twice — "You must not do that!… Worship God" (Revelation 19:10; 22:9). Even the highest created messenger refuses the worship due God alone; that refusal is the control case.

They prayed to him, sang to him, worshipped him

The first believers gave Jesus exactly that reserved worship. They prayed to him: Marana tha, "Our Lord, come!" (1 Corinthians 16:22), an invocation; Stephen's "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit" (Acts 7:59); the church defined as "all those who… call upon the name of our Lord Jesus Christ" (1 Corinthians 1:2), the Scriptures' own idiom for invoking Yahweh (Joel 2:32). They sang to him: the Philippians hymn applies Isaiah 45's "every knee shall bow" to Jesus, and Pliny reports from outside the church, c. 112, a "hymn to Christ, as to a god." And they worshipped him where they refused everyone else: the doxology to "him who sits on the throne and to the Lamb" (Revelation 5:13–14), and God's own command, "Let all God's angels worship him" (Hebrews 1:6). The Lamb receives what the angel forbids.

Support for P2 — it was early, and the agency escape breaks at the altar

The timeline will not cooperate with a slow drift

The most explosive evidence is also the earliest. Marana tha is Aramaic — a fossil of devotion in the original language of the Palestinian church, reaching behind Paul's Greek to the first believers in Jerusalem in the 30s. The Philippians hymn is widely regarded as pre-Pauline, older than the letter that carries it (the dating is debated — Gordon Fee argues Paul composed it — but the worship stands either way). Larry Hurtado's major study concluded that this "binitarian" devotion emerged "amazingly early," within the first years, among Jews who would have counted worshipping any creature blasphemy. The development was in the articulation; the words the church later coined came slowly, the worship was there at the beginning.

Was he only a supreme agent? It breaks at the altar

The strongest objection (Jehovah's Witnesses, biblical Unitarians) invokes the Jewish principle of agency, the shaliach: "a man's agent is as the man himself." An ambassador is received as though he were the king without being the king, and much New Testament language of Jesus being "sent" and "given" fits it. But agency has a ceiling: no shaliach in Jewish thought ever received the cultic devotion — the prayer, the invocation, the hymn offered to a god — that the sources give Jesus. It is exactly why the angel in Revelation, God's own messenger, refuses the worship the Lamb accepts. The honest limit: the developed creedal vocabulary (Nicaea's "one essence") came later, and this does not by itself prove the worship warranted. But the framework explains the titles and the sending-language; it breaks at the altar.

The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: Ehrman's developmental thesis — he grants the devotion began strikingly early (he rejects the Constantine myth), but denies it amounts to the later dogma: early reverence, he says, is not yet worship of Jesus as God in the full sense. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the reply below.

"Early reverence isn't full worship — it grew into that over time" Hard

Strongest form

Bart Ehrman rejects the crude "Constantine invented it" story and grants that devotion to Jesus began early. His subtler claim: an initial "exaltation" reverence (God raised the man Jesus to heavenly status) grew, over time and reflection, into the full worship of Jesus as God in the same sense as the Father. The loftiest worship texts are the church's later theology, not the earliest layer.

Reply

Concede the true part first: the church's formal vocabulary for what it was doing — the fourth-century language of "one essence" — genuinely developed later. But the datum is not the vocabulary; it is the practice, and the practice is in the earliest layer we can reach. Marana tha is Aramaic, pre-Pauline, from the Jerusalem church of the 30s; the Philippians hymn is pre-Pauline (Fee argues Paul composed it — the worship stands either way); Hurtado dates the binitarian devotion "amazingly early." These are not the top of a slow climb — they are the bedrock. The refinement was in how the church explained the worship of Jesus, never in whether it offered it.

"The worship of Jesus was voted in at Nicaea under Constantine" Common

Strongest form

Everyone has heard it: in 325 the emperor Constantine gathered bishops who made Jesus divine — and worthy of worship — by a vote. Before Nicaea he was a revered prophet, not an object of worship.

Reply

It runs aground on the dates. The worship of Jesus is in the earliest sources we possess, roughly 250 years before Nicaea: an Aramaic prayer to the risen Jesus from the first Judean church (Marana tha), a pre-Pauline hymn sung to him, and — from a hostile outsider — Pliny's report (c. 112) that Christians sang "a hymn to Christ, as to a god," two centuries before the council. Nicaea did not decide whether to worship Jesus; that was long settled. It excluded Arius, who said the Son was a created being — a debate within a church already worshipping him.

"Jesus was only a supreme agent — honoured as God's representative, not worshipped as God" Trickier

Strongest form

The Jewish principle of agency (the shaliach) holds that "a man's agent is as the man himself." An ambassador is honoured and received as though he were the king, without being the king. On this reading Jesus is God's supreme agent, given honour and even a kind of worship as the perfect representative — and much New Testament language of his being "sent" and "given" fits it naturally.

Reply

Grant the language of sending; the reading still has a ceiling, and the worship evidence crashes through it. No shaliach in Jewish thought ever received the cultic devotion — the prayer, the invocation, the hymn offered to a god — that the sources give Jesus. Agency conferred authority; it never conferred that adoration. That is exactly why the angel in Revelation, himself God's messenger and agent, refuses the very worship the Lamb accepts. The framework explains the titles and the sending-language; it breaks at the altar.

"Bowing to Jesus is just homage — Jews bowed to kings; it doesn't prove worship" Trickier

Strongest form

Your worship texts often just say people "bowed" or "fell down" before Jesus (the Greek proskyneō). But Israelites bowed before kings, too — the same gesture. Posture proves nothing about deity; it is ordinary homage to a superior.

Reply

Correct — and the case concedes it in as many words: the posture of homage was never the reserved line (1 Chronicles 29:20 uses one verb of prostration for both the LORD and King David). That is precisely why the load-bearing evidence is not the posture-word at all, but the substance of worship — the things that could never be given to a king: an Aramaic prayer to the risen Jesus, invocation of his name, the hymn sung "to Christ, as to a god," and the worship an angel refuses. Where the argument leans on Matthew's proskyneō, its force comes from the frame (Jesus refuses Satan's worship, then receives his disciples' and claims all authority), not the word.

"The worship texts are from the latest books (Revelation, John) — the earliest layer didn't do it" Trickier

Strongest form

Your strongest worship scenes — the Lamb enthroned in Revelation, the exalted claims of John — come from the latest New Testament writings, decades on. That fits a slow development: the worship crested late.

Reply

The case is built to survive that, because it rests its weight on the earliest material, not the latest. Marana tha is pre-Pauline Aramaic from the Jerusalem church of the 30s — a prayer to the risen Jesus in the original language of the first community. The Philippians hymn is widely regarded as pre-Pauline, older than the letter (Fee argues Paul composed it — the worship stands either way). Paul's undisputed letters (the 50s) already define the church as those who "call upon the name" of Jesus. Revelation and John make the worship vivid; they did not invent it. The highest devotion sits in the earliest stratum, which is the opposite of what a slow-development story predicts.

"Even if they worshipped him early, that doesn't make it true" Hard

Strongest form

Suppose the first Christians really did worship Jesus, and early. All that shows is that people believed and worshipped early — and early worship can still be misplaced. You have not shown Jesus is actually God, only that he was adored soon.

Reply

Correct, and the argument says so plainly. It answers a narrower, historical claim — that the worship of Jesus developed slowly over centuries — by showing the devotion is in the earliest layer we can reach. Whether that worship was warranted — whether Jesus is in fact God — is a separate question, argued on separate grounds: the resurrection of Jesus and his own words and deeds, each treated in its own track. What this closes off is only the specific charge that the worship was a late invention. (It is, though, a strong pointer: strict monotheists who would die before bowing to an idol do not lightly start praying to a crucified man.)
