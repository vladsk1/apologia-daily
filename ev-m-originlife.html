The Origin of Life Argument

The deep dive tells you what the argument is. This track makes you able to use it — reconstruct it from memory, answer its hardest objections, and hold the line in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

How did the first living cell arise from non-living chemistry? This is the origin-of-life question, and it remains genuinely unsolved in mainstream science. The design argument focuses on a specific feature of even the simplest life: it runs on specified information. A cell's DNA stores a digital code that directs the assembly of functional proteins, and that code is not just complex but meaningfully arranged, the way letters in a sentence carry instructions. The argument asks what kind of cause is known to produce that sort of functional, information-rich arrangement.

The reasoning is an inference from experience: in every case we can trace to its source, large quantities of specified information come from a mind. Undirected chemistry has not been shown to generate it. Researchers such as Stephen Meyer press this point, while noting that some estimates of how rare functional sequences are (for example, work by Douglas Axe) are contested by other biochemists. The honest claim is therefore comparative — a mind is the cause we already know can do this job — not a proof that nature could never do it.

Scientists pursuing the RNA-world hypothesis, metabolism-first models, and self-organisation continue to make progress, and these are serious lines of research. So this is offered as an inference to the best explanation given what we currently know, not as a claim that the matter is settled or that "scientists agree life was designed." Note the scope: this argument reaches an intelligent cause — not yet the Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Identifying that being with the God revealed in Christ is the work of the other arguments and of revelation. The fuller case for the Christian God rests above all on the historical evidence for the resurrection (see The Minimal Facts Argument). This is a design inference, not a doctrinal test: Christians who hold young-earth, old-earth and evolutionary-creation views all confess the same creeds, and this site does not adjudicate between them.

Before you start · key terms

Eight words this argument leans on

Learn these first — the objections below turn on them, and most weak versions of the argument come from blurring one of them.

The information problem“The origin of life” is at least three problems — metabolism (a self-sustaining network of reactions), self-replication, and the origin of biological information — and the third is the deepest. The discovery of the genetic code revealed that the cell is not just a bag of reacting molecules but an information-processing system, so the explanatory target is not “how did chemistry get complicated?” but “where did the specified sequence — the code itself — come from?”
Specified informationLife’s sequences are aperiodic yet functionally specific, like the letters of a sentence — unlike crystals (ordered but repetitive, carrying almost no information) and random polymers (no functional message). Stephen Meyer, following others, calls this “specified complexity” or “specified information.” To stay non-circular the term is defined strictly functionally: a sequence carries specified information when, of the astronomically many sequences of its length, it falls in the tiny set that actually folds and replicates — a property fixed by chemistry and measurable in the laboratory, not by appeal to any author’s purpose.
The sequence hypothesisFrancis Crick’s 1958 proposal that a molecule of DNA carries its instructions by the precise order of its bases, the way letters carry meaning by their order in a sentence: “Information,” Crick wrote, “means here the precise determination of sequence.” Michael Polanyi made the point structurally — the order of the bases is not dictated by the chemical bonding of the backbone (any base can follow any other), so the sequence is a boundary condition the chemistry of its medium leaves open.
The RNA-world hypothesisThe dominant answer since the 1980s to the chicken-and-egg loop (DNA needs proteins to copy, proteins need DNA’s information): RNA can both store information and catalyze reactions, so an RNA molecule that could copy itself would in principle break the loop. Named by Walter Gilbert in 1986; made concrete by the discovery of catalytic RNAs (ribozymes). There has been real progress (Sutherland’s 2009 activated ribonucleotides; Szostak’s protocell work), but no one has demonstrated a self-replicating RNA arising and sustaining itself under realistic prebiotic conditions — and even one would presuppose a specified sequence, pushing the information question back a step rather than dissolving it.
Inference to the best explanationThe design argument is best understood not as “science can’t explain it, therefore God,” but as an abductive inference about a specific kind of effect — the same reasoning a forensic scientist or an archaeologist uses. It is comparative (which of the available causes best explains the data?) and defeasible (overturnable by a laboratory result), never a deductive proof.
Argument from knowledge, not ignoranceThe distinction that answers the god-of-the-gaps charge: the inference does not say “we can’t imagine how nature did it” but “we positively know one kind of cause — a mind — that produces specified information, and no undirected cause has yet been shown to.” What sets it apart from a bare gap argument is not that it can be overturned (gap arguments are overturned all the time) but that it rests on a positive, known cause rather than on ignorance alone.
Defeasible (held provisionally)The inference is explicitly revisable: demonstrate an undirected chemical route from simple precursors to specified information and it is overturned. The honest claim is comparative and provisional — the current best explanation, not the final word — and anyone who says “abiogenesis is impossible” has overreached the evidence.
The scope of the inference (the deism gap)Even if it succeeds, the argument points to a designing intelligence behind life’s code — and no further. It says nothing of incarnation, resurrection, or covenant; it reaches an intelligent cause, not yet the Father, Son and Holy Spirit. It is one strand in a cumulative natural theology, not the whole rope: identifying that mind with the God revealed in Christ is the work of revelation and, above all, of the historical evidence for the resurrection.
The argument, formally

Three lines. The whole weight rests on the two premises.

P1

The origin of the first life required the production of large amounts of functionally specified information — the genetic code and the sequence-specificity of biological macromolecules.

P2

Of the available causes — chance, physical-chemical necessity, the two combined, or intelligent agency — only intelligent agency is known, from repeated experience, to produce specified information of this kind.

∴ C

Therefore intelligent design is, at present, the best explanation for the origin of biological information.

This is an inference to the best explanation (abductive), not a deductive syllogism — so the hardest objection is the god-of-the-gaps charge, which contests the form of the inference itself; others target P2, arguing that chemistry can in principle bridge the gap.

Then the conceptual step — what kind of cause fits the data

In every case where we know the causal story of an information-rich artifact, the cause is a mind, and no undirected cause has yet been shown to produce such information—though that experience is of human minds, which is why the inference stays comparative rather than demonstrative. Even so, a mind is the one cause we already know can do this job.

Defending Premise 1

The origin of the first life required the production of large amounts of functionally specified information — the genetic code and the sequence-specificity of biological macromolecules.

Precision check: the question is not merely “how did chemistry get complicated?” but “where did the specified sequence—the functional arrangement of parts, the code itself—come from?” It is not whether life looks designed—everyone grants that—but whether the best explanation of one specific feature, the coded information at life’s foundation, is an undirected process or a mind. And it is about the first cell, not about the mechanisms of descent thereafter: none of the argument depends on rejecting common descent or biological evolution more broadly.

Life is built on digital code Established biology

DNA stores information as a four-letter digital code, and proteins require precise amino-acid sequences to fold and function. The analogy is not merely popular: the genetic code is a real mapping from three-letter codons to amino acids, read by dedicated cellular machinery. Even the smallest-genome cells that can be grown on their own in the laboratory, such as the parasitic bacterium Mycoplasma genitalium, carry several hundred genes and are already staggeringly information-rich. Even JCVI-syn3.0 (2016), a synthetic minimal cell stripped as far as anyone has managed, still needs 473 genes, many of unknown function.

What “information” means here Defining the target

Crystals are highly ordered but carry almost no information (they repeat). Random polymers carry no functional message. Life sits in a third category: aperiodic sequences that are also functionally specific, like the letters of a sentence. In 1958 Francis Crick proposed the sequence hypothesis: a molecule of DNA carries its instructions by the precise order of its bases. “Information,” Crick wrote, “means here the precise determination of sequence.” What the word specified adds is the further constraint that the sequence not merely be improbable but actually do something—fold, catalyse, replicate. Michael Polanyi made the point structurally: the order of the bases is not dictated by the chemical bonding of the DNA backbone—any base can follow any other—so the sequence, like the text on a printed page, is a boundary condition that the chemistry of its medium leaves open.

Honest limit: Claude Shannon’s theory treats a message’s meaning as, in Shannon’s words, “irrelevant to the engineering problem”—so a random string and a working gene of equal length carry equal Shannon information.

The improbability of functional folds Contested estimate

Douglas Axe estimated, by extrapolating from experiments on a β-lactamase enzyme domain, that sequences performing a specific function by any domain-sized fold might be as rare as one in 1077 of possible sequences of that length (a figure that has been sharply contested; see the objection below) — and even on far more generous estimates, no demonstrated undirected pathway yet bridges the gap.

Honest limit: this is a real scientific dispute, and the design argument should not lean on a single contested number as though it were settled. The case must rest on more than one line.

Objection — “Specified information” smuggles in a mind

Much depends on whether “specified information” names a genuine natural kind, or quietly smuggles in the intentional, semantic sense that belongs to messages authored by minds—which would make the conclusion circular.

Reply

The argument should therefore rest on a strictly functional definition: a sequence carries specified information when, of the astronomically many sequences of its length, it falls in the tiny set that actually folds and replicates—a property fixed by chemistry and measurable in the laboratory, not by appeal to any author’s purpose. Defined this way, the claim that such functional specificity is, in our experience, mind-produced becomes a claim about a measurable feature of nature—though the experience it rests on is still our experience of human minds, which is why critics press the analogy, and why the inference stays comparative rather than demonstrative.

The room: Francis Crick (the sequence hypothesis) and Michael Polanyi supply the vocabulary; Stephen Meyer, following others, calls the target “specified complexity” or “specified information.” Even the most committed evolutionists grant that living things look designed—Dawkins defines biology as “the study of complicated things that give the appearance of having been designed for a purpose”—and for them the aim is to explain that appearance away. Apologia Daily takes no institutional position binding the Christian faith to intelligent design as science, nor to any particular view of evolutionary biology or reading of the timescales of Genesis; old-earth, young-earth, and evolutionary-creationist believers alike hold the Nicene faith in common while differing on these natural-historical questions.
Defending Premise 2

Of the available causes — chance, physical-chemical necessity, the two combined, or intelligent agency — only intelligent agency is known, from repeated experience, to produce specified information of this kind.

Precision check: the conclusion is that intelligent design is, at present, the best explanation for the origin of biological information—a probabilistic, defeasible, comparative claim. It could be overturned tomorrow by a laboratory result, and the honest defender should say so cheerfully. We do not know that no undirected pathway exists; we know that none has been demonstrated. And it points, if it succeeds, to a designing intelligence behind life’s code—and no further. It says nothing of incarnation, resurrection, or covenant. The fuller case for the Christian God rests on revelation and, above all, on the historical evidence for the resurrection (see The Minimal Facts Argument).

The one known cause of information The engine of the argument · comparative

In every case where we know the causal story of an information-rich artifact—a paragraph, a software program, a coded message—the cause is a mind. Information of this functional, sequence-specific sort is, in our uniform experience, the signature of intelligence. Intelligence is the one known cause of information of this kind, and no undirected cause has yet been shown to produce it.

Honest limit: the experience it rests on is still our experience of human minds, which is why critics press the analogy, and why the inference stays comparative rather than demonstrative.

The alternatives look inadequate Contested numbers

Chance faces steep improbabilities: a single fold at Axe’s odds is not, by itself, beyond the roughly 1080 particles and 1017 seconds the observable universe affords as trials—but a working cell requires many such folds together in one integrated, coded system, and it is that compound improbability, not any single protein, that outstrips the resources of chance. Necessity (law-like chemistry) tends to produce repetitive order, not aperiodic specificity. And selection cannot be invoked at the very first step, because natural selection requires heritable self-replication to already exist—which is precisely what is being explained.

Honest limit: the origin-of-life problem is not only about one protein fold but about an integrated, coded system; where the numbers are contested, say so, and lean on the convergence.

No undirected mechanism has been demonstrated Unsolved · active field

The Miller–Urey experiment (1953) produced some amino acids, but never life or information—a heap of amino acids is to a cell what a bucket of letter tiles is to the works of Shakespeare—and it assumed an atmosphere now widely thought to be unrealistic. The chirality (homochirality) problem remains: life uses one “handedness” while undirected chemistry yields both. And there is a chicken-and-egg problem—DNA needs proteins to be built, but proteins are built from instructions in DNA. The dominant answer since the 1980s is the RNA-world hypothesis, proposed in various forms by Carl Woese, Francis Crick, Leslie Orgel, and named by Walter Gilbert in 1986. RNA can both store information (like DNA) and catalyze reactions (like a protein); the discovery of catalytic RNAs (ribozymes) by Thomas Cech and Sidney Altman, which won the 1989 Nobel Prize, made the idea concrete. An RNA molecule that could copy itself would, in principle, break the loop. Even granting a pool of nucleotides, no one has demonstrated a self-replicating RNA arising and sustaining itself under realistic prebiotic conditions. Even a working RNA replicator would not by itself explain the origin of the information—it would presuppose a functional, specified sequence to get started, pushing the central question back one step rather than dissolving it. The field is genuinely divided between replicator-first and metabolism-first camps, with no consensus mechanism in either.

Honest limit: Miller–Urey was a genuine landmark that showed some biological building blocks form spontaneously under plausible energy input; later work by Miller and his colleagues found that much of the shortfall in less reducing conditions came from oxidation of the products during analysis. There has been real progress—John Sutherland’s group synthesized activated pyrimidine ribonucleotides under prebiotically plausible conditions (2009)—and origin-of-life research is active and unfinished. This is not a stagnant field. But that is itself the point of P2: no undirected mechanism has yet been demonstrated to do the job.

The field’s own authorities concede the difficulty Difficulty conceded · inference is not

Francis Crick wrote that “the origin of life appears at the moment to be almost a miracle, so many are the conditions which would have had to have been satisfied to get it going.” Leslie Orgel and Gerald Joyce candidly contrasted the optimistic “molecular biologist’s dream” with the “prebiotic chemist’s nightmare” of actually making RNA from scratch. The Nobel laureate Christian de Duve pressed the case against chance: “a string of improbable events—the same lottery number being drawn twice, or the same bridge hand being dealt twice in a row—does not happen naturally.” Theodosius Dobzhansky put the circularity bluntly: “Prebiological natural selection is a contradiction of terms.” There is no scientific consensus mechanism for the origin of life; the leading textbooks and review articles describe the problem as open.

Honest limit: Crick added at once that this “should not be taken to imply” that life could not have begun by ordinary chemistry, and he himself floated directed panspermia rather than design. Neither de Duve nor Dobzhansky was an advocate of design—de Duve held that life was “a cosmic imperative,” an all-but-inevitable consequence of chemistry, and it was chance, not a naturalistic origin, that he rejected—which is exactly why their judgement counts here. The difficulty is widely conceded; the inference to a designer is not.

Objection — This is a god-of-the-gaps argument Hardest

The design inference simply baptizes ignorance. The history of science is a history of gaps closing: lightning, disease, planetary motion, and the diversity of species were all once attributed to direct divine action and later given natural explanations. To infer a designer from today’s missing mechanism is to repeat a long-discredited move and to bet against the very process that has reliably delivered. Worse, it can be a science-stopper: if “a mind did it” is on the table, why keep working at the chemistry? The warning is not only a skeptic’s: the phrase “God of the gaps” was popularized by the Christian mathematician and chemist Charles Coulson (Science and Christian Belief, 1955), and evolutionary creationists press the objection today.

Reply

The concession: absence of a current explanation is not proof of impossibility, and anyone who says “abiogenesis is impossible” has overreached the evidence. The reply: the design inference, properly stated, is not an argument from ignorance but an argument from knowledge—from a known causal power. We are not saying “we can’t imagine how nature did it.” We are saying “we positively know one kind of cause that produces specified information, and no undirected cause has yet been shown to.” It is in principle defeasible: demonstrate an undirected chemical route from simple precursors to specified information, and the inference is overturned. What distinguishes it from a bare gap argument is not that it can be overturned—gap arguments are overturned all the time—but that it rests on a positive, known cause rather than on ignorance alone; and because it can be overturned, it is held as provisional. The science-stopper worry also cuts the other way—many design theorists actively want the chemistry pursued, precisely because a clean negative result strengthens the inference and a positive result would settle it. The right posture is to keep doing the chemistry while being candid that, as of now, it has not delivered.

Objection — The improbability numbers are wrong

Douglas Axe’s 1-in-1077 figure has been sharply contested. Critics—including biologists such as Arthur Hunt and the authors at venues like the Panda’s Thumb—argue that Axe’s experimental method measured something narrower than the true density of all functional folds, that functional sequences are far more common than he estimated, and that proteins can arise and improve gradually rather than by one-shot assembly. The strongest form comes from the peer-reviewed literature: in 2001 Anthony Keefe and Jack Szostak reported in Nature that they had screened a library of some six trillion proteins, each with 80 random amino acids, and recovered four new ATP-binding proteins that “appear to be unrelated to each other or to anything found in the current databases of biological proteins.” They estimated that roughly one random-sequence protein in a hundred billion has “ATP-binding activity comparable to the proteins isolated in this study,” and their conclusion was direct: functional proteins “are sufficiently common in protein sequence space” that “they may be discovered by entirely stochastic means, such as presumably operated when proteins were first used by living organisms.” Set beside Axe’s 1 in 1077, that is a gap of some sixty-six orders of magnitude. If function is anywhere near as common as Keefe and Szostak found, the critic says, the probability strand of the design argument does not merely weaken; it largely fails.

Reply

That result should be stated without hedging, and it confirms what was conceded above: Axe’s figure cannot be treated as the settled rarity of protein function. But the two experiments did not measure the same thing, and the gap between their numbers partly reflects that. Keefe and Szostak selected 80-residue random proteins for binding a small molecule, and based their frequency estimate on the four families recovered before any mutagenesis. Axe tested random replacements within a weakly functional β-lactamase domain for a working enzyme active site, which, he notes, requires tertiary structure, and then extrapolated to the prevalence of sequences performing a specific function by any domain-sized fold. Which measure better represents what the first cell required is itself part of the dispute, and neither experiment settles it. The verdict is bounded: Keefe and Szostak’s work is a serious, peer-reviewed reason not to lean on Axe’s number; it does not show that undirected chemistry can produce the integrated, coded system a cell requires. The comparative inference does not require Axe’s exact figure: even on far more generous estimates of functional density, no demonstrated undirected pathway yet bridges the gap from chemistry to a self-replicating, information-bearing system. The probability argument is one strand of a convergent case, not its load-bearing pillar.

The room: Stephen Meyer develops the argument at book length in Signature in the Cell (2009); James Tour, himself a Christian and a sharp critic of the field, argues that it has vastly underestimated how far it is from synthesizing even the components of a cell. Graham Oppy and Sahotra Sarkar, and many Christians, press the god-of-the-gaps objection. Eugene Koonin, no friend of design, has written candidly about how intractable the origin of the translation system and the genetic code remains, even invoking the multiverse to supply enough probabilistic resources. The majority of working scientists expect a route to be found, and that expectation is not irrational given the history of the discipline; the design inference is a minority position in the academy, and most experts disagree with the design conclusion even while agreeing that the problem is unsolved.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

This is what separates someone who knows the origin-of-life argument from someone who can defend it. Each gets its best form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged just below.

The one that matters most: god-of-the-gaps — an unsolved problem is not an argument for design. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"This is just a god-of-the-gaps argument" Hard

Strongest form

You're inserting God wherever science hasn't yet found an answer. Lightning, disease, planetary motion and the diversity of species were all once put down to direct divine action and later given natural explanations; inferring a designer from today’s missing mechanism repeats that move and can stop science. And the warning is not only a skeptic’s: the Christian chemist Charles Coulson popularized the phrase, and evolutionary creationists press it today.

Reply

First the concession: absence of a current explanation is not proof of impossibility, and anyone who says “abiogenesis is impossible” has overreached. The reply: “we positively know one kind of cause that produces specified information, and no undirected cause has yet been shown to.” What distinguishes it from a bare gap argument is not that it can be overturned—gap arguments are overturned all the time—but that it rests on a positive, known cause rather than on ignorance alone; and because it can be overturned, it is held as provisional.

"The RNA world solves it / abiogenesis is just unfinished" Trickier

Strongest form

RNA is both a replicator and a catalyst, so an RNA world bridges the DNA–protein gap — and the research is steadily progressing.

Reply

There is real progress—Sutherland’s group made activated pyrimidine ribonucleotides under prebiotically plausible conditions (2009)—but no one has demonstrated a self-replicating RNA arising and sustaining itself under realistic prebiotic conditions. Even if it worked, the information problem is only pushed back a step, not solved — you still need a long, functional, specified sequence to get started.

"Given enough time and trials, even rare events happen" Trickier

Strongest form

A vast universe and billions of years of chemistry make even highly improbable events likely to happen somewhere.

Reply

The probabilistic resources are finite: roughly 10^80 particles and about 10^17 seconds of cosmic history. A single fold at Axe’s odds is not, by itself, beyond those resources—but a working cell requires many such folds together in one integrated, coded system, and it is that compound improbability, not any single protein, that outstrips the resources of chance. And the comparative inference does not require Axe’s contested figure; the probability argument is one strand of a convergent case, not its load-bearing pillar. More time only helps if each trial has a real chance, and that's exactly what's in dispute.

"Natural selection / self-organization can build the information" Hard

Strongest form

Natural selection plus self-organizing chemistry could ratchet complexity upward gradually, with no mind required.

Reply

Natural selection requires heritable self-replication to already exist — but that's the very thing being explained, so it can't be invoked to explain the first life. And self-organizing order, like a crystal, is low-information repetition: regular, predictable patterns. Genes carry aperiodic specified information, which self-organization has never been shown to produce.

"We don't know the prebiotic conditions, so the design inference is premature" Hard

Strongest form

The absence of a mechanism may simply reflect our ignorance of early-Earth chemistry, not a real explanatory gap. Drawing a design inference now is jumping the gun.

Reply

It is true that early-Earth conditions are poorly known, and the right response is not to deny it but to locate the inference correctly. But the design inference doesn't rest on ignorance — it rests on a positive track record (minds reliably make specified information) plus the persistent failure of every undirected proposal so far. So it stands as the current best explanation, and remains revisable if prebiotic chemistry ever delivers a real mechanism.

The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

There is no scientific consensus mechanism for the origin of life; the leading textbooks and review articles describe the problem as open, and that is said not only by design theorists but by researchers who are no friends of design — Eugene Koonin (candid about how intractable the origin of the translation system and the genetic code remains, even invoking the multiverse for probabilistic resources), Leslie Orgel, and Robert Shapiro. The field is vigorous and advancing on sub-problems, and is genuinely divided between replicator-first routes (the RNA world — Sutherland, Szostak) and metabolism-first routes (alkaline hydrothermal vents — Michael Russell, Nick Lane, William Martin), with no agreed mechanism in either. The design inference is a minority position in the academy, defended by a serious but small community (Stephen Meyer; the synthetic chemist James Tour; the Discovery Institute’s circle; sympathetic philosophers), and is widely — though not universally — judged to fall outside methodological naturalism. It is pressed against by philosophers such as Graham Oppy and Sahotra Sarkar, by working scientists generally, and, notably, by many Christians (evolutionary creationists; the Christian chemist Charles Coulson popularized “God of the gaps”). The live disputes are sharp: whether functional sequences are vanishingly rare (Axe’s 1-in-10⁷⁷) or far commoner (Keefe and Szostak); whether an undirected route can bridge the chemistry-to-code gap; and whether the design inference is science at all. Most working scientists expect a natural route to be found, and given the history of the discipline that expectation is not irrational.

A fairness most defenders skip: Honest defenders concede the observations. Absence of a current explanation is not proof of impossibility — anyone who says “abiogenesis is impossible” has overreached; we do not know that no undirected pathway exists, only that none has been demonstrated. The RNA-world and metabolism-first programs are serious and genuinely advancing on sub-problems, not stagnant. Douglas Axe’s 1-in-10⁷⁷ figure is sharply contested — Keefe and Szostak found functional proteins far commoner — so the probability strand cannot carry the argument alone. Early-Earth conditions are poorly known. But the inference does not follow from these concessions, because it rests not on ignorance but on a positive, known cause — intelligence is the one cause shown to produce specified information — together with the persistent failure of undirected proposals so far; and it is defeasible, revisable the moment prebiotic chemistry delivers a real mechanism. As a claim that a mind is the best present explanation of life’s informational signature, it stands; as a proof that nature could never do it, it was never made.

The bounded verdict: The design inference from biological information does not prove that life was designed. What it establishes is narrower and real: that the confident assurance science has essentially solved the origin of life outruns the evidence; that there is a genuine, deep mystery at the foundation of biology — code-like, functionally specified information, of which intelligence is the one known cause and which no undirected cause has yet been shown to produce; and that, among the causes currently on offer, intelligent agency is the one we know can produce the relevant effect, which makes design the best present explanation of life’s informational signature. It is a probabilistic, defeasible, comparative claim that could be overturned tomorrow by a laboratory result, and the honest defender should say so cheerfully. And its reach stops at a designing intelligence behind life’s code — not yet the Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Identifying that mind with the God revealed in Christ is the work of revelation and, above all, of the historical evidence for the resurrection; this is one strand in a cumulative natural theology, not the whole rope.

    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    Weigh that sociological fact honestly — it is true and it matters. The design inference is a minority position in the academy, defended by a serious but small community, and it is widely, though not universally, judged to fall outside methodological naturalism. Most working scientists do expect an undirected route to be found, and given the history of the discipline that expectation is not irrational. But notice what the consensus establishes and what it does not. What is agreed across the field — by Koonin, Orgel and Shapiro, no friends of design — is that there is at present no consensus mechanism for the origin of life: the problem is open. The disagreement is not that these researchers have found an undirected route and the design theorist denies it; it is that most judge design to be ruled out as an explanation in principle, by the ground rule of methodological naturalism, rather than weighed and found wanting on the evidence. So the bounded verdict is this: the expert majority is a real reason for humility, and it should be stated plainly — but a consensus that the problem is unsolved, held alongside a methodological commitment to admit only undirected causes, is not evidence that an undirected cause has been shown to do the job. Among the causes currently on offer, intelligence remains the one known to produce the relevant effect, and the inference is held provisionally, as the current best.
