Where Did Human Equality Come From?

The deep dive tells you what the argument is. This track makes you able to use it — reconstruct it from memory, answer its hardest objections, and hold the line in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

You believe — almost without having to be told — that every human being is of equal worth: the newborn and the dying, the genius and the person with a profound disability, the celebrated and the destitute. But that conviction is not obvious and not natural. As a measurement it is plainly false: human beings are radically unequal in strength, health, intellect and usefulness, in every capacity you can weigh or test. “Equal worth” is a value laid over that inequality, not a fact read off from it. So where did it come from?

For most of recorded history almost no one believed it, and the civilisations that gave the West its philosophy believed close to the opposite. Aristotle held that some people are “marked out” from birth for rule and others for subjection; a Roman Senate could calmly send four hundred innocent household slaves to their deaths for the “public advantage.” Into that world came a different claim — Jewish in its root, not first Christian — that God made humanity, male and female alike, in his own image, so that worth is conferred, not earned, and identical in each. The church carried it into the Gentile world and welded it to a second claim: that God the Son, in his true human nature, had been executed by crucifixion, Rome’s instrument for erasing a human being’s standing. Even the atheist historian Yuval Noah Harari concedes the premise: “there are no such things as rights in biology.”

But the scope must stay honest, and this track keeps it honest throughout. This is a genealogy of a conviction, not a proof of a creed. That equal worth has deep Christian and Jewish roots does not by itself show Christianity is true — a belief can have a noble pedigree and still be false, and the case for the faith rests on the resurrection, made elsewhere in the Library. The church has also betrayed this very doctrine. What the argument establishes is narrower, and about origins: the load-bearing premise that every person carries an equal, unearned worth entered the West through the image of God, and had no other stable home in the ancient world.

Before you start · key terms

Eight words this argument leans on

Learn these first — the objections below turn on them, and most weak versions of the argument come from blurring one of them.

Equal, unearned worthThe conviction — held almost without having to be told — that every human being is of equal worth: the newborn and the dying, the genius and the person with a profound disability, the celebrated and the destitute. Strip away rank, usefulness, intelligence and strength, and a bare human worth remains, the same in each, outranking everything else about them. We treat its denial not as a mistake of fact but as a kind of blasphemy — “dehumanising.” It is a value, not a measurement.
Value, not a measurementThe decisive distinction. As a measurement equal worth is plainly false: human beings are radically unequal in every capacity you can name — strength, health, intellect, beauty, longevity, usefulness to others — and nothing you can weigh, count or test comes out equal. “Equal worth” is a claim about what a person <em>is</em>, laid over a mass of evidence that they are not equal in what they can <em>do</em>. That it feels self-evident is itself the thing to explain, not evidence that it is natural.
The image of God (imago Dei)The claim that God made humanity — not the king, not the free man, not the Greek, but humanity, male and female alike — in his own image, so that worth is not earned or measured but conferred, bestowed on every human being by the Maker, and therefore identical in each regardless of capacity or rank. Its root is not first of all Christian but Jewish: the doctrine developed in Jewish scripture and Jewish interpretation and stands on its own feet. The church did not invent it; what it did was carry it into the Gentile world at scale.
Natural inequalityThe core of ancient thinking (Larry Siedentop): the assumption that some human beings are higher and others lower not by accident of circumstance but by nature, from birth, in their very constitution. Aristotle states it as plain description — “from the hour of their birth, some are marked out for subjection, others for rule.” Ancient justice <em>ranked</em> persons, keeping each in the tier nature assigned; modern justice <em>equalises</em>. They are nearly opposite ideas wearing the same word.
The cross reversed the contemptCrucifixion was Rome’s instrument for erasing a human being’s standing — the death reserved for slaves and rebels, engineered to strip its victim of dignity in public. And Christians said that God the Son, in his true human nature, had been executed by that very method (said of the one divine person without implying that the divine nature suffered or that the Father was crucified). The empire’s supreme act of contempt became the object of worship — the Alexamenos graffito jeers at exactly this — and a civilisation that came to adore as God a man put to a slave’s death could no longer coherently hold the humiliated contemptible.
Genealogy, not proofThe bounded shape of the whole claim. Tracing where a value came from tells you its history and its cost; it never by itself tells you the creed it grew from is true. A belief can have a noble pedigree and still be false, and the case for the faith’s truth rests above all on the resurrection and the historical and philosophical arguments made throughout the Library, not on its social fruit. But “not a proof” is not “not evidence.”
The honest atheist witness (Harari)The atheist historian Yuval Noah Harari grants the premise from the far side of the aisle: “there are no such things as rights in biology,” and “<em>Homo sapiens</em> has no natural rights, just as spiders, hyenas and chimpanzees have no natural rights.” Equality, in his account, is a conviction inherited from a Christian framework, not a fact you can find in the world. He is quoted only for the premise he shares — that equal worth is not written into nature — not for his own conclusion, that equality and rights are useful fictions, which this argument rejects.
Origins, not custodyThe precise scope of the historical claim. The conviction did not travel a straight or purely churchly road: much of its modern legal and political articulation came through the Enlightenment, sometimes phrased against the church and in a deliberately secular idiom of natural rights. The claim here is narrower, and about origins rather than custody — that the load-bearing premise those later thinkers inherited and secularised, that every person carries an equal and unearned worth, entered the Western imagination through the image of God and had no other stable home in the ancient world.
The argument, formally

Three lines. The whole weight rests on the two premises.

P1

Human equality — that every person has an equal, unearned worth — is a value, not a biological fact: human beings are radically unequal in every measurable capacity, so equal worth cannot be read off from nature (as even the atheist Harari concedes).

P2

This conviction was not the default of the ancient world, which assumed natural inequality; it entered the Western imagination through the Jewish, then Christian, claim that every human bears the image of God, and had no other stable home in antiquity.

∴ C

Therefore the conviction of equal worth has deep Jewish and Christian roots — a genealogy of the value, not a proof of the creed. A belief can have a noble pedigree and still be false; the case for the faith’s truth rests above all on the resurrection. But it is not nothing: equal, unearned worth is hard to ground in a universe with no God behind it (some hold it needs none, as a brute fact; the Christian answers that a moral source explains it better) — a step toward a moral source above nature (shared with Judaism), pointing toward the same God the resurrection reveals.

This is a genealogy of a conviction, and its conclusion is deliberately bounded: it shows that equal worth is not simply obvious or natural but was won, against the grain of the world, through the image of God. On its own it is no proof of the creed — a belief can have a noble pedigree and still be false — but “not a proof” is not “not evidence.”

The honest atheist witness: Harari Quoted for the premise only

You do not have to be a believer to accept the diagnosis. The historian Yuval Noah Harari, an atheist, puts it with admirable candour in Sapiens. Examine the American Declaration of Independence’s ringing claim that “all men are created equal,” he says, and biology gives it no support whatever: “there are no such things as rights in biology,” and “Homo sapiens has no natural rights, just as spiders, hyenas and chimpanzees have no natural rights.” Equality, in his account, is a story we tell — a conviction inherited, he notes, from a Christian framework — and not a fact you can find in the world.

Honest limit: Harari draws a conclusion from this that Christians do not share — that human equality and rights are useful fictions with no ground in reality; the point of quoting him is not that conclusion but his honesty about the premise.

Objection — “Human equality is just obvious”

It feels self-evident. Any decent person recoils at treating people as unequal, and calls it “dehumanising” — you don’t need a religion or a history lesson to see that everyone is of equal worth.

Reply

The feeling of obviousness is itself the thing to explain. As a measurement equal worth is false — humans differ in every testable way. For most of history most societies did not believe it, and the ancient world often believed the reverse: Aristotle held some are marked out from birth for rule, others for subjection. A conviction that had to be won against the grain of the world is not the default setting of the human mind. Its obviousness to a modern Westerner is evidence of how completely one particular inheritance has soaked into the air we breathe.

Objection — Harari: equality is a useful fiction

Equality is a story we tell, not a fact you can find in the world; human equality and rights are useful fictions with no ground in reality.

Reply

Harari’s own conclusion — that human equality and rights are useful fictions with no ground in reality — is one this track rejects; he is quoted only for the premise he shares with the argument, that equal worth is not a fact of nature. That it is not a fact of nature does not make it a fiction: some philosophers hold such worth simply obtains with no God, and the Christian’s claim is the modest one, that a Maker who confers it explains the conviction better than leaving it a brute fact.

Defending Premise 2

This conviction was not the default of the ancient world, which assumed natural inequality; it entered the Western imagination through the Jewish, then Christian, claim that every human bears the image of God, and had no other stable home in antiquity

Precision check: a claim about origins rather than custody. Nor did the conviction travel a straight or purely churchly road: much of its modern legal and political articulation came through the Enlightenment, sometimes phrased against the church and in a deliberately secular idiom of natural rights. The claim is that the load-bearing premise those later thinkers inherited and secularised — that every person carries an equal and unearned worth — entered the Western imagination through the image of God and had no other stable home in the ancient world. The root is Jewish; the church did not invent it.

The world that ran the other way Strong

The ancient Mediterranean was hierarchical in its account of being. Some human beings were held to be higher and others lower not by accident of circumstance but by nature, from birth, in their very constitution. Aristotle states it without embarrassment, because to him it was simply description: “For that some should rule and others be ruled is a thing not only necessary, but expedient; from the hour of their birth, some are marked out for subjection, others for rule.” The intellectual historian Larry Siedentop puts the point flatly: “the core of ancient thinking” was “the assumption of natural inequality.” Modern justice equalises; ancient justice ranked.

Honest limit: Individual voices did dissent: some Stoics and Roman jurists reasoned toward a common humanity. Seneca could write that a slave is a fellow human being; the jurist Ulpian recorded the principle that, so far as natural law is concerned, all human beings are equal. But none of it became a movement, a law, or a settled conviction. Seneca kept his slaves; Ulpian’s maxim sat inside a legal code that enforced slavery. Siedentop’s larger thesis — that Christianity is where the equal individual was first thought — is itself debated among historians of political thought; the narrower point, that the dominant strand of ancient thought assumed natural inequality, is widely accepted.

What the old stories took for granted Strong

In the Babylonian creation epic Enuma Elish, humanity is manufactured from the blood of a slain god, and mankind is charged with the service of the gods, so that the gods may be at ease. The older Mesopotamian poem Atrahasis is blunter still: humanity is created to take over the gods’ labour — “let man bear the load of the gods.” We are, in these stories, the gods’ unpaid workforce, brought into existence for our usefulness. It is an anthropology in which a person’s worth just is their utility.

In AD 61 a senator named Pedanius Secundus, the prefect of the city of Rome, was murdered by one of his own slaves. Ancient custom prescribed that the entire household of slaves — around four hundred men, women and children, nearly all of them innocent — be executed together. The jurist Gaius Cassius carried the Senate, arguing that “there is some injustice in every great precedent, which, though injurious to individuals, has its compensation in the public advantage.” The four hundred went to their deaths. The individual is real but negligible; the collective is what counts.

The claim that broke it — Jewish in root The core warrant

The opening page of Genesis says that God made humanity — not the king, not the free man, not the Greek, but humanity, male and female alike — in his own image. The claim is that worth is not earned or measured but conferred, bestowed on every human being by the Maker, and therefore identical in each regardless of capacity or rank. That doctrine developed in Jewish scripture and Jewish interpretation, and it stands on its own feet; the church did not invent it. What the church did was carry it into the Gentile world at scale.

The cross reversed the contempt Suggestive

The church welded the image of God to a second claim that struck the ancient ear as insane. Crucifixion was Rome’s instrument for erasing a human being’s standing: the death reserved for slaves and rebels, engineered to strip its victim of dignity in public. And Christians said that God the Son, in his true human nature, had been executed by that very method. Paul knew how it sounded — he called the message of a crucified Messiah “a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles.” On the Palatine Hill in Rome, scratched into a plaster wall around AD 200, a Roman hand drew a man lifting his hand toward a figure hanging on a cross — a figure with the head of a donkey — and wrote beneath it, “Alexamenos worships his god.” It is a jeer at a Christian for adoring a crucified man, and it is probably the earliest surviving image of the crucifixion. Inside the church, the same reversal was stated as membership: “There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus.”

Honest limit: Galatians 3:28 is a claim about standing before God, not a description of social facts; it names those three axes precisely because they were the axes on which the ancient world ranked persons. And whatever the civilisation went on doing in practice, the church has been a repeated traitor to its own doctrine.

From the body to natural rights Contested thesis

In the Roman world an enslaved person had no bodily integrity a master was bound to respect. The historian Kyle Harper has documented how the early church broke with that assumption at its root, holding that every body, the slave’s included, bore a God-given dignity no owner’s power could annul. The philosopher Nicholas Wolterstorff has argued this is the real seedbed of the modern idea of natural, inherent human rights: rights are grounded in a worth that is bestowed, and the moral resources of Greek and Roman antiquity could not, by themselves, have produced it. The historian Brian Tierney has argued that canon lawyers commenting on Gratian’s Decretum (c. 1140) were already speaking of natural rights as powers belonging to individual persons.

Honest limit: Wolterstorff’s is a contested thesis, defended and disputed within the philosophy of rights; it is cited as the most developed scholarly statement of the grounding, not as a settled result.

Objection — “The Greeks and Romans already believed in a common humanity”

Some Stoics and jurists reached toward it: Seneca could call a slave a fellow human being, and the jurist Ulpian recorded that by natural law all human beings are equal. The idea was already there.

Reply

Those voices were real, and honesty names them. But two things are true of them: they grounded common humanity in a shared nature (for the Stoics, a shared capacity, reason) rather than in a worth bestowed on each, and — decisively — none became a movement, a law, or a settled conviction. Seneca kept his slaves; Ulpian’s maxim sat inside a code that enforced slavery. The dissent lived as an idea and never became anything more.

Objection — “A noble pedigree is no proof — and the church enslaved people” Hardest

A conviction can have Christian roots and still be a human invention that outgrew them; and the church blessed slaveholding, tolerated caste, turned on the Jewish people from whom it had received the teaching of the image of God, and looked away from cruelties its own scriptures condemned.

Reply

The pedigree point is correct, and this track concedes it in our own voice: on its own this is no proof of the creed — a belief can have a noble pedigree and still be false, and the case for the faith’s truth rests above all on the resurrection. But “not a proof” is not “not evidence.” The church’s failures are real, and no account of where the standard came from lessens them. But we can call the church’s betrayals betrayals only because there was a standard to betray — and it was, in no small part, the church’s own standard that the reformers turned back against it. Many of the abolitionists who indicted Christian slaveholders did so from Genesis and the image of God, even as other Christians marshalled Scripture to defend slavery, in what became a genuine crisis of biblical interpretation (Mark Noll). Taken by itself this line of evidence reaches a moral source above nature — a step toward God, shared with Judaism, not yet the resurrection, but pointing toward the same God the resurrection reveals.

The honest state of the debate

A fairness most defenders skip: The premise is conceded from the far side of the aisle by the atheist historian Yuval Noah Harari (“there are no such things as rights in biology”), who nonetheless draws a conclusion Christians reject — that equality is a useful fiction with no ground in reality. Larry Siedentop’s larger thesis, that Christianity is where the equal individual was first thought, is itself debated among historians of political thought; the narrower point drawn on here, that the dominant strand of ancient thought assumed natural inequality, is widely accepted. Nicholas Wolterstorff’s grounding of inherent rights in a worth bestowed by God, and Brian Tierney’s canon-law genealogy of natural rights, are contested theses within the philosophy and history of rights, cited as the most developed scholarly statements of the case, not as settled results. Some philosophers hold equal worth simply obtains with no God. And the church has been a repeated traitor to its own doctrine — it blessed slaveholding, tolerated caste, and turned on the Jewish people from whom it had received the teaching of the image of God; Mark Noll documents the American slavery debate as a genuine crisis of biblical interpretation.

The bounded verdict: So when you find yourself certain — as you should be — that a trafficked child and a titan of industry are of exactly equal worth, that the strong owe something to the weak simply because they are human, you are not stating a fact of biology or a deliverance of pure reason. You are standing at the end of a long and improbable history, most of which ran the other way. The conviction of equal, unearned worth entered the Western imagination through the image of God — Jewish in root, carried by the church — and had no other stable home in the ancient world. That is a genealogy of a value, not a proof of a creed: on its own it is no proof of the faith, which rests above all on the resurrection. But it is not nothing — equal worth is hard to ground in a universe with no God behind it, so taken by itself this line of evidence reaches a moral source above nature, shared with Judaism, pointing toward the same God the resurrection reveals.

    "Even in Aristotle’s own day, Greeks argued that slavery was a mere human convention, contrary to nature — the very position he wrote his defence of natural slavery against.     How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    Honesty requires naming the dissent, and the essay does: Aristotle argues for natural slavery precisely because others in his own day were calling slavery a mere human convention, contrary to nature. But that cuts the other way — that Aristotle had to argue for natural inequality shows it was the dominant assumption he was defending. And here is the decisive fact: none of that dissent became a movement, a law, or a settled conviction that the weak have a claim on the strong simply by being human. Seneca kept his slaves; the jurist Ulpian’s maxim that by natural law all human beings are equal sat inside a legal code that enforced slavery. The dissent lived as an idea and never became anything more. The conviction that every person carries an equal, unearned worth — conferred rather than earned, and identical in each — entered Western law and sentiment largely through a different route: the image of God, Jewish in root and carried by the church into the Gentile world.
