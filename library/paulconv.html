Why Did Paul Convert to Christianity? His Conversion and the Early Creed

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

Short on time? Read the quick answer →

The witness who had everything to lose

Most testimony to the resurrection comes from Jesus' own followers. The women at the tomb had followed Jesus; the eleven had left their nets for him; the grieving, a skeptic will say, could be suspected of seeing what they longed to see. That is precisely what makes one witness different. Before he was the Apostle Paul, he was Saul of Tarsus, and he did not love Jesus, did not follow him, did not grieve him, and did not expect him. He hunted his followers. He was, by his own unembarrassed admission, trying to destroy the movement he would die defending.1

This is the testimony that resists the easy explanations. You cannot call it grief-driven wish-fulfillment in a man who was not grieving. You cannot call it the fevered hope of a disciple in a man who thought the whole thing a blasphemy worth killing for. Whatever happened on the Damascus road, it turned the church's most capable enemy into its most relentless missionary — and it did so early enough that Paul could carry the resurrection claim back to the very men who said they had seen it, and check. This essay is about that man and that check. The four-line creed he quotes in 1 Corinthians 15 is treated in depth in its own companion essay;2 here the creed is the supporting witness, and Paul is the case.

The argument, stated with care

The reasoning from Paul to the resurrection is an inference to the best explanation, and it runs in a chain:

1. Saul of Tarsus was a sincere, capable persecutor of the early church — a hostile witness with no prior allegiance to Jesus.
2. He underwent a sudden, total transformation that he attributed to an encounter with the risen Jesus.
3. He paid for that claim with hardship and, by strong early tradition, finally with his life, and he had independent means to verify it — meeting the named witnesses in Jerusalem within a few years of the crucifixion.
4. The ordinary naturalistic engines of religious conviction — grief, prior hope, social pressure, gradual legend — do not fit his case.
Therefore Paul's transformation is best explained by his sincerely having experienced what he claimed, and his testimony anchors the resurrection appearances to the earliest moment of the movement.

Notice the modest shape of the conclusion. It does not, by itself, prove that Jesus rose bodily from the dead; an honest critic can grant that Paul was sincere and still ask whether he was mistaken. What the argument establishes is that the resurrection belief was held, from the start, by a man uniquely insulated from the usual psychological pressures — and that fact narrows the field of explanations dramatically. Where the argument ends, and where the harder inference to a real resurrection begins, is something we will mark plainly.

Saul the persecutor: the hostile witness

The most important fact about Paul is the one he never tried to hide. Writing to the Galatians, he reminds them of "my former life in Judaism, how I persecuted the church of God violently and tried to destroy it. And I was advancing in Judaism beyond many of my own age among my people, so extremely zealous was I for the traditions of my fathers" (Galatians 1:13-14).3 To the Philippians he lists his credentials with a kind of bitter pride: "circumcised on the eighth day, of the people of Israel, of the tribe of Benjamin, a Hebrew of Hebrews; as to the law, a Pharisee; as to zeal, a persecutor of the church" (Philippians 3:5-6).4

This is not a hostile source describing Paul; it is Paul describing himself, in letters whose authenticity even the most skeptical critics accept. The German historian Martin Hengel, in his study The Pre-Christian Paul, insisted that "knowledge of Saul the Jew is a precondition of understanding Paul the Christian," and reconstructed a man of serious Pharisaic training and zealous conviction — exactly the sort of educated insider who understood what the Christian claim meant and rejected it as intolerable.5 Saul was not a confused bystander. He grasped the claim that a crucified man was Israel's Messiah, and he found it a scandal — for a hanged man was under God's curse (Deuteronomy 21:23), and a crucified Messiah was a contradiction in terms to a first-century Pharisee. This was no private distaste but the public heart of the message he was trying to destroy: what the Christians proclaimed was, in Paul's own later words, “Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles” (1 Corinthians 1:23) — the one claim best calculated to make an educated Pharisee treat the movement as a blasphemy to be stamped out.

This is the heart of the hostile-witness argument. Consider what Paul did not have:

No grief. Paul had never been a disciple. He had nothing to mourn, no relationship to recover, no beloved teacher whose loss the mind might soften with a comforting vision. The bereavement-vision mechanism that skeptics invoke for the grieving disciples has no purchase on a man who wanted the dead man's followers in chains.

No prior expectation. Far from hoping for Jesus' vindication, Saul's entire theological framework ruled it out. He was not primed to "see" a risen Messiah; everything he believed told him a crucified pretender stayed dead and disgraced. As N. T. Wright stresses, the resurrection of Jesus forced Paul to rethink from the ground up everything he had previously believed about God, Messiah, and the end of history.6

The depth of that reversal shows in what Paul later did with the very text that had condemned the claim. The Scripture that made a hanged man accursed, he came to quote as the hinge of his gospel, applying it directly to the crucified Christ: “Cursed is everyone who is hanged on a tree” (Galatians 3:13, drawing on Deuteronomy 21:23). The man who had hunted the church because a crucified Messiah stood under God's curse ended by preaching that curse as the point of the whole story. A person does not take his own strongest objection and make it the centre of his message on his own initiative — and that is exactly why the absence of any prior expectation cuts so deep here. Saul's framework did not merely fail to anticipate a risen Messiah; it branded the claim accursed, on scriptural grounds he had mastered. That a considered, text-based revulsion could reverse completely is far harder to explain as wishful drift than as what Paul insisted it was: a meeting with the one he had been certain was dead and disgraced.

No social incentive. Conversion cost Saul everything an ambitious Pharisee valued — status, security, the approval of his teachers, his very identity. He traded the persecutor's authority for the convert's danger, and was soon being lowered in a basket through a hole in the Damascus wall to escape the very kind of arrest he had once carried out (2 Corinthians 11:32-33).7

A movement's enemies do not usually defect to it, at ruinous personal cost, on the strength of nothing. Something happened.

The transformation and its own explanation

Paul tells us what he thinks happened, and he tells us tersely. He places his own experience at the end of the resurrection witness list — "last of all, as to one untimely born, he appeared also to me" (1 Corinthians 15:8) — using the same verb, ōphthē ("he appeared"), used of the appearances to Peter, the Twelve, and the rest.8 Elsewhere he describes it as God having been "pleased to reveal his Son to me" (Galatians 1:16) and asks, pointedly, "Have I not seen Jesus our Lord?" (1 Corinthians 9:1).9

Two features of his account deserve weight. First, Paul never speaks of his experience as a private inward awakening but as a seeing — and, crucially, he groups it with the others' experiences as the same kind of event, even while marking it as last and abnormal. Second, the transformation was not gradual. He did not study his way into the church over years; by his own testimony and the independent narrative of Acts, the persecutor became the proclaimer almost at once. Whatever its cause, the cause was sudden, external in seeming, and powerful enough to overturn a life.

Paul's reversal did not stay private, either; it became public knowledge in the region he had terrorized, and quickly. Writing to the Galatians, he recalls that the churches of Judea "only were hearing it said, 'He who used to persecute us is now preaching the faith he once tried to destroy.' And they glorified God because of me" (Galatians 1:23-24).10 This is not Paul advertising himself; it is Paul reporting what other Christian communities were already saying about him — a near-contemporary echo that originated apart from Paul's own self-description, circulating among the very people he had hunted, while they were alive to deny it if it were false.

The primacy of Paul: the historian's most direct witness

Step back and ask a simple question: of everyone who claimed to have seen the risen Jesus, whose testimony can we read for ourselves? For Peter, the Twelve, and the more than five hundred, we have it only at second hand — through the early creed Paul passed on, and through Gospels and an Acts written about them by others. Paul is the exception. He alone speaks to us directly, in his own letters, in the first person, a man we can name and date. The New Testament scholar Michael Licona calls this "the primacy of Paul": with everyone else we read a report about a witness; with Paul we read the witness.11

Three things give that fact its force. The first is that the documents are early and their authorship is not seriously disputed. Seven of the letters bearing Paul's name — Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, and Philemon — are accepted as genuinely his by near-universal agreement across the critical spectrum, and are dated to roughly AD 48-60. Every one of these letters therefore falls within about fifteen to thirty years of the crucifixion: first-generation correspondence from a known author, standing closer to the events it reports than most literary sources a historian of antiquity is glad to have.

The second is that Paul writes throughout as a man reporting his own experience, not relaying someone else's. "Have I not seen Jesus our Lord?" he asks (1 Corinthians 9:1);9 "last of all... he appeared also to me" (1 Corinthians 15:8);8 and he even takes the pen from his scribe to add a line in his own hand (Galatians 6:11). Every other appearance in the record reaches us as testimony about a witness; Paul's reaches us from one.

The third is what this early, first-person testimony carries with it — the two features developed elsewhere in this essay. Paul was a hostile witness, as the previous section showed, and he was in direct personal contact with the Jerusalem leadership, as the next section will show. Here is Licona's payoff: because Paul met Peter, James, and John, laid his gospel before them, and reports that they endorsed it rather than corrected it — they "added nothing" and gave "the right hand of fellowship" (Galatians 2) — his letters become a means of recovering what the Jerusalem apostles themselves proclaimed, and that proclamation already had a risen Jesus at its heart. This is endorsement, not an audit: not that the eyewitnesses fact-checked Paul's memory line by line, but that the two streams of testimony, set side by side by the men who owned them, did not conflict.

None of this, it must be said plainly, proves that Jesus rose. That Paul is our earliest and most direct witness establishes only that the resurrection claim was made — in the first person, by a named, formerly hostile eyewitness, at the movement's foundation — not that the claim is true. The primacy of Paul tells us only how close to the source we stand: as close as ancient history ever lets us stand. What happened at the source is a question for the rest of the evidence to decide.

The Jerusalem visits: Paul met the witnesses

Here the argument gains a feature most ancient testimony lacks: the witness himself describes personal acquaintance with the other named witnesses — not a forensic audit of who-saw-what. In Galatians 1, written to defend his independence, Paul lays out a timeline he has every reason to report accurately, because his opponents could have contradicted it. After his conversion, he says, he did not immediately consult the Jerusalem leaders; he went to Arabia, returned to Damascus, and only "after three years" went up to Jerusalem "to visit Cephas and remained with him fifteen days" — adding, "But I saw none of the other apostles except James the Lord's brother" (Galatians 1:18-19).12

The verb Paul chooses — historēsai, the only time it appears in the New Testament — carries the sense of inquiry, of getting to know a person and his account, not merely paying a social call.13 For fifteen days Paul had Peter to himself. It strains credulity to suppose the subject of the resurrection — the thing both men staked their lives on — never came up. And the man he names alongside Peter, James the Lord's brother, is precisely one of the named recipients of an appearance in the creed Paul quotes (1 Corinthians 15:7).

Fourteen years later, Paul made a second, more public visit. He went up to Jerusalem again, this time laying his gospel "before those who seemed influential... in order to make sure I was not running or had not run in vain" (Galatians 2:1-2). The "pillars" — James, Cephas, and John — "added nothing to me," recognized the grace given him, and "gave the right hand of fellowship" (Galatians 2:6-9).14 Whatever later friction there was at Antioch (Galatians 2:11-14), on the core gospel Paul and the original eyewitnesses were agreed. This matters enormously: the man who said he had seen the risen Jesus on the Damascus road and the men who claimed to have seen him in Jerusalem reached and confirmed a shared gospel, twice, and did not contradict each other.

The skeptic's natural worry — that the appearance traditions are independent inventions that grew up separately and were harmonized late — founders here. Paul reports direct, early, personal contact with the principal witnesses. He explicitly tells the Corinthians that the message is shared: "Whether then it was I or they, so we preach and so you believed" (1 Corinthians 15:11).15

Anchoring the creed's early date

This is where Paul's biography and the famous creed lock together. The creed of 1 Corinthians 15:3-7 is, by wide scholarly agreement, a formula Paul "received" and "delivered" — pre-Pauline tradition he is passing on, not composing.16 The question is only how early. Paul's own chronology supplies the answer. If the crucifixion fell around AD 30-33 and Paul's conversion within a few years of it, then his receipt of the tradition — at his conversion, or confirmed in that fifteen-day Jerusalem visit "after three years" — pushes the formula's origin into the first half of the 30s, within roughly two to five years of the cross.

It is worth stressing how little of this depends on Christian presupposition. The atheist New Testament scholar Gerd Lüdemann judged that "the elements in the tradition are to be dated to the first two years after the crucifixion of Jesus... not later than three years."17 Bart Ehrman, equally no friend of the resurrection, grants that the creed is early and that Paul received it from those before him.18 The full case for this dating, and what it does and does not prove, is the burden of the companion essay on the creed; the point here is simply that Paul is the reason we can date it. His autobiography in Galatians is the chronological backbone. Remove Paul, and the creed floats free of any fixed date; with Paul, it is nailed to the foundation of the movement.

The strongest objection: it was only a vision

The most serious naturalistic reply does not deny that Paul had an experience or that he was sincere. It reinterprets the experience. Paul, the objection runs, had a subjective vision — a powerful inner episode, perhaps psychologically generated — which he, in good faith, took for an encounter with the risen Lord. No corpse need have left any tomb.

Gerd Lüdemann presses this case from inside the early date he himself defends. He grants that Paul had a real, transformative "appearance" experience that goes back to the earliest period, but interprets it as the resolution of a repressed inner conflict — a "Christ complex" in which Saul's unconscious attraction to the gospel he was violently resisting finally broke through.19 Ehrman, more cautiously, locates Paul's experience in the broad category of visionary experiences that historians can document while staying agnostic about their cause.20 One should concede what is true here: the bare report "Paul saw Jesus" is, on its face, logically compatible with a vision rather than a bodily appearance. The argument cannot be settled by the word ōphthē alone.

But the vision hypothesis carries burdens that grow heavier the closer one looks. First, Lüdemann's "Christ complex" is, as the philosopher William Lane Craig argues in a detailed assessment, a speculative psychoanalysis of a man we cannot put on the couch — read into the texts rather than out of them, and resting on the assumption that Saul was secretly drawn to what he was in fact destroying.21 The texts give no hint of inner turmoil; they give us a man advancing confidently in his zeal.

Second, the vision reading was designed for the grieving disciples and does almost no work for Paul. Bereavement and longing are the usual psychological fuel for such experiences, and Paul had neither. A theory that explains the disciples' visions by their grief cannot, without strain, be transferred to the one witness who was not grieving.

Third — and this is the honest limit on both sides — Paul's case does not stand alone. He himself anchors his experience to a shared tradition of bodily appearances and to named witnesses he had personally interviewed. The vision hypothesis, to do its work, must explain not only Paul but the empty tomb, the group appearances, and the conversion of James, the skeptical brother who likewise had no grief-driven longing for a risen Messiah.22 James is treated in his own essay on the conversion of skeptics; here it is enough to note that Paul is not an isolated anomaly to be explained away but one of two hostile witnesses, converging. A theory that must independently invent a different psychological accident for each non-grieving convert is buying its way out of the evidence rather than explaining it.

There is a sharper, mirror-image form of the objection that presses downward rather than up. Bart Ehrman notes that Paul's is the only appearance described in the first person by the person who had it, and that Paul characterizes it as a heavenly revelation — God "was pleased to reveal his Son to me" (Galatians 1:16), rendered in Acts as a blinding light and a voice rather than a face-to-face meal. If Paul's experience belongs on the same list as the others, the objection runs, then perhaps the whole list should be read down to Paul's level — all of them visionary, none of them bodily. This is the vision theory in its strongest form, and it gets a full answer here. The answer is that the shared verb ōphthē ("he was seen") establishes a shared kind of claim, not a shared mechanism; Paul lists his experience alongside the others without telling us the physiology of any of them. And three things block a clean level-down. The Gospel appearances are reported with a tangible, bodily texture Paul's Damascus light does not share — shared meals, touch, wounds probed. The empty tomb, argued in its own essay, leaves a bodily absence no purely inward vision accounts for. And Paul himself, far from dissolving resurrection into a vision, insists on a sōma — a body — "what is sown is perishable; what is raised is imperishable" (1 Corinthians 15:42-44), the exact opposite of a disembodied apparition. The list can be read up toward Paul's conviction that he had truly seen the risen Lord; it cannot be read down into mere subjectivity without breaking against the bodily data on every side.

A medical objection: “it was a temporal-lobe seizure”

A different naturalistic reply comes from medicine rather than psychoanalysis, and it deserves to be put at full strength. In 1987 the Journal of Neurology, Neurosurgery, and Psychiatry published a paper by D. Landsborough, “St Paul and temporal lobe epilepsy,” which offered evidence “to suggest a neurological origin for Paul’s ecstatic visions” and postulated that those visions and Paul’s physical state at his conversion were both manifestations of temporal lobe epilepsy.23 The case assembles itself readily from the texts. In Acts, Saul is surrounded by a sudden light, falls to the ground, hears a voice, and is then three days without sight, neither eating nor drinking (Acts 9:3-9). In his own letters Paul speaks of visions and revelations and of being caught up into paradise (2 Corinthians 12:1-4), of a “bodily ailment” (Galatians 4:13), and of a recurring affliction he could not shake: “a thorn was given me in the flesh, a messenger of Satan to harass me,” and three times he pleaded for it to leave him (2 Corinthians 12:7-8).24 Put those together, the argument runs, and you have a single dramatic seizure followed by prolonged postictal blindness, set inside a chronic condition with recurring ecstatic auras. The proposal has not gone away. A 2024 paper in Dementia & Neuropsychologia reads the thorn as that chronic condition, concludes that Paul “probably had temporal lobe epilepsy,” and finds in his letters — intense religiosity, a sense of personal destiny, prolific writing — the traits of the so-called Gastaut-Geschwind syndrome.25

One thing should be granted plainly, because it is true: seizures can be accompanied by vivid religious experiences. A 2008 review in Epilepsy & Behavior by Orrin Devinsky and George Lai reports that between 0.4 and 3.1 percent of patients with partial epilepsy have had religious experiences during seizures, and that many of the religious conversions associated with epilepsy occurred after a seizure, in the postictal period.26 Nor is anyone’s dignity at stake in the question; a great many people live with epilepsy, and nothing in Paul’s gospel would be diminished if he had been one of them. The only question is whether a seizure explains what happened to Saul. It does not, for four reasons.

First, the diagnosis cannot actually be made. Epilepsy is diagnosed in a living patient — by taking a history, observing the episodes, and today by recording the brain’s electrical activity. A retrospective diagnosis has none of these; it has a few sentences of ancient narrative, written for other purposes, and a modern pattern-match. The medical literature is itself divided on Paul. The authors of the 2024 paper concede that his diagnosis of epilepsy “is not consensual among historians,” that other authors argue for a mood disorder with psychotic features instead, and that the religiosity and sense of destiny they read as symptoms “are observed not only in epileptic people.”25 A 2021 review in Epilepsy & Behavior argued that the evidence that Paul and several other religious figures had epilepsy is weak, stressed “the limitations of imposing contemporary neurological frameworks” on them, and concluded that the relationship between religion and epilepsy “has been overstated in the academic literature.”27 And the same method, applied to the same texts, has produced a rival diagnosis altogether: a 2012 paper in the Journal of Neuropsychiatry and Clinical Neurosciences proposed that Paul’s experiences resemble what are now defined as psychotic symptoms, while acknowledging “limitations inherent to a retrospective diagnostic examination.”28 When one set of verses yields epilepsy for one reader and psychosis for another, the verses are not settling the diagnosis; the framework each reader brings to them is doing the work.

Second, the thorn cannot carry the weight put on it. Paul never says what it was. The International Standard Bible Encyclopaedia of 1915 put the matter bluntly: “The data are insufficient to enable us to ascertain its real nature, and all the speculations on the point are therefore inconclusive.” Its own survey shows the spread: Ramsay proposed a recurring malarial fever, Farrar ophthalmia, a disease of the eyes, Krenkel epilepsy — a case the article judged “a special pleading for a foregone conclusion.”29 An ailment that interpreters have identified in turn as fever, eye disease, and seizures cannot be counted as evidence for any one of them without first assuming it. More important, Paul’s own words set the thorn against his revelations, not behind them: it was given “to keep me from becoming conceited because of the surpassing greatness of the revelations,” and he calls it “a messenger of Satan to harass me” (2 Corinthians 12:7). The same article made the point a century ago: Paul “would not have called his visions ‘a messenger of Satan.’” The theory must make one condition the source both of what Paul experienced as God revealing his Son to him (Galatians 1:16) and of what he experienced as an affliction sent to humble him — a fusion his own account never makes and gives us no means to supply.

Third, the account describes an event, not a symptom. A seizure happens inside one person’s brain. The narratives the diagnosis relies on describe something that reached the men traveling with Saul as well: they “stood speechless, hearing the voice but seeing no one” (Acts 9:7); they “saw the light but did not understand the voice” (Acts 22:9); “we had all fallen to the ground” (Acts 26:14).30 These details need careful handling. They are Luke’s accounts, not Paul’s letters; they differ in what they say the companions perceived; and a skeptic is free to discount them. But the diagnosis cannot discount them without discounting itself. The fall, the voice, the three days of blindness and fasting — every clinical detail of the Damascus episode that the seizure theory works with — come from these same narratives in Acts; Paul’s own letters mention none of them. The theory cannot take Luke’s symptoms and discard his companions without a reason; and the reason usually given — that the accounts disagree — cuts against the symptoms too, since the blindness and fasting also vary across the three tellings. So the diagnosis is built on the one source it most needs to distrust.

Fourth, and most important, a seizure could explain at most an experience, never its content. Grant, for the sake of argument, that something neurological happened on the Damascus road. The question this essay has been asking would be untouched: why did this experience issue in this conclusion? Saul’s framework, as we saw, branded a crucified Messiah accursed under Deuteronomy 21:23. The content was not unknown to him — he was persecuting the people who preached it — but a seizure supplies no reason to adopt it, and everything he believed pointed the other way; to say the seizure tipped him into his victims’ creed is to fall back on the hidden-attraction reading already found to be speculation the texts do not support. The conversions after seizures reported by Devinsky and Lai show that a seizure can be the occasion of a change of belief; they do not show that it can supply the belief, and in Saul’s case the belief is what needs explaining: that a man his own Scriptures pronounced accursed had been raised and was Lord. Nor does a seizure explain what followed: a mission sustained for some three decades through beatings, imprisonments, and shipwreck (2 Corinthians 11:23-25), argued out in letters of sustained reasoning, and — above all — checked. Paul did not simply trust his experience; he went up to Jerusalem, spent fifteen days with Peter, met James, and later laid his gospel before the pillars, who added nothing to it. None of this questions whether a person with epilepsy can think clearly and live purposefully; of course they can. It says that the brain event the theory posits, whatever it was, is not what produced the claim, sustained it, or had it recognized as one shared gospel by the men who said they had seen the risen Jesus in Jerusalem. And even if a neural correlate of Paul’s experience could somehow be identified, the point made in this library’s essay on the argument from religious experience would still apply: showing that an experience has a neural correlate shows nothing about whether it is veridical.

Behind all four stands the problem the vision theory also meets. Paul places his experience at the end of a list — Cephas, the Twelve, more than five hundred at one time, James, all the apostles (1 Corinthians 15:5-8). Even a successful diagnosis would explain one man. The group appearances, the empty tomb, and the conversion of James would each still need an explanation of their own, and the rule stated against the vision theory applies here unchanged: a theory that has to supply a separate natural accident for every witness it cannot otherwise reach is not explaining the evidence but buying its way out of it.

So the answer to the objection is no: the temporal-lobe epilepsy theory does not hold. It cannot show that Paul had epilepsy — the diagnosis is contested, as even its recent proponents acknowledge, the “thorn” is never identified, and Paul himself sets it against his revelations rather than behind them. And even if some neurological event had occurred, the theory would explain neither the event as it is reported — an encounter that, on Luke’s account, reached the men beside him — nor its content: a persecutor’s sudden conviction that the crucified man he held accursed was raised and Lord, a lifelong mission built on that conviction, and a gospel he then set before the other witnesses and found to be theirs as well. What would be needed to overturn that is not a possible diagnosis but an explanation of the content, and the seizure theory does not offer one. As with every alternative in this essay, answering it does not by itself prove the resurrection; it removes one more way of explaining Paul without it.

The everyday objection: “people convert all the time”

A more ordinary objection arrives from a different direction, and it is usually put as a question: people change religion constantly, every tradition has its dramatic converts, and some of them describe experiences as vivid as Paul’s. Why should this one turn need any special explanation at all?

The reply is narrow, and it should stay narrow. Most conversions — in every tradition, this one included — run through testimony: someone hears a case made, weighs what others report, and comes to believe them. There is nothing second-rate about that; it is how nearly all of us come to nearly all of what we believe. It is simply not what Paul claims. He does not say he was persuaded by the disciples’ preaching; he says he was stopped by something he took himself to have perceived, at a point when he was not weighing Christian testimony but suppressing it. An explanation calibrated to persuasion-by-report therefore does not transfer to him: not because his experience was of a better kind than anyone else’s, but because his is not that kind of case.

Two things this does not say, and one it leaves standing. It does not say that converts in other traditions are insincere, or that they never report first-person experiences of their own; many do — those are real reports, and what they are reports of is exactly the disputed question, which the argument from religious experience treats as the strongest objection in its own field, and which that essay argues limits its case rather than destroying it. And it does not say that Paul’s experience was therefore veridical; this essay’s own answer, stated above and again below, is that Paul alone cannot settle that. What is left standing is the real question — whether Paul’s case is distinctive among experience-driven conversions at all — and the answer to that is not that he had an experience, but the three things already laid out here: no grief, no prior expectation, no social incentive, in a man who then went and checked his account against the named men who claimed the same thing. The commonness of conversion is a fact about persuasion; Paul is not an instance of it.

Even the atheist philosopher Michael Martin, a determined critic of Christianity, grants that — apart from Paul — the New Testament preserves no first-hand claim by a named individual to have seen the risen Jesus; Paul alone (1 Corinthians 15:8) writes as a contemporary eyewitness in his own hand.31 That a hostile critic concedes the point underscores how unusual Paul's testimony is — though it is, of course, no concession that the resurrection occurred.

The live dispute, honestly stated, is not over what happened to Paul but over how to explain it. Did he encounter a risen person, or undergo a vivid subjective experience he sincerely misread? That question cannot be closed by Paul's testimony in isolation; it is decided by the cumulative case — whether a bodily resurrection or some combination of naturalistic accidents better explains the whole pattern of the empty tomb, the multiple appearances, and the two hostile-witness conversions together. Gary Habermas's long-running survey of the academic literature finds broad agreement that the disciples and Paul had early, sincere experiences they took to be appearances of the risen Jesus; what that survey does not show, and does not claim to show, is consensus that the experiences were veridical.32 The data are agreed; the inference is where the real debate lives.

What Paul's conversion establishes — and what it doesn't

Paul's conversion does a specific and powerful job. It plants the resurrection belief, at the earliest traceable moment, in the mind of a witness who had every motive to reject it and none to invent it. It removes from the table the tidiest naturalistic explanations — grief, wish-fulfillment, prior expectation, slow legendary growth — at least for this witness. And through Paul's own movements between Damascus and Jerusalem, it anchors the early creed to a fixed date and ties the appearance traditions back to the named men who claimed to have seen the Lord.

What it does not do is prove, by itself, that Jesus rose. A determined skeptic can still say Paul was sincere and mistaken — that a subjective experience, however inexplicable in its trigger, fell short of a real encounter with a living man. That remaining gap is closed only by the convergence of the evidence: the empty tomb, the range and independence of the appearances, and the second hostile conversion of James, each treated in its own essay. And this is what we hold to be true: Jesus of Nazareth was bodily raised from the dead, and the wider case — the empty tomb, the failure of the alternatives, the conversions of James and Paul — shows it. Paul is not the whole argument. He is its most stubborn single witness — the enemy who changed sides and then went, in person, to check the story with the men who started it.

That is why his testimony has weighed on serious inquirers for two thousand years, believer and skeptic alike. And it is why the case is best made, as Peter himself is remembered to have urged, "with gentleness and respect" (1 Peter 3:15) — confident in what the evidence shows about the man, and candid about where his testimony alone stops.

Frequently asked questions

Why does Paul's conversion count as evidence if he could just have been sincerely mistaken?

It counts because of who Paul was, not merely because he was sincere. A critic can grant Paul was sincere and still ask whether he was mistaken, so the argument is an inference to the best explanation, not a proof of bodily resurrection. What it does establish is that the resurrection belief was held, from the start, by a hostile witness insulated from grief, prior hope, and social incentive—a fact that dramatically narrows the field of naturalistic explanations. Paul is the first premise of the case, not the whole of it — and we hold that the whole case shows Jesus did rise.

Wasn't Paul's Damascus road experience just a subjective vision, not a real encounter?

No — though the bare words 'Paul saw Jesus' are logically compatible with a vision, the vision reading does not fit. Gerd Lüdemann interprets it as a repressed 'Christ complex' breaking through. But as William Lane Craig argues, that is speculative psychoanalysis of a man we cannot examine, read into texts that show him advancing confidently in his zeal. Bereavement and longing fuel such experiences, and Paul, the persecutor, had neither. And Paul himself, far from dissolving resurrection into a vision, insists on a body — 'what is sown is perishable; what is raised is imperishable' (1 Corinthians 15:42-44).

Could Paul's Damascus experience have been an epileptic seizure?

No — the theory does not hold. It rests on a retrospective diagnosis from a few ancient sentences, which even its recent proponents admit is not agreed among historians, and which a 2021 review in Epilepsy & Behavior judged to rest on weak evidence. Paul's 'thorn in the flesh' is never described, and he sets it against his revelations, calling it 'a messenger of Satan', rather than treating it as their source. The fall and blindness the diagnosis relies on come only from Luke's accounts in Acts, which also report the companions affected; the theory cannot use one without facing the other. Above all, a seizure could explain at most an experience, never its content: a persecutor's conviction that the crucified man he held accursed was raised and Lord, a lifelong mission, and a gospel he then checked with Peter and James.

How can we trust that Paul's message matched what the original disciples claimed?

Paul describes meeting the other witnesses in person. In Galatians 1 he reports staying fifteen days with Peter—using historēsai, a verb of inquiry—and meeting James, both named recipients of appearances in the creed. Fourteen years later he laid his gospel before the 'pillars,' who 'added nothing.' The man who said he had seen the risen Jesus on the Damascus road and the men who claimed to have seen him in Jerusalem reached and confirmed a shared gospel, twice, and did not contradict each other.
