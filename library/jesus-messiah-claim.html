Did Jesus Claim to Be the Messiah? The Historical Case from His Deeds, His Trial and His Cross

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

Above the head of a dying man outside Jerusalem, the charge against him was posted: “THE KING OF THE JEWS” (Mark 15:26).1 All four Gospels report it, each in slightly different words. It is a strange charge to hang over a preacher from Galilee who raised no army and was arrested at night with no force behind him. Somewhere between the towns of Galilee and that cross, a royal claim had been made, or heard, that Rome treated as a capital matter. This essay asks whose claim it was. Did Jesus of Nazareth present himself as Israel’s Messiah, the anointed king the prophets had promised? Or did his followers give him the title after his death and write it back into his life?

Stating the Question Carefully

Three questions are easily blended and need to be kept apart. The first is whether Jesus is the Messiah. That turns on whether God raised him from the dead, and it is argued on its own evidence in our essays on the early creed of 1 Corinthians 15 and the minimal facts. The second is whether Jesus claimed to be God. That is a different and larger claim, taken up in Did Jesus Claim to Be God? and The Titles of Jesus. The third, our subject here, is narrower and strictly historical: in his own ministry, did Jesus present himself as Israel’s Messiah?

The answer this essay defends is yes, with a qualification that matters. Jesus seldom used the title of himself, and he discouraged others from spreading it. He made the claim mainly by what he did: by the Scripture he read as fulfilled in himself, by the shape he gave his movement, by the way he entered Jerusalem and acted in its temple, and by the answer he gave when he was put on trial. Rome then executed him under a royal charge. No one of these strands is decisive by itself, and each has been disputed. The case rests on how they converge.

What “Messiah” Meant

“Messiah” (Hebrew mashiach, Greek Christos, “anointed one”) did not, by itself, mean “divine.” It meant God’s anointed king — the heir of David who would restore Israel.

But Jewish hope in Jesus’ day was not one thing, and not every Jew held it. The Psalms of Solomon, a Jewish collection from the decades after the Roman general Pompey took Jerusalem in 63 BC, pray for God to raise up “their king, the son of David” to cleanse Jerusalem of the nations and gather a holy people (Psalms of Solomon 17). The Rule of the Community from Qumran looks for a prophet and “the Messiahs of Aaron and Israel,” a priestly figure beside a royal one (1QS 9:11). Many writings of the period mention no messiah at all. So there was no single job description waiting for a candidate. Still, where a deliverer was hoped for, the dominant hope was for a triumphant one, and in its most familiar form he was a son of David who would restore Israel and set its enemies right.2 That is the background against which anything Jesus did would have been heard.

Six Strands of Evidence

1. “He Has Anointed Me”: Isaiah 61 and the Reply to John

Luke opens Jesus’ public ministry in the synagogue at Nazareth. He is handed the scroll of Isaiah and reads: “The Spirit of the Lord is on Me, because He has anointed Me to preach good news to the poor.” Then he sits down and says, “Today this Scripture is fulfilled in your hearing” (Luke 4:18–21, reading Isaiah 61:1–2). The verb is the root of the title: he has anointed me. A concession is owed here. The scene in this form is Luke’s alone. Mark and Matthew tell of a rejection at Nazareth without the reading, and Luke has placed the episode at the head of the ministry as a kind of programme. Taken by itself, it could be Luke’s theology rather than Jesus’ own words.3

What keeps the theme from being only Luke’s is a second scene, which Matthew and Luke share. John the Baptist, in prison, sends to ask: “Are You the One who was to come, or should we look for someone else?” Jesus does not answer yes or no. He answers with a list: “The blind receive sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, and the good news is preached to the poor. Blessed is the one who does not fall away on account of Me” (Matthew 11:3–6; Luke 7:20–23). The list is drawn from Isaiah: eyes of the blind opened, the lame leaping (Isaiah 35:5–6), good news to the poor (61:1). Jesus invokes it as his credential. The tradition also has rough edges that a writer polishing a hero would be unlikely to add: the Baptist, whom the Gospels honour, is shown in doubt, and Jesus closes with a warning against taking offence at him.4

That answer could have been heard in a particular way, because at least one Jewish text written before Jesus already made a similar connection. The fragmentary “Messianic Apocalypse” from Qumran (4Q521) opens with heaven and earth listening to “his anointed” and goes on to the deeds of the coming age: sight for the blind, healing for the wounded, life for the dead, good news for the poor. Who performs those deeds is debated. On the majority reading, God himself “makes the dead live,” and specialists disagree over whether he acts through the anointed figure. Either way, the pairing is there before Jesus: an anointed one named beside the healing and good news of Isaiah 61, with the raising of the dead added. In that setting Jesus’ reply to John is not an evasion. It points to the signs of the anointed one’s time and leaves John to draw the conclusion.5

2. The Kingdom and the Twelve

Jesus’ message was the kingdom of God: “The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is near” (Mark 1:15). Announcing God’s reign is not, by itself, claiming to be its king; prophets announced it too. What gives the message a royal shape is the movement Jesus built around it. He chose twelve men from among his followers (Mark 3:13–19), and the number was not accidental: Israel had twelve tribes. E. P. Sanders, no friend of easy harmonization, put the call of the Twelve on his short list of the most secure facts about Jesus.6 A saying preserved by both Matthew and Luke spells out what the number meant: “I bestow on you a kingdom, just as My Father has bestowed one on Me, so that you may eat and drink at My table in My kingdom and sit on thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel” (Luke 22:29–30; compare Matthew 19:28). The common core in both forms is the promise of thrones and of judging the twelve tribes; the language of “My kingdom” and “My table” is Luke’s.

The Twelve are to judge Israel’s tribes. The one who appoints them stands above them. A man who hands out thrones in a restored Israel is not casting himself as one more prophet. Matthew’s form promises “twelve thrones” to the disciples while Judas is still among them, which is not the kind of promise the church would have been likely to invent after the betrayal. The saying does not use the title. It does describe a king’s court.

3. Greater than Solomon, Lord of David

Several sayings set Jesus beside Israel’s royal house. To a generation demanding a sign he answers that “One greater than Solomon is here” (Matthew 12:42, a saying preserved also in Luke 11:31). Solomon was David’s son, the king who built the temple. The Greek phrase is neuter, “something greater,” and some read it of the in-breaking kingdom rather than of Jesus himself; but in context the surpassing reality is embodied in the one who brings it, and Jesus lets the comparison rest on himself.7

In the temple courts, during his last week, Jesus poses a riddle: “How can the scribes say that the Christ is the Son of David?” when “David himself calls Him ‘Lord’” (Mark 12:35–37, citing Psalm 110:1). It is a question that does not deny he is the son of David but presses the hearers to see more than they have seen. Jesus does not name himself in it, but the riddle has a point only if the question of the Messiah is live in that place and that week. Where the riddle leads beyond royal sonship is taken up in The Titles of Jesus.

4. The Final Week: A Donkey and a Temple

Jesus entered Jerusalem on a young donkey he had sent his disciples to fetch, and the crowd shouted, “Blessed is the coming kingdom of our father David!” (Mark 11:1–10). Zechariah had pictured Jerusalem’s king arriving “humble and riding on a donkey” (Zechariah 9:9), and Matthew and John quote him. Our essay on messianic prophecy calls this the clearest example of a stageable prophecy, one the Gospels themselves present Jesus as deliberately arranging. That is exactly why it is weak evidence of fulfilled prediction and strong evidence of a claim. A man who arranges to ride into the city of David on a donkey, days before Passover, in front of pilgrims who knew Zechariah, is not keeping his self-understanding to himself. He is acting it out. How large and how public the scene was is debated — had it been a mass royal procession, one would expect Rome to have acted at once — and the argument needs only that Jesus chose the gesture.8

Shortly afterwards he drove the traders out of the temple courts, quoting Isaiah: “My house will be called a house of prayer for all the nations” (Mark 11:15–17; Isaiah 56:7). Around that act clusters a saying about destroying the temple and raising another. The temple saying is multiply and awkwardly attested. It appears independently in Mark and John, two streams many scholars treat as largely separate. More telling, it surfaces not as a triumphant Christian slogan but as an accusation used against Jesus at his trial (Mark 14:58) and as a taunt at his execution (15:29). E. P. Sanders treated Jesus’ conflict over the temple as among the most secure things we can know about him, and judged a saying about its destruction probably authentic.9

Read against Israel’s Scriptures, the act can carry a royal meaning. In Nathan’s oracle it is David’s heir who builds God’s house: “He will build a house for My Name, and I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever” (2 Samuel 7:13). Zechariah says the same of the coming “Branch”: “He will build the temple of the LORD; He will be clothed in splendor and will sit on His throne and rule” (Zechariah 6:13). At Qumran, the Florilegium (4Q174) already read Nathan’s promise as a promise about a coming figure. Two limits apply. The exact form of the destroy-and-rebuild saying is debated, and Mark puts it on the lips of false witnesses; and Jesus nowhere explains his action by citing 2 Samuel 7. The royal reading is an inference, made by N. T. Wright among others, not a statement of the text. But it is the inference the setting invites, and in Mark it is straight after the temple testimony that the high priest asks whether Jesus is the Christ (14:57–61).

5. “Are You the Christ?”

According to Mark — the earliest Gospel — the high priest asks directly, “Are You the Christ, the Son of the Blessed One?” Jesus answers, “I am, and you will see the Son of Man sitting at the right hand of Power and coming with the clouds of heaven” (Mark 14:61–62). (Matthew’s parallel gives the more oblique “You have said it yourself”; some judge that indirect form more original.) Either way the question is put in messianic terms, and Jesus does not deny it. Before Pilate the same question returns in the form a Roman governor would use: “Are You the King of the Jews?” “You have said so” (Mark 15:2). Luke has the accusers spell out what the title meant to Rome: they charge him with “proclaiming Himself to be Christ, a King” (Luke 23:2).10

A messianic claim does not appear, on its own, to have counted as blasphemy: about a century later, by rabbinic tradition, the leading rabbi Akiba hailed the rebel Simon bar Kosiba as “King Messiah” and drew a colleague’s ridicule, not a charge of blasphemy.11 So the blasphemy verdict in Mark turns on the second half of Jesus’ answer, Daniel’s clouds and Psalm 110’s seat. Why that half was heard as blasphemy belongs to the question of deity and is argued in Did Jesus Claim to Be God? For the question of this essay the first half is enough: on Mark’s account, asked whether he was the Christ, Jesus said, “I am.”

Yet even this scene must be held with an honest caveat: the historicity of Mark’s night trial before the Sanhedrin is itself disputed — Ehrman, among others, regards it as likely a later construction rather than a reported transcript. The sharpest form of that doubt — that no one friendly to Jesus could have known what passed in the chamber — is itself overstated, for a transmission route need not be sympathetic: a follower was in the high priest’s courtyard (Mark 14:54, 66–72); the tradition names a member of the council who, on Luke’s account, had not consented to its verdict (Mark 15:43; Luke 23:50–51); and the movement soon drew in people with access to those circles, the trained Pharisee Paul among them (Acts 22:3; Galatians 1:13–14), and, Acts reports, priests and Pharisees (Acts 6:7; 15:5). None of that makes the scene a verbatim transcript, but it blunts the claim that its words could not possibly have been reported. The argument does not stand or fall with it. If the trial scene is set aside, the weight shifts back onto the acts already surveyed, and onto the charge Rome wrote over the cross.

6. “The King of the Jews”

The historian Martin Hengel, in the standard study of the subject, gathered the ancient sources to show that crucifixion was what Josephus called “the most wretched of deaths,” reserved for slaves, violent criminals, and rebels against Rome, deliberately public and deliberately obscene. The charge posted over Jesus named his offence, and it was a royal one. All four Gospels report it (Mark 15:26; Matthew 27:37; Luke 23:38; John 19:19), and its wording is telling. “King of the Jews” is not how Christians spoke of Jesus; they called him Christ and Lord. In the Gospels the phrase is on the lips of outsiders — the Magi, Pilate, the soldiers, and the titulus itself; the only Jewish leaders who use it are the chief priests of John 19:21, and they quote the inscription in order to object to it. Where Jews speak for themselves they say “King of Israel” (Mark 15:32; John 1:49; 12:13), and the priests at the cross mock him as “the Christ, the King of Israel” (Mark 15:32). It reads like a Roman formula, not a church confession. And Rome had reason to treat such a claim as sedition. After Herod the Great died in 4 BC, Josephus reports a run of men who reached for royal power in Judea and Galilee — Judas son of Hezekias, Simon, the shepherd Athronges — and remarks that whenever a band of rebels found a leader, “he was created a king immediately.”12 Pilate’s charge places Jesus, in Rome’s eyes, in that category, though he had raised no army.

The charge records what Rome concluded, not by itself what Jesus said, and that limit should be kept. What it does make secure is the end of the story: Jesus died as a man Rome treated as a royal pretender. The question is how the title came to be attached to him.

Why the Strands Converge

Each strand has critics, and each, taken alone, allows a lower reading. What is harder to explain away is the pattern: a man who answers John’s question with the anointed one’s deeds, appoints twelve to judge Israel’s tribes, rides into Jerusalem on Zechariah’s pattern, acts with authority over the temple, does not deny the title at his trial, and dies under a royal charge. The strands come from Mark, from the material Matthew and Luke share, and from John, and they include actions as well as sayings. They are not all independent: the entry, the temple action, the trial and the charge belong to one sequence, and a single alternative can account for much of it. But a Jesus who never thought of himself in messianic terms must still explain the reply to John and the thrones of the Twelve, which come from his Galilean ministry, apart from that week. A Jesus who did fits all of them at once.

The Strongest Objections, Stated at Full Strength

“The Messianic Secret”: Wrede’s Challenge

The most influential case against all this was made in 1901 by the German scholar William Wrede in Das Messiasgeheimnis in den Evangelien, translated as The Messianic Secret.13 Wrede noticed how often the Jesus of Mark commands silence: to demons who name him (Mark 3:11–12), to people he has healed (1:43–44; 5:43; 7:36), to the disciples after Peter’s confession (8:29–30), and after the transfiguration, “until the Son of Man had risen from the dead” (9:9). He argued that these commands are not memories but a theological device. The church believed after Easter that Jesus was the Messiah, but it had inherited traditions of a life that had not been messianic; the secrecy motif reconciled the two by explaining why no one had recognised him at the time. Mark 9:9 is the key on this view: the secret lasts until the resurrection, which, Wrede held, is when belief in Jesus’ messiahship began. Wrede changed the study of the Gospels, and versions of his thesis are still held.

The observation is sound; the theory built on it, we will argue, does not hold. First, the commands are not one thing. Many concern healings, not messiahship, and Ulrich Luz distinguished a “miracle secret” from a “messianic secret” in Mark; J. D. G. Dunn, who reports that distinction, argued against Wrede that the secrecy in Mark has a historical rather than a purely theological origin.14 Nor do the healing commands work as concealment: “the more He ordered them, the more widely they proclaimed it” (Mark 7:36). Second, much of Mark’s messianic material is not secret at all. Blind Bartimaeus shouts “Son of David” on the road out of Jericho; the crowd tries to silence him, and Jesus calls him over and heals him (Mark 10:46–52). The entry into Jerusalem and the temple action are public acts, and the answer at the trial is not hushed. A device meant to hide an unmessianic life has left the most messianic scenes in plain view.

Third, the reticence that is real has a better explanation. “Messiah” was a dangerous word. John records (a detail only John has) that after the feeding of the crowd Jesus withdrew, “realizing that they were about to come and make Him king by force” (John 6:15), and the pretenders Josephus describes show what Rome did with kings made by crowds. A man who meant something other than a war on Rome had every reason to avoid the title in public and to redefine it in private, and that is what Mark shows: Peter confesses him as the Christ, and Jesus at once “began to teach them that the Son of Man must suffer many things” (8:29–31). Mark has certainly shaped the secrecy motif, and nothing in this essay rests on any one command being historical. But Wrede’s theory explains the secrecy only by leaving the cross unexplained. A life with nothing messianic about it, neither claim nor acclaim, does not end under the charge “King of the Jews.”

“The Church Made Him Messiah After Easter”

A second objection takes up where Wrede left off. Perhaps Jesus was a prophet of the kingdom and nothing more. His disciples, convinced after his death that God had raised him, read Psalm 110 (“Sit at My right hand”) and Psalm 2 as describing his enthronement, concluded that he must be the Messiah, and read the title back into the story of his life. Some of the earliest Christian formulas can sound like this: God “made” him Lord and Christ (Acts 2:36), and he “was declared with power to be the Son of God by His resurrection from the dead” (Romans 1:4).

The objection runs into something a resurrection would not explain on its own. Suppose the first followers did become convinced that God had raised Jesus. In the categories of the time, that would mark him out as a vindicated martyr; it would not, by itself, show him to be the Messiah. Where Jews hoped for resurrection, they looked for the dead to be raised together at the end of the age (Daniel 12:2), but nothing in our sources treats rising from the dead as the sign of the Messiah. When popular talk held that Jesus was John the Baptist risen from the dead — Herod himself said so (Mark 6:14–16) — no one concluded that the risen John was the Christ; the guesses were John, Elijah, or a prophet, not the Messiah (6:14–15; cf. 8:28). So the title the first Christians gave the crucified Jesus is very hard to derive from Easter alone. The apostles did read the resurrection as God’s confirmation of it — God has “made this Jesus, whom you crucified, both Lord and Christ” (Acts 2:36) — but a confirmation needs something to confirm. Paul’s formula has the same shape: the one “declared with power to be the Son of God by His resurrection” is already “His Son, who was a descendant of David according to the flesh” (Romans 1:3–4). The enthronement psalms could explain how the first Christians spoke of a messiahship they already believed in. They do not explain why they chose those psalms for a crucified man, unless the royal question had been raised before the cross. In Mark it is Jesus himself who twice reaches for Psalm 110 (12:35–37; 14:62). Something like the claim was already attached to him before he died, at least in the charge Rome wrote over his cross, its way of naming a royal pretender: “The King of the Jews” (Mark 15:26).15 Having it, they could have let it go. A martyr, a prophet, a righteous sufferer: any of these would have been easier to preach than a crucified Messiah. They kept the hardest claim, and they did not keep it as they had first held it: the title now belonged to a crucified and cursed man, raised ahead of the last day, and nothing in their tradition had a place for that.

“He Never Plainly Said It”

This objection is right about how often Jesus used the title, and that much should be conceded. Jesus rarely calls himself Messiah in the Gospels. The clearest cases are answers under questioning: “I am” to the high priest in Mark, and the guarded “You have said so” to Pilate. There is a direct self-identification to the Samaritan woman — “I who speak to you am He” (John 4:25–26) — but this essay builds its historical case without leaning on John. The claim, so far as we can recover it, was made mainly in deeds, and through the charge on which he died.

The strongest form of the objection builds on exactly that concession. Paula Fredriksen, in Jesus of Nazareth, King of the Jews, argues that Jesus proclaimed the coming kingdom of God but did not publicly claim the title. On her reconstruction, on his last Passover visit his followers and the Passover crowds hailed him as Messiah, and Pilate executed him to stop the excitement from spreading; the excitement grew from his preaching that the kingdom was near, not, on her account, from a public claim on his part to the throne. The account sets out to explain two things: the royal charge without a royal claim, and a puzzle any account must face, why Rome killed Jesus but did not round up his followers.16

The reply is that the acclamation has to come from somewhere, and in the Gospels it comes from something Jesus did. The crowd’s shout about “the coming kingdom of our father David” answers an entry that, on Mark’s account, Jesus himself arranged on Zechariah’s pattern; the details of that arrangement may owe something to the evangelist’s telling, but the reply to John and the thrones of the Twelve cannot be explained by a crowd in Jerusalem. An account in which others called him Messiah while he did nothing to invite it has to read each of them — the reply to John, the thrones of the Twelve, the entry, the temple action — as a prophet’s sign rather than a king’s, and set aside the answer at the trial. Part of Fredriksen’s case should be granted: Jesus did not campaign for the title, and the crowds’ enthusiasm may well have weighed with Pilate. Her puzzle about the disciples has an answer on our account too: Jesus led no armed rising, so Pilate could kill the claimant and let his followers go. The disagreement is over whether Jesus invited the hope placed on him, and the strands above say he did. That is a judgment of the best explanation, not a proof.

“So Is This an Argument That Jesus Is God?”

No, and it should not be used as one. Christians sometimes speak as if “Messiah” were itself a claim to deity, and that runs two questions together.

Here honesty requires a concession that some apologists resist. “Son of David” carries the same freight as “Messiah”: it names Jesus as the legitimate royal heir, the king of the line of David. None of these titles, taken in isolation, settles the question of divinity. A thoughtful first-century Jew could call a man “Messiah,” “Son of David,” even “Son of God” in the royal sense, while affirming the strictest monotheism.17 We must let the contested claim be labelled as contested: these titles are royal and human before they are anything moreRoyal titles, then filled fullerIs sayingIn their Old Testament background, "Messiah," "Son of David," and (in its first sense) "Son of God" named a human Davidic king — which the New Testament then expands beyond its banks.Not sayingNot adoptionism — not that Jesus was a mere man later promoted to divine sonship. So the conclusion of this essay is a royal one: Jesus presented himself as Israel’s anointed king. That he also claimed more than royalty — to forgive sins as only God forgives, and to sit at God’s right hand — is a separate argument, made in Did Jesus Claim to Be God? and The Titles of Jesus. The church confesses both: the Messiah of Israel is the eternal Son, fully God and fully man, two natures in one person.

A Word on Fairness

One further point belongs here, in the spirit of fairness this site is committed to. The case that Jesus claimed to be Israel’s Messiah must never be turned into a weapon against Judaism — as if the argument were that “real” Jews should therefore have accepted him, or that his rejection by some authorities counts against his own people. The Hebrew Scriptures themselves portray prophets opposed by the establishment, and Isaiah 53 speaks of a servant despised and rejected (Christians read it of Jesus; Jewish tradition has often read it of Israel); division over a prophet is among the most Jewish features of the whole account, not a strike against it. Amy-Jill Levine’s warning is well taken: the recovery of the Jewish Jesus is poisoned the moment it is used to disparage the tradition that formed him.18 The first church was itself entirely Jewish. The argument concerns what Jesus claimed, and it should be made in gratitude, not in triumph.

The Honest State of Scholarship

This is a live question, and the field is divided. Wrede’s view, that the messianic claim belongs to the church rather than to Jesus, still has heirs, and Fredriksen, as we saw, doubts that Jesus made the claim while agreeing that he died under it. But the claim is not defended only by believers. Bart Ehrman, the best known of skeptical New Testament scholars, writes of “my claim that Jesus himself told the disciples that he thought he was the messiah” — on his reconstruction, a king in the kingdom God was about to bring, not a crucified one.19 Two limits apply to every side. All six strands reach us through the Gospels, so the case is a historian’s judgment of probability, not a demonstration. And those who agree that Jesus claimed the title still disagree about what he meant by it. The distinction worth keeping is between a verdict and a consensus: this essay has given its reasons for its verdict, and it does not pretend the field has settled the question.

Conclusion: What This Does and Does Not Establish

So the answer to the question in the title is yes. Jesus presented himself as Israel’s Messiah, more in deed than in word, and he died for it. He answered John with the signs Isaiah tied to the anointed one, built his movement around a restored Israel, entered Jerusalem in a way that recalled Zechariah’s king, acted with authority over the temple, on Mark’s account accepted the title when the high priest put it to him, and was crucified as “the King of the Jews.” The secrecy in Mark does not overturn that, and Easter alone is very hard to make its source, because in the categories of the time a resurrection, by itself, would mark a vindicated martyr, not a Messiah.

What this does not establish is just as important. It shows that Jesus made a claim; it does not show that the claim was true. Many men in that century were hailed as kings, and their causes died with them. Whether Jesus’ claim was true turns on what happened after the cross, and that case is made on its own evidence in our essays on the early creed and the minimal facts. Jewish tradition has also weighed the claim by whether the work long expected of the Messiah — the ingathering of the exiles, a rebuilt temple, peace among the nations — was accomplished; Christians answer that the Messiah’s work is begun and not yet finished, a question taken up in our essay on messianic prophecy. We hold that the claim was true: that God raised the crucified Jesus and so vindicated the one Rome had executed as a pretender, and that the Messiah of Israel is the eternal Son, fully God and fully man, the Lord of all.

Frequently asked questions

Did Jesus ever say 'I am the Messiah'?

Rarely, and mostly when he was asked. In Mark, when the high priest asks, "Are You the Christ?", Jesus answers, "I am" (Mark 14:61–62), though Matthew's version is more guarded and the historicity of the trial scene is disputed. He did not campaign for the title. He made the claim mainly by what he did: answering John the Baptist with the signs Isaiah tied to the anointed one, choosing twelve to judge Israel's tribes, and riding into Jerusalem on Zechariah's pattern. Rome then crucified him as "The King of the Jews."

If Jesus claimed to be Messiah, why the 'messianic secret'?

Because the title was dangerous and easily misunderstood, not because he made no claim. A crowd once tried to make him king by force (John 6:15), and Rome executed would-be kings. Many of the silence commands in Mark concern healings, not messiahship, and much of Mark's messianic material is public: Bartimaeus's "Son of David," the entry into Jerusalem, the answer at the trial. Wrede's theory that the secret hides an unmessianic life struggles to explain why that life ended under a royal charge; later critics who explain the charge by the crowd's acclaim rather than Jesus' own claim are answered in the essay.

Isn't 'Messiah' the same as claiming to be God?

No. "Messiah" means "anointed one," God's anointed king of David's line, and a first-century Jew could use it of a man while holding the strictest monotheism. So the case that Jesus claimed to be Messiah is a royal claim; it does not by itself settle the question of deity. That Jesus also claimed more than royalty is a separate question, argued in our essays on Jesus' self-claims and his titles; the church confesses that the Messiah is also the eternal Son, fully God and fully man.
