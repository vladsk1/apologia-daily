Deep Dive Essay Pro

Did the New Testament Quote the Old Testament Out of Context?

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

The Charge, at Full Strength

"Christians just read Jesus back into the Old Testament after the fact." It is one of the most common objections a believer meets, and it is worth stating at full strength. On this telling, the New Testament writers comb the Hebrew Scriptures for anything that can be made to sound like Jesus, lift verses out of the settings that gave them meaning, override what they plainly meant to the people who first wrote and treasured them, and then dress the result up as "prophecy" — creative midrash passing itself off as evidence. If that is what the apostles were doing, the whole structure of "fulfillment" is a conjuring trick, and a thoughtful reader is right to walk away from it.

That is a serious objection, and it calls for a serious answer — one about the method itself, not merely about this or that disputed verse. Typology — reading an earlier person, event, or institution as a God-shaped pattern that both corresponds to, and is overtaken by, its completion in Christ — is one of the ways the New Testament reads the Old, and it is the one critics find easiest to caricature.1 Our companion essays take up the forward-looking predictions and the full case for typology in their own right; the question here is narrower, and in a way prior: when the New Testament reaches for the Old, is it doing something describable and disciplined, or is it simply proof-texting? The short answer is that its use of the Old is graded, self-aware, and open to inspection — and that the forward-leaning reading it performs was not imposed from outside, because the Hebrew Scriptures already read themselves that way.

A Test Case: Did Matthew Take Hosea Out of Context?

Start where the charge bites hardest — at the much-contested quotation in the second chapter of Matthew. Matthew says that Joseph took the child to Egypt and "remained there until the death of Herod. This was to fulfill what the Lord had spoken by the prophet, 'Out of Egypt I called my son'" (Matthew 2:15). Now open Hosea and read the whole verse: "When Israel was a child, I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son" (Hosea 11:1). In Hosea the verse is plainly about the past — Israel's exodus — and contains no future-tense verb at all. The "son" is the nation, not an individual, and the very next verse turns to that son's unfaithfulness: "they kept sacrificing to the Baals" (11:2). The critic can press a third point: Matthew attaches the quotation to the family's flight into Egypt, while their return out of Egypt comes only later (2:19–21). Past history read as prediction, a nation turned into one man, and a verse placed at the wrong moment — that is the charge at full strength, and it has been made in print. In 1961 S. Vernon McCasland titled his Journal of Biblical Literature article, bluntly, "Matthew Twists the Scriptures," arguing that Matthew had mistaken Hosea's memory of the exodus for a prophecy.2 If that is right, it is no small problem for this essay: the first Gospel would be quote-mining, and "typology" would be a respectable name for it.

Yet Matthew is not mistaking Hosea for a forecast, because Matthew's "fulfill" is wider than prediction. The New Testament writers use the word "fulfill" (Greek plēroō) in more than one sense, and not always to mean "a prediction came true." Often it means something closer to "filled full": an earlier pattern, person, or institution reaches its intended completion in Christ. Matthew's own Gospel shows the range. Jesus says he came not to abolish "the Law or the Prophets" but "to fulfill them" (Matthew 5:17) — and commandments are not predictions. Matthew can even say that "all the Prophets and the Law prophesied until John" (11:13), the Law included. So when Matthew writes "fulfill" over a verse about the past, he is not claiming Hosea predicted it. He is drawing an analogy: Jesus relives and succeeds in Israel's story. As J. R. Daniel Kirk puts it, Jesus came not simply "to embody principles or even fulfil prophetic predictions, but to take the story of Israel to himself."3

So the verdict is plain: the objection does not hold. Matthew is not quote-mining Hosea, and he has not mistaken a memory for a forecast. He quotes a verse about God's son Israel because his whole claim is that Jesus sums up Israel's story in himself and brings it to its goal — out of Egypt, through the waters, into the wilderness, and faithful where Israel faltered. The objection is right about Hosea's grammar; it is wrong about what Matthew is claiming. And what Matthew is claiming is the very typology this essay has described: a real history, a genuine correspondence, and an escalation, because this Son does what the first son did not. The honest limit is clear: this shows that Matthew reads in context and reads coherently; it does not, by itself, prove to a skeptic that Jesus is the one in whom Israel's story reaches its goal. That case begins, as it always does here, with the resurrection.

Four Ways the New Testament Cites the Old — and Only One Is Typology

Step back from Hosea for a moment, because one of the most useful replies to the charge that the New Testament simply quote-mines is to notice that "quoting the Old Testament" is not one move but several, and Matthew keeps them distinct. Matthew's second chapter — the very stretch where the charge is pressed hardest — is the clearest classroom, because within a few verses it reaches for the Hebrew Scriptures in four different ways, and only one of them is typology.

First, direct prediction. When Herod asks where the Messiah is to be born, the chief priests answer from Micah 5:2: "in Bethlehem of Judea" (Matthew 2:5–6). That is a forward-looking forecast of a place, checked against an event — prophecy in the strict sense, and the business of our companion essay on messianic prophecy, not of this one. Second, typology. "Out of Egypt I called my son" (Hosea 11:1, at Matthew 2:15) is the case we have just examined: not a prediction but a pattern — Israel's story relived and brought to its goal in the one faithful Son. Third, application. Over the murder of the infants Matthew sets Jeremiah's lament for exiled Israel: "A voice was heard in Ramah … Rachel weeping for her children" (Jeremiah 31:15, at Matthew 2:17–18). Here Matthew does not even use his usual purpose-formula "so that it might be fulfilled"; he writes "then was fulfilled" — a shift many read as marking the looser bond of an analogy applied — a fresh grief that answers to an old one, in a chapter (Jeremiah 31) whose very next breath is a promise of return and hope.4 Fourth, summary. That Jesus "would be called a Nazarene" (Matthew 2:23) is credited not to one prophet but to "the prophets," plural, and matches no single verse — Matthew is gathering up a strand of prophetic expectation rather than citing a text, a practice with Old Testament precedent of its own (Ezra 9:10–12 folds several commands together under "your servants the prophets").

A Contextual, Not an Atomistic, Method

The payoff is simple, and it weakens the objection rather than merely parrying it. A writer out only to raid the Scriptures for proof-texts would not trouble to vary his method, still less to flag what many take to be the looser links with a looser verb. Matthew's citations are sorted, graded, and self-aware — which is the opposite of arbitrariness. And that care has a historical setting. David Instone-Brewer's study of the earliest datable Jewish scribal exegesis argues that its interpreters, before AD 70, characteristically assumed Scripture was coherent and was to be read with its context, not atomised into isolated slogans; the free, proof-texting manner often imagined for this period is in large part a later development.5 Klyne Snodgrass, surveying the New Testament's own practice, reaches a kindred verdict: its authors attend to the context of what they cite far more often than the caricature allows. The point should not be overstated — some New Testament citations are highly compressed, and the question is a live one among specialists — but the headline holds: Matthew's method is describable and open to inspection, not a conjuror's trick. Typology, on this map, is one clearly bounded category among several, with its own warrant and its own honest limits — exactly as this essay has treated it, and no wider.

But Couldn’t You Find Patterns Anywhere?

Behind every particular quarrel about Hosea or Micah lies a deeper worry, and it is the one that really does the work. The human mind is a pattern-finding machine — it sees faces in clouds and animals in the stars — so give a devout reader two texts and a motive, the objection runs, and “echoes” will appear on demand. A method that can find Jesus anywhere has found him nowhere in particular. This is the disease the Jewish scholar Samuel Sandmel diagnosed, in his 1961 presidential address to the Society of Biblical Literature, parallelomania: “that extravagance among scholars which first overdoes the supposed similarity in passages and then proceeds to describe source and derivation as if implying literary connection flowing in an inevitable or predetermined direction.” The warning is exactly right, and Christians should own it rather than flinch from it. The honest reply is not to deny that patterns can be over-read — they can, and have been — but to show that hearing a real scriptural echo is a disciplined act with checks that an interpreter can be held to in advance, not a free association anyone can perform with any text.

The most influential attempt to specify those checks is Richard B. Hays’s. In Echoes of Scripture in the Letters of Paul (1989) he set out seven tests for deciding whether a proposed allusion is actually present in the text, and they read less like a licence to find echoes than like a standard a critic could use to rule them out.6 Availability: was the source text actually within reach of the writer and his first readers? Volume: how loud is the echo — a shared distinctive phrase, or merely a common idea anyone might hit on? Recurrence: does the same author return to the same passage elsewhere, so that the link is characteristic rather than a one-off? Thematic coherence: does the proposed echo actually fit the argument the author is making, or has it been dragged in? Historical plausibility: could the author have intended the effect being claimed, and could his readers have caught it? History of interpretation: have other readers down the centuries heard the same echo, or is it a novelty visible only to its proposer? Satisfaction: does the reading, taken as a whole, make the passage make more sense? The point of the list is that each test is a way of being told no. A “parallel” that was not available, is barely audible, never recurs, cuts against the argument, and no one before ever noticed is not a parallel; it is the interpreter’s own face in the cloud. The controls do real work precisely because many proposed echoes fail some of them.

Two honest notes keep this from proving too much. Hays himself was the first to grant that these are guides, not an exact science; applied to a given verse they often leave a residue of genuine uncertainty, and careful scholars still disagree about particular cases. That is a feature, not a defect — a method that returned a clean verdict on every text would be the suspicious one. And the reading these tests discipline is, by Hays’s own account, a retrospective one. His later studies of the Gospels describe the evangelists as reading the Scriptures “backwards” — from the end of the story to its beginning, in the light of the resurrection — so that correspondences become visible that no one could have read off the earlier texts in advance as bare predictions.7 This is a description of what the New Testament writers did, not a proof handed to an outsider; Hays is careful to distinguish figural reading from the claim that the Old Testament is a coded set of advance predictions. Which marks the honest limit once more: the controls show that the apostles’ reading is disciplined and open to inspection, not that a skeptic is thereby obliged to accept its conclusion. That, as ever here, turns on the resurrection.

Rules, Not Whims: The Method in Its Jewish World

It is also worth noticing that the apostles did not invent a strange new way of handling Scripture. They used the recognized interpretive techniques of their own Jewish world — techniques with names, rules, and a long pedigree — which is one more reason their practice is describable rather than arbitrary. Jewish tradition preserves a list of seven middot (“measures,” or rules of interpretation) associated with the great first-century-BC teacher Hillel, the earliest such list we have.8 Two of them turn up constantly in the New Testament. The first is qal wa-homer (“light and heavy”), an argument from the lesser case to the greater: if something holds in a smaller matter, how much more in a weightier one. It is the backbone of Paul’s reasoning in Romans 5, where the refrain is “much more”: “since, therefore, we have now been justified by his blood, much more shall we be saved by him from the wrath of God” (5:9); “if while we were enemies we were reconciled … much more … shall we be saved by his life” (5:10); “much more have the grace of God and the free gift … abounded for many” (5:15). Jesus himself reasons this way — if God clothes the grass, “how much more will he clothe you” (Luke 12:28). The second is gezerah shawah (“equal ordinance”), linking two passages that share a key word so that one illuminates the other — the move behind, for instance, the way Hebrews draws Psalm 2 and Psalm 110 together around the single thread of divine sonship and priesthood. These are not tricks; they are the ordinary scholarly tools of the period, and their presence shows the apostles arguing by shared rules their contemporaries would recognize and could test.

A sharper parallel, and one that needs careful handling, comes from the community that left us the Dead Sea Scrolls. Among them is a running commentary on the prophet Habakkuk (the Habakkuk Pesher, 1QpHab), in which the interpreters read the prophet’s oracles as finding their fulfillment in persons and events of their own time — the founding of their community, its leaders, its enemies. Here is a Jewish group, roughly contemporary with the New Testament, convinced that ancient prophecy was reaching its appointed goal in the present. That much the New Testament writers share with them, and it is worth saying plainly, because it shows that reading prophecy as presently fulfilled was a live Jewish instinct, not a Christian oddity. But the differences are just as important, and overstating the similarity would be a mistake.9 The Qumran method, called pesher (“interpretation”), treated the prophet’s words as a sealed mystery (a raz) whose real meaning was hidden even from the prophet himself and unlocked only by the special inspiration granted to the community’s own Teacher — an esoteric key for insiders, decoding the text onto the group’s private history. The New Testament’s appeal is of a different order. Its claims are anchored not to a hidden decoding but to public, datable events — above all a crucifixion under Pontius Pilate that non-Christian writers also report — and to a person its witnesses said was raised and seen; and they were argued out in the open, in synagogue debates where opponents could and did push back. The apostles used the interpretive furniture of their world; what was new was not a secret technique but the claim that the story had reached its climax in events anyone could investigate.

A Second Test Case: Peter Argues from Psalm 16

If the method is really describable, it should be consistent across different authors, and it should be possible to watch one of them show his working. Peter does exactly that in the first Christian sermon. Preaching at Pentecost, he quotes David: “For you will not abandon my soul to Hades, or let your Holy One see corruption” (Psalm 16:10, at Acts 2:27). Now a lazy proof-texter would simply assert that the psalm is about Jesus and move on. Peter does the opposite: he pauses to argue the point, and the argument is one a hostile listener could check on the spot. “Brothers,” he says, “I may say to you with confidence about the patriarch David that he both died and was buried, and his tomb is with us to this day” (Acts 2:29). The logic is exposed for inspection: David wrote “you will not let your Holy One see corruption,” yet David plainly did see corruption — his tomb was a known Jerusalem landmark — so, on Peter’s reading, the psalm’s language reaches past its first singer to someone whose flesh did not decay.10 Peter even grounds the move in the psalm’s own context, noting that David was “a prophet” who “knew that God had sworn with an oath to him” to set a descendant on his throne (2:30–31). Paul runs the identical argument from the same verse in a different city (Acts 13:35–37), which is itself a mark of method rather than improvisation: two apostles, independently, make the same inspectable case.

Whatever one finally concludes about the argument, notice its shape. It is not an assertion but a chain of reasoning, laid out so that it can be weighed, resting on a public fact (the location of David’s tomb) and on the plain sense of the psalm’s own words. This is the opposite of pulling a verse out of the air. And the honest limit holds here as everywhere in this essay: Peter’s argument shows that the apostolic use of the Old Testament is reasoned and open to challenge, not that a skeptic must grant its conclusion. The conclusion — that the psalm speaks of one whom God raised — stands or falls with whether God in fact raised him, which is a question about an event, not about a method of reading.

The same argued quality marks a very different author. The letter to the Hebrews builds a sustained case on Psalm 110:4 — “The LORD has sworn and will not change his mind, ‘You are a priest forever after the order of Melchizedek’” — and it does so by reasoning from the wording. The writer presses the word “forever” (a priesthood that does not end in death), the oath that introduces it (a divine commitment that “will not change”), and the pointed choice of Melchizedek rather than Levi, drawing out the implication that a priesthood older than, and independent of, the Levitical line is being promised (Hebrews 7:11–25). One may or may not be persuaded by the chain; the point for this essay is the same as with Peter. This is exegetical argument — claims advanced from the actual words of the text, set out to be examined — not a verse plucked and asserted. Across authors as different as the fisherman Peter and the cultured writer of Hebrews, the method keeps one consistent shape: show your working.

The Harder Cases, Honestly

A case made only from its easy examples is not worth much, so three genuine difficulties belong on the table. Facing them squarely is what separates an argument from an advertisement.

Why the New Testament often quotes the Greek

The first difficulty is textual. The New Testament writers frequently quote the Old Testament in the form it took in the Septuagint — the Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures made by Jews from the third century BC onward — and the Greek sometimes differs from the Hebrew text we read today. To a modern eye this can look like carelessness, or worse, like quoting a mistranslation to score a point. It is neither. For the Greek-speaking Jews and God-fearers of the synagogues where the gospel was first argued, the Septuagint simply was their Bible, read and revered as Scripture; quoting it was the natural thing to do, exactly as an English preacher today quotes an English translation.11 A clean example is Hebrews 10:5, which puts on Christ’s lips the words of Psalm 40: “Sacrifices and offerings you have not desired, but a body have you prepared for me.” The Hebrew of Psalm 40:6 reads, more literally, “ears you have dug for me” (the ESV renders it “you have given me an open ear”); the Septuagint has “a body you have prepared for me,” and it is the Greek that Hebrews follows. One need not pretend the difference is trivial: it is a real divergence, and scholars debate how the Greek reading arose and how much weight the argument of Hebrews places on it. The fair conclusion is a measured one. Using the translation your audience actually reads is ordinary and reasonable, not deceptive; and some specific cases where the Greek and Hebrew diverge remain genuinely contested, which honesty requires naming rather than smoothing over.

A “fuller sense” — and a question Christians answer differently

The second difficulty is harder, and it is as much theological as textual. Grant that Hosea 11:1 or Psalm 16 meant something definite in its own setting — how can a later writer find in it a meaning its original human author need not have had in view, without that being a sleight of hand? One long-standing proposal is the idea of a sensus plenior, a “fuller sense”: a deeper meaning intended by God, the ultimate author of Scripture, that completes rather than cancels the sense the human author wrote — the way a sentence early in a novel can carry a weight the reader feels only on a second reading, once the ending is known.12 On this view the exodus really rescued Israel, and really taught Israel about redemption, long before any Christian read it; the fuller sense does not erase that but builds on it. It is important to be candid that this is one proposed account of how the New Testament reads the Old, and that thoughtful Christians disagree about it — over whether a text can carry a meaning beyond its author’s intention at all, over how far such a sense may be pressed, and over who is authorized to discern it. Those disagreements run along lines this essay does not presume to settle; our concern is only the narrower, shared point that the original meaning is honored, not voided, and that reading a text in the light of how its story ends is a recognizable literary act rather than a conjuror’s trick. The Hebrew Scriptures are not discarded on this reading; they are read as reaching their appointed climax.

The genuinely difficult minority

The third difficulty is the simplest to state and the most important to concede: not every New Testament citation is easy to account for, and a minority are compressed, allusive, or combine several texts in ways that remain debated among scholars who take the New Testament with complete seriousness. Matthew’s “he would be called a Nazarene” (2:23), credited to “the prophets” but matching no single verse, is one; his attribution of a Zechariah passage to Jeremiah (27:9) is another long-noted puzzle. Admitting this does not weaken the case; it strengthens it. A method is shown to be disciplined precisely by where its practitioners draw the line between the clear and the contested — and an argument that pretended every citation was equally transparent would have forfeited the right to be believed about the clear ones. The caricature says the apostles ripped verses out of context at will. The reality is a graded practice with a wide band of straightforward cases, a careful middle, and a candidly acknowledged hard edge.

A Century of Study

None of this is a recent improvisation of Christian apologists; it is the subject of a mature scholarly literature. The modern discussion is often dated to 1952, when the Cambridge scholar C. H. Dodd published According to the Scriptures, arguing that the New Testament writers did not grab isolated proof-texts at random but drew on a recognized set of larger Old Testament passages, citing a single verse as a pointer to the whole context around it.13 If Dodd is even broadly right, the “they lift single verses out of context” charge has things backwards: the brief quotation is a signpost to a passage the writer expects his readers to think through entirely. A decade later Barnabas Lindars, in New Testament Apologetic (1961), traced how the earliest believers actually deployed these texts in argument and worship, and how their use developed; his work, too, treats the practice as a describable historical phenomenon rather than a bag of tricks. In the decades since, the field has only deepened, culminating in reference works such as the Commentary on the New Testament Use of the Old Testament edited by G. K. Beale and D. A. Carson, which walks through essentially every New Testament citation of the Old, verse by verse, weighing the textual form, the original context, and the use made of it. Scholars within that literature disagree about plenty — how far typology extends, how to read this or that compressed citation — but the disagreement is itself the point. A practice that sustains this kind of fine-grained analysis — where the criteria actually divide the clear citations from the contested ones — is not plausibly arbitrary proof-texting.

Dodd’s whole-context thesis deserves a concrete illustration, because it inverts the objection so neatly. Consider the cry from the cross. When Jesus prays the opening words of Psalm 22, “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” (Matthew 27:46), the obvious reading is that he is isolating one line of desolation. But Psalm 22 does not stay in the dark: before it ends it turns from dereliction to rescue and to public praise — “I will tell of your name to my brothers … all the ends of the earth shall remember and turn to the LORD” (Psalm 22:22–27). On Dodd’s reading the quotation of the first line is a way of gesturing at the whole poem, its ending included — the signpost, not the destination. Whether or not one accepts that this is what the Gospel intends, it shows the kind of thing the charge ignores: a brief citation can be a doorway into an entire passage rather than a slogan torn from it. The accusation “you ripped a verse out of its context” assumes the writer wanted less of the context; Dodd’s evidence is that, again and again, he wanted more of it.

The Old Testament Already Reads Itself Forward

A further reply meets the charge that forward-looking reading was imposed on these texts only after the fact: the Hebrew Scriptures already read themselves this way, long before any Christian. When Jacob blesses Judah — "the scepter shall not depart from Judah... until tribute comes to him" (Genesis 49:10) — Ezekiel is widely read as taking up the same unfulfilled expectation against a toppled crown: "until he comes, the one to whom judgment belongs, and I will give it to him" (Ezekiel 21:27). And the Pentateuch can be read as closing with a glance past its own greatest prophet: Moses had promised "a prophet like me" (Deuteronomy 18:15), yet the book's final lines record, after his death, that "there has not arisen a prophet since in Israel like Moses" (Deuteronomy 34:10) — the promise left open, its fulfillment still outstanding. This is the phenomenon biblical scholars call inner-biblical exegesis, documented by scholars such as Michael Fishbane (Biblical Interpretation in Ancient Israel): later writers within the Hebrew Bible reading earlier promises as still reaching forward. One need not settle here what each text finally refers to; the narrower point is a recognized feature of the texts themselves — the forward-leaning, "this is not yet fulfilled" habit of reading is native to the Hebrew Scriptures themselves, not a lens Christians fitted over them from outside.

What This Shows — and What It Does Not

What is not seriously disputed is that the New Testament authors themselves read this way, deliberately and pervasively. The question is how far to follow them, not whether they did it.

So the method objection fails. "Christians just rip verses out of context and call it prophecy" is a charge that cannot survive a close look at what the New Testament actually does. Its use of the Old is sorted into distinct kinds — direct prediction, typology, applied analogy, summary — and flagged as such, sometimes with different language for the looser links; it is argued in a way that can be inspected and weighed; and the forward-leaning reading at its heart was not smuggled in from outside, because Israel's own Scriptures had already learned to read their promises as still reaching ahead.

Two honest limits keep the verdict from overreaching. This does not show that every citation is a strict prediction fulfilled to the letter; it shows something more modest and more interesting — that the New Testament writers themselves did not treat them all that way, and neither should we. And showing that Matthew reads in context is not yet showing that Jesus is the one in whom Israel's story reaches its goal; that case rests, as it always does here, on the resurrection. What this essay establishes is narrower, and still substantial: the New Testament's reading of the Old is describable, graded, and self-aware — not a conjuror's trick.

Frequently asked questions

Didn't Matthew take "Out of Egypt I called my son" (Hosea 11:1) out of context?

No. Hosea 11:1 is about Israel's exodus, and Matthew does not claim Hosea predicted Jesus. In Matthew, "fulfill" (plēroō) can mean "fill full," not only "a prediction came true." Hosea's "son" is Israel, God's firstborn (Exodus 4:22), and Matthew tells Jesus' early life as Israel's story walked again: out of Egypt, to the waters of the Jordan, forty days in the wilderness answered from Deuteronomy, and faithful where Israel faltered. Hosea 11 itself closes with a promised return from Egypt (11:10–11), and Israel's own prophets read the first exodus as the pattern of a greater one. Matthew needs the verse's context, not less of it: the quotation only works because its son is Israel. That is typology, not quote-mining.

Aren't you just reading Jesus into everything? Couldn't you find patterns anywhere?

This is the strongest objection, and it has a name: parallelomania, put into circulation by the Jewish scholar Samuel Sandmel in 1961 (a term he said he had not coined but found in an earlier work). We grant that patterns can be over-read, and deny the inference. A method that can find Jesus everywhere has found him nowhere in particular. That is exactly why responsible typology uses controls: the strongest types are ones the New Testament authors themselves draw (Adam, the Passover lamb, Melchizedek, the tabernacle); a genuine type shows correspondence plus escalation, not mere resemblance; and it belongs to the one unfolding story of redemption. We flag which readings are apostolic and which are later devotion, and lean chiefly on the former.
