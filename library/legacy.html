Deep Dive Essay Pro

Did Christianity Change the World?

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

You almost certainly believe that every human being is of equal worth. That the weak deserve protection from the strong. That compassion is a virtue and cruelty a vice. That a person's value does not depend on their usefulness, their birth, or their capacity.

You probably think these are obvious. They are not. For most of recorded history almost nobody believed them, and the societies that produced Western philosophy did not believe them. This essay is about where they came from—and, just as importantly, about what that question can and cannot settle.

The Question, Stated Carefully

This is the section that governs everything else, and skipping it is how this argument goes wrong.

First: nothing here is evidence that Christianity is true. A belief system can be enormously beneficial and entirely false, and a religion's social record is not a truth-claim about God. Anyone who argues “look what Christianity did for the world, therefore it is true” has committed a plain error, and a thoughtful sceptic will name it immediately. The case for the faith's truth rests on the historical and philosophical arguments made elsewhere in this library; it does not rest here, and it must not be smuggled in.

Christianity's claim on anyone is that it is true, not that it is useful. If it were false, its social benefits would be a reason to be grateful for a mistake—not a reason to believe it.

Second: what is being claimed is genealogical. The defensible thesis is not that Christianity made the world nicer. It is that a specific set of moral convictions now treated as self-evident—universal human dignity above all—are not self-evident, have a traceable history, and that Christianity is a major part of that history. This is a claim about where a standard came from, not about how well anyone has lived up to it.

Third: historical causation is contested, and the honest form of the claim admits it. Large civilisational changes have many causes, and disentangling them is exactly what historians argue about. The most that should be said is what this library already says of a parallel case in the history of science: this is a strong causal claim that remains genuinely debated among historians, even as the underlying facts are not.

Holland's Thesis

The most discussed recent statement of the case is Tom Holland's Dominion (2019). Holland is a historian of classical antiquity who wrote the book as a historian rather than as an advocate; in it he describes having lost the Christian faith of his childhood, and describes the discovery that prompted the project.1

He had spent his career loving the ancient world. And the more closely he studied Greece and Rome, the more he realised that he did not share their moral universe at all. The Spartans practised infanticide as policy. Roman power rested on slavery so ordinary that no significant voice questioned it. Crucifixion was designed to strip a human being of dignity in public. Caesar's slaughter of a million Gauls was a boast. There was nothing in the classical world that corresponded to the conviction that the weak have a claim on the strong simply by being human—and Holland found that this conviction, which he held instinctively, was not something he had got from the ancients he admired.

His conclusion is that it came from Christianity, and specifically from the most unlikely element in it: a God executed by torture as a criminal slave. That image inverted the ancient assumption that the gods vindicate the powerful. Holland's careful formulation is worth keeping precise: human rights and equality are not facts of nature waiting to be noticed, but a Christian inheritance—something the West received from a particular history rather than deduced from reason alone.2

Glen Scrivener makes a similar case for a general readership in The Air We Breathe (2022), whose title carries the point: the values feel like atmosphere because we have never been without them, and things we breathe are hard to see.3

What Changed, Concretely

The general thesis is only as good as the specifics, so here are four, stated at the strength the evidence supports and no higher.

The image of God, and the crucified God

Two convictions do most of the work. The first is that every human being bears the image of God (Genesis 1:27), which grounds worth in something no one can lose—not in strength, status, intellect, or usefulness. The second is that God himself was executed by the method Rome reserved for slaves and rebels. Paul knew exactly how absurd this sounded to his contemporaries: he calls the message of a crucified Messiah a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Greeks (1 Corinthians 1:23). A civilisation that came to venerate a torture victim as the image of the divine had, in principle, lost the ability to say that the humiliated are contemptible.

Care of the abandoned and the sick

Early Christian communities refused the exposure of unwanted infants and organised care for the sick and the poor—including, as the emperor Julian complained while trying to compete with them, poor who were not their own. That material is set out in the companion essay on the growth of the early church. The institutional legacy is real but should be stated precisely: what Christian foundations pioneered was not medicine and not hospitals as such—the ancient world had healing shrines and military infirmaries—but charitable institutions for the sick poor, of which Basil of Caesarea's complex outside the city, established around 370, is the standard early example.4

Abolition

Slavery was a human universal, defended by Aristotle as natural and practised on every continent. Its abolition was not. The organised British campaign that began in 1787 was driven substantially by Christian conviction—Quaker networks, the Clapham evangelicals, Thomas Clarkson's evidence-gathering, William Wilberforce's parliamentary persistence—alongside the testimony of formerly enslaved Africans such as Olaudah Equiano, whose own narrative frames his case in Christian terms.5

But the honest sentence is the next one. The most sophisticated biblical defences of slavery were also Christian, produced in quantity by clergy and theologians who read the same texts and found slavery sanctioned in them. The American conflict over slavery was in significant part an argument between Christians using scripture against each other, and the pro-slavery side had the easier proof-texts.6 Abolition was a Christian achievement that had to be won against Christians.

The self-critical reflex

That last observation generalises into the most interesting feature of the tradition. Christianity supplies not only convictions but a mechanism for turning them against itself: a standard external to any given community's practice, before which that community can be arraigned. The reformers of the church have almost always argued not that Christianity should be abandoned but that it should be obeyed.

The Ledger Against

An essay that stopped there would be advertising, not history. The record includes the following, and none of it is incidental.

The Crusades, and the massacres that accompanied them. The judicial machinery of the inquisitions, and the execution of heretics by burning. The witch trials of the early modern period, prosecuted with enthusiasm in Protestant and Catholic territories alike. Forced conversions, and the destruction of indigenous religion and culture across the Americas, Africa, Asia, and the Pacific under the cover of mission. Centuries of Christian anti-Judaism—ghettoes, expulsions, blood libels, pogroms—which did not by itself produce the Shoah but supplied a great deal of the soil it grew in. The churches' extensive complicity in the Atlantic slave trade and in colonial expropriation, and the theological defence of racial segregation and of apartheid. The residential and industrial schools. And, in living memory, the sexual abuse of children by clergy and its concealment by institutions, across more than one tradition.7

Three things must be said about that list, and the order matters.

It is not a caricature and it is not exhaustive. Each item is a settled matter of historical record, and the argument of this essay has to survive them being true.

It cuts across every tradition. Catholics, Orthodox, and Protestants are all implicated, in different proportions in different centuries, and this essay does not attempt to distribute the blame among them—partly because that would require adjudicating the disputes that divide them, and partly because the pattern is precisely that no branch was exempt.

And the genealogical argument must never be used to soften it. There is a move available here that would be intellectually respectable and morally disastrous: to observe that the standard by which we condemn these things is itself substantially a Christian inheritance, and to let that observation function as mitigation. It is not mitigation. That the church supplied the ruler does not shorten what the ruler measures. Anyone who reaches for Holland's thesis as a way of making the Crusades or the abuse crisis feel smaller has misunderstood it and should be told so.

What the observation does establish is narrower and still worth something: the critique is internal. When the church is arraigned for cruelty, hypocrisy, and the abuse of the weak, it is being arraigned by its own book. The prophets, and Jesus, reserved their harshest language for religious authorities who exploited the people they were meant to protect.

The Strongest Objections

Correlation, not causation

The most serious objection is methodological. Europe changed for many reasons over the centuries in question—the recovery of classical texts, printing, commerce and the growth of a middle class, the plague's disruption of the labour market, the scientific revolution, Atlantic contact, industrialisation. Christianity was ambient throughout, which means it is present in every explanation and decisive in none that can be isolated. A variable that never varies cannot be shown to be the cause.

This objection is strong and cannot be fully answered. The most that can be said in reply is that the thesis does not rest on statistical isolation but on the historical genealogy of specific ideas—that one can trace the language of universal human dignity through particular texts, controversies, and campaigns, and that the trail runs through Christian sources. That is the ordinary method of intellectual history, and it is weaker than experiment. It should be offered as such.

The counter-genealogy: these are Enlightenment values won against the church

The standard secular account credits religious toleration, human rights, and the abolition of judicial torture to Enlightenment thinkers who fought clerical power to establish them—and it can point to churches on the wrong side of most of those fights.

Much of that is simply true and should be granted without wriggling. The reply is not that the churches were the heroes but that the Enlightenment's moral premises were not self-generated: its case against cruelty and for equality drew on convictions already in circulation, which is why the arguments landed. Holland's provocation is that secular humanism is less the opposite of Christianity than a Christian heresy—a claim that is contested, and that a reader should weigh rather than accept.8

Other traditions also produced compassion and charity

They plainly did, and any account that implies otherwise is both false and offensive. Buddhist compassion, the Islamic institutions of zakat and the charitable endowment, Jewish tzedakah, Confucian reciprocity, and countless local traditions of hospitality and care all long predate or run parallel to Christian charity, and several are older than it.9

The claim being made is therefore not that Christianity invented kindness—it did not, and no serious version of this argument says so. The narrower claim concerns the specific idea that every human being, without qualification by status, capacity, tribe, or usefulness, possesses equal and inviolable worth, and the further idea that the lowest have a privileged claim. That combination is historically unusual, and the case is that its route into Western law and sentiment ran largely through Christianity. Even that is a claim about a route, not about a monopoly.

If the values can survive without the beliefs, who needs the beliefs?

A fair question, and it does not have a knock-down answer. Values can plainly be held by people who reject the doctrines that produced them; most of the modern West is the proof. Whether they remain stable once detached from their roots is a genuinely open question on which honest people differ, and the confident Christian prediction that they must collapse has been made for two centuries without being clearly vindicated. What the history does suggest is more modest: these convictions were not obvious, are not the default setting of human societies, and were not deduced from reason alone—so treating them as self-evident is a way of forgetting how recently and how partially they were won.

The Verdict

Stated plainly, in both directions.

This is not evidence that Christianity is true, and it has not been offered as any. It also does not show that Christians have been better than other people; the ledger above is the answer to that, and it is a heavy one.

What it does show is that a widespread modern assumption is false. The assumption is that basic moral egalitarianism—the equal worth of every person, the special claim of the weak—is simply what a decent person naturally thinks, requiring no particular history. It is not. It is a specific inheritance with a traceable pedigree, and Christianity is a major part of that pedigree. That claim is contested in its strong forms and defensible in its careful one.

For a Christian reader, the right response is neither triumph nor defensiveness. The tradition's own account of itself predicts exactly this mixed record: a message entrusted to people who fail it, whose failures are judged by the very standard they were given. The honest boast is not that Christians have been good. It is that Christians were handed something better than themselves—and that when the church is at its worst, what convicts it is its own gospel.

Frequently asked questions

Doesn't all the good Christianity did prove it's true?

No, and this essay refuses that inference explicitly. A belief system can be enormously beneficial and entirely false; social benefit is not a truth-claim about God. Christianity's claim on anyone is that it is true, not that it is useful. The case for its truth rests on the historical and philosophical arguments made elsewhere in this library.

What about the Crusades, the Inquisition, slavery, and the abuse crises?

The essay sets them out in full rather than managing them, and adds the harder point: the most sophisticated biblical defences of slavery were themselves Christian, so abolition was a Christian achievement won against Christians. It also refuses the move of using the genealogical argument as mitigation. That the church supplied the moral standard does not shorten what that standard measures.

Isn't it just Enlightenment thinking, not Christianity, that gave us human rights and equality?

This is the strongest counter-account and much of it is granted: churches were on the wrong side of many of those fights. The reply is not that churches were the heroes but that the Enlightenment's moral premises were not self-generated—its case against cruelty and for equality drew on convictions already in circulation. Tom Holland's stronger claim, that secular humanism is a development from within Christianity rather than a repudiation of it, is contested and is presented as contested.
