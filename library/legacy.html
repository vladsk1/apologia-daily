Deep Dive Essay Pro

Did Christianity Change the World?

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

You almost certainly believe that every human being is of equal worth. That the weak deserve protection from the strong. That compassion is a virtue and cruelty a vice. That a person's value does not depend on their usefulness, their birth, or their capacity.

You probably think these are obvious. They are not. For most of recorded history almost nobody built a society on them, and the societies that produced Western philosophy did not. This essay is about where they came from—and, just as importantly, about what that question can and cannot settle.

The Question, Stated Carefully

This is the section that governs everything else, and skipping it is how this argument goes wrong.

First: nothing here is evidence that Christianity is true. A belief system can be enormously beneficial and entirely false, and a religion's social record is not a truth-claim about God. Anyone who argues “look what Christianity did for the world, therefore it is true” has committed a plain error, and a thoughtful sceptic will name it immediately. The case for the faith's truth rests on the historical and philosophical arguments made elsewhere in this library; it does not rest here, and it must not be smuggled in.

Christianity's claim on anyone is that it is true, not that it is useful. If it were false, its social benefits would be a reason to be grateful for a mistake—not a reason to believe it.

Second: what is being claimed is genealogical. The defensible thesis is not that Christianity made the world nicer. It is that a specific set of moral convictions now treated as self-evident—universal human dignity above all—are not self-evident, have a traceable history, and that Christianity is a major part of that history. This is a claim about where a standard came from, not about how well anyone has lived up to it.

Third: historical causation is contested, and the honest form of the claim admits it. Large civilisational changes have many causes, and disentangling them is exactly what historians argue about. The most that should be said is what this library already says of a parallel case in the history of science: that the very idea of universal “laws of nature” took shape within a Christian frame is a strong causal claim that remains genuinely debated among historians, even as the underlying facts are not.1

Holland's Thesis

The most discussed recent statement of the case is Tom Holland's Dominion (2019). Holland is a popular historian and broadcaster whose earlier books are on Greece, Rome and Islam; Dominion is a work of synthesis for a general readership rather than specialist research, and it has been reviewed accordingly—widely praised for its sweep, and criticised for the flattening any sweep costs.2

Holland's route to the argument is worth reporting because it began not in faith but in disenchantment: the more closely he studied Greece and Rome, the less he found he shared their moral universe at all. Roman power rested on chattel slavery of an ordinariness that has few parallels; crucifixion was engineered to strip a human being of dignity in public; Caesar was reported to have killed a million Gauls and enslaved a million more, and reported it as a credit to him.3 Individual voices in that world did question such things—Aristotle defends slavery as natural precisely because others were calling it a mere convention against nature, and Stoics from Seneca to the jurist Ulpian could write that a slave is a fellow human being, or that by natural law all are born equal.4 What did not exist was any movement, party, or body of law proposing that the institution be ended, or any settled conviction that the weak have a claim on the strong simply by being human. That conviction, Holland argues, the West received from a particular history rather than deducing it from reason—and the most unlikely thing in that history is at its root: a God executed by torture as a criminal.

Glen Scrivener makes a similar case at a popular level in The Air We Breathe (2022), whose title carries the point: the values feel like atmosphere because we have never been without them.5

What Changed, and Where It Came From

The thesis is only as good as the specifics, so here are three, stated at the strength the evidence supports and no higher.

The image of God, carried to a crucified God

Two convictions do most of the work, and the first is not Christian in origin. That every human being bears the image of God (Genesis 1:27) is a Jewish doctrine, developed in Jewish scripture and rabbinic interpretation long before and independently of the church; Judaism's own account of universal human worth stands on its own feet, and this essay does not claim it. Christ is one person in two natures, fully God and fully man; he died in his true human nature, and God did not cease to be God. by the method Rome reserved for slaves and rebels. Paul knew exactly how this sounded: he calls the message of a crucified Messiah a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles (1 Corinthians 1:23). A civilisation that came to worship a crucified man as God had, in principle, lost the ability to say that the humiliated are contemptible—though in practice it went on saying so for centuries, which is the ledger below.6

Care of the abandoned and the sick

Early Christian communities refused the exposure of unwanted infants and organised care for the sick and the poor—including, as the emperor Julian complained while trying to compete with them, poor who were not their own. That material is set out in the companion essay on the growth of the early church. The institutional legacy is real, and the precise claim is stronger than the usual apologetic overstatement. Christians did not invent medicine, and the ancient world had healing shrines and military infirmaries. What has no clear ancient precedent is the hospital as an institution: a permanent, charitably funded foundation open to the sick poor as such and staffed to care for them. Basil of Caesarea's complex outside that city, established around 372, is the standard early example. The point is contested at the edges—some historians urge caution about how sharp and how early the break was—but institutional priority in charitable care of the sick poor is what survives the debate.7

Abolition

Slavery was near-universal across complex societies, defended by Aristotle as natural and practised on every inhabited continent. Its abolition was not universal; it happened. The organised British campaign that began in 1787 drew heavily on Christian conviction—Quaker networks, Granville Sharp and Thomas Clarkson's evidence-gathering, and, from 1791, the Clapham circle around William Wilberforce—alongside the testimony of formerly enslaved Africans such as Olaudah Equiano, whose narrative frames its case in Christian terms.8 But the causation is genuinely disputed and the best modern account is not a providential one: Christopher Leslie Brown argues that Quaker and evangelical antislavery long predated the campaign, and that what changed in the 1780s was political—after the loss of America, antislavery offered Britain a way to recover moral standing. The Christian conviction was necessary and was not sufficient; the enslaved themselves, from the Haitian revolution to the Jamaican rebellions, forced the question as much as any committee did.9

And the honest sentence is the next one. The most sophisticated defences of slavery were also Christian, produced in quantity by clergy and theologians. When the isolated proof-texts were read on their own, the pro-slavery side had the easier of them—which is why the American conflict became, in Mark Noll's account, a crisis of biblical interpretation itself. What the pro-slavery reading did not have was the whole: the abolitionists read from creation in God's image, from the exodus, from the condemnation of the man-stealer in Exodus 21:16 and 1 Timothy 1:10—which names the slave-trader precisely—and argued that the canon read entire runs against an institution its parts merely regulate.10 Abolition was a Christian achievement that had to be won against Christians.

The Ledger Against

An essay that stopped there would be advertising, not history. The record includes the following, and none of it is incidental.

The First Crusade's massacre of the Rhineland Jewish communities in 1096 and of the population of Jerusalem in 1099. The judicial machinery of the medieval and Spanish inquisitions, and the burning of heretics. The witch trials of the early modern period, prosecuted in Protestant and Catholic territories alike. The expulsion of the Jews from Spain in 1492, and centuries of Christian anti-Judaism before and after it—ghettoes, forced disputations, blood libels, pogroms. Forced conversion and the destruction of indigenous religion and culture across the Americas, Africa, Asia and the Pacific under the cover of mission. The churches' extensive complicity in the Atlantic slave trade and in colonial expropriation, and the theological defence of racial segregation and of apartheid—the latter repudiated by the tradition that built it only at Rustenburg in 1990. The residential schools for indigenous children in Canada, whose abuses a national commission documented in 2015, and the industrial schools and Magdalene institutions of Ireland. And, in living memory, the sexual abuse of children by clergy and its concealment, across traditions.11

Three things must be said about that list, and the order matters.

It is not a caricature, and it is not exhaustive. Each item is a settled matter of historical record. Nothing that follows is offered as a reason to think any of it smaller than it is.

It cuts across every tradition. Catholics, Orthodox and Protestants are all implicated, in different proportions in different centuries; this ledger, like the thesis it tests, is weighted toward the West, which is not a claim that the Eastern churches have a clean record. This essay does not distribute the blame among the traditions—partly because doing so would require adjudicating the disputes that divide them, which is not its business.

And the genealogical argument must never be used to soften any of it. There is a move available here, and it must be refused. One could observe that the standard by which we condemn these things is itself substantially a Christian inheritance, and let that observation function as mitigation. It does not, and it never did. Where a standard came from and whether it was obeyed are logically independent—and if anything, being handed the standard is an aggravating circumstance for the people who broke it, not a mitigating one. Anyone who reaches for Holland's thesis to make the Crusades or the abuse crisis feel smaller has misunderstood it and should be told so.

The Strongest Objections

It took eighteen centuries, and only in one part of Christendom

This is the objection a well-read sceptic actually presses, and the essay has handed them its premises already: if universal dignity was in the deposit from the first century, why did abolition, toleration and the rest wait until the eighteenth—and arrive then only in north-western Europe, while Byzantine, Russian, Ethiopian and Iberian-American Christendoms, as Christian as any, produced none of it?

The reply is not a dodge, and it is the claim the rest of this essay has quietly presupposed and should now state: Christianity was a necessary ingredient in a particular combination, not a sufficient cause. A standard can be held and disobeyed for centuries—that is exactly what the ledger records—and its being disobeyed is evidence about human beings, not about where it came from. That the decisive changes came late, and in one place, alongside printing, commerce and the Enlightenment, shows that Christianity by itself did not produce them. It does not show that they would have come without it. The genealogical claim is about a route, not a monopoly and not a guarantee.

These are Stoic or Enlightenment values, not Christian ones

Two counter-genealogies press from opposite directions, and both deserve better than the essay's thesis alone would give them.

The classical one: the Stoics and the Roman jurists already reasoned about a common humanity—Seneca on the shared humanity of slaves, Ulpian's principle that by natural law all are equal. That is a real tradition of universalist moral reasoning, older than Christianity, and Christian writers drew on it heavily. Two things can be said in reply, and neither is a knock-out. The Stoic universalism is a universalism of reason: what all share is a rational nature, which grades—the sage really is further along than the fool—where the image of God does not. And it did not become institutional: Seneca owned slaves, Ulpian's principle sat in a code that enforced slavery, and no Stoic proposed abolishing anything. Whether that is a difference of doctrine or only of historical accident is itself disputed, and a reader should treat it as disputed.

The modern one: the standard secular account credits toleration, human rights and the abolition of judicial torture to Enlightenment thinkers who fought clerical power to establish them, and can point to churches on the wrong side of most of those fights. The factual half of that is simply true and should be granted without wriggling: the churches were often on the wrong side. The reply is not that they were the heroes but that the Enlightenment's moral premises were not self-generated—its case against cruelty and for equality drew on convictions already in wide circulation, a claim that has to be argued from the sources rather than inferred from the fact that the arguments succeeded.12 A sharper version of this objection cuts the other way and the essay should name it: Samuel Moyn argues that “human rights” in their modern sense are a twentieth-century phenomenon—the phrase coined in the 1940s, the idea breaking through only in the 1970s—and that the long Christian, Enlightenment and classical genealogies are all retrojection. That is a serious position, it is not refuted here, and a reader weighing this essay should weigh it too.13

Other traditions also produced compassion and charity

They plainly did, and any account that implies otherwise is simply false. Jewish tzedakah grows from the Scriptures Christians also confess, so it is less a parallel to the Christian claim than a shared root of it. Beyond that shared root, Buddhist compassion reaches all sentient beings, wider than any human-focused ethic—though compassion for all is a different kind of claim from the inviolable, equal, rights-bearing worth of each human being, which is the specific idea at issue here; Mohist “impartial care” argued in classical China for concern without regard to rank or kin; and the Qur'an's teaching that, in its common reading, to save one life is to save all humanity is a universalist claim in the same family. The Islamic institutions of zakat and the charitable endowment are real and vast, though they postdate Christianity by six centuries rather than predating it.14

The claim being made is therefore not that Christianity invented kindness—it did not, and no serious version of this argument says so. The narrower claim concerns the specific idea that every human being, without qualification by status, capacity, tribe or usefulness, possesses equal and inviolable worth, together with the further idea that the lowest have a privileged claim; and the case is that the route by which that specific combination entered Western law and sentiment ran largely through Christianity. Even that is a claim about a route, not a monopoly. The question is not which tradition is kindest, which is unanswerable.

If the values can survive without the beliefs, who needs the beliefs?

A fair question, and it does not have a knock-down answer. Values can plainly be held by people who reject the doctrines that produced them; the modern West is the obvious case in point. Whether they remain stable once detached from their roots is genuinely open, and the confident Christian prediction that they must collapse has been made for two centuries without being clearly vindicated. So the honest answer is that nobody knows, and Christians who claim to know are overreaching. What the history does settle is narrower and is not nothing: these convictions are not the default setting of human societies, were not deduced from reason alone, and were won late and partially. Whatever else is true, they are not self-evident—and anyone who holds them owes an account of why they hold, rather than treating them as the air.

The Verdict

What it does show is that a widespread modern assumption is false. The assumption is that basic moral egalitarianism—the equal worth of every person, the special claim of the weak—is simply what a decent person naturally thinks, requiring no particular history. It is not. It is a specific inheritance with a traceable pedigree, in which Judaism is the root and Christianity a major part of the route into the Western world. That claim is contested in its strong forms and defensible in its careful one.

What it does not show is that Christianity is true, and it has not been offered as any evidence of it. Nor does it show that Christians have been better than other people; the ledger above is the answer to that, and it is a heavy one.

For a Christian reader, the right response is neither triumph nor defensiveness. The tradition never promised that the people entrusted with its message would live up to it, and it does not depend on their having done so—which is just as well, because the ledger is what they did with it. The honest thing to say is not that Christians have been good. It is that they were handed something better than themselves: a gospel they did not invent and have never lived up to, about a Lord who forgives his church its history as he forgives everything else, and by whose standard—not the church's own record—the church stands or falls.

Frequently asked questions

Doesn't all the good Christianity did prove it's true?

No, and this essay refuses that inference explicitly. A belief system can be enormously beneficial and entirely false; social benefit is not a truth-claim about God. Christianity's claim on anyone is that it is true, not that it is useful — if it were false, its social benefits would be a reason to be grateful for a mistake, not a reason to believe it. The case for its truth rests on the historical and philosophical arguments made elsewhere in this library.

What about the Crusades, the inquisitions, slavery, and the abuse crises?

The essay sets them out in full — the 1096 Rhineland massacres, the burning of heretics, the witch trials, colonial forced conversion, the theological defence of apartheid, and the clergy abuse crisis — and adds the harder point: the most sophisticated defences of slavery were themselves Christian, so abolition was won against Christians. It also refuses the move of using the genealogical argument as mitigation. That the church supplied a moral standard does not shorten what that standard measures; being handed the standard aggravates the failure to keep it.

Isn't it just Enlightenment thinking, not Christianity, that gave us human rights and equality?

This is one of the strongest counter-accounts and much of it is granted: the churches were on the wrong side of many of those fights. The reply is not that churches were the heroes but that the Enlightenment's moral premises were not self-generated — its case against cruelty and for equality drew on convictions already in wide circulation. A sharper objection runs the other way: Samuel Moyn argues that human rights in their modern sense are a mid-twentieth-century development and that all the long genealogies are retrojection. That position is serious and is not refuted here.
