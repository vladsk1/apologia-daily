Why a Crucified Messiah? The Scandal of the Cross

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

Every movement leads with its best foot. The earliest Christians led with their worst. At the center of the message they carried out of Jerusalem was not a teaching, a miracle, or an empty tomb, but an execution — and not a noble one. They proclaimed as Israel’s anointed king a man Rome had nailed to a cross, the death it reserved for slaves and rebels, the death a Jew’s own Scriptures called a curse. It was, by any ordinary reckoning, the single fact most likely to kill the movement in its cradle. Paul knew it exactly: “we preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles” (1 Corinthians 1:23).1 This essay asks why — why the first Christians built their gospel on the one claim their world found most offensive, and what that strange choice tells the historian and the believer alike.

How offensive the cross was

It is hard, at this distance, to feel the scandal. The cross is now jewelry. In the first century it was an instrument of torture so degrading that polite Romans avoided the word. The classicist Martin Hengel, in the standard study of the subject, gathered the ancient sources into a single verdict: crucifixion was “the most wretched of deaths,” reserved for slaves, violent criminals, and rebels against Rome, deliberately public and deliberately obscene.2 Cicero told a Roman jury that “the very word ‘cross’ should be far removed not only from the body of a Roman citizen but from his thoughts, his eyes, his ears.”3 To announce that one worshipped a crucified man was, to a cultured Greek or Roman, simply folly — the word Paul himself uses.

For a Jew the offense cut deeper, and touched God directly. The Law was explicit: “a hanged man is cursed by God” — “if a man has committed a crime punishable by death and he is put to death, and you hang him on a tree… you shall bury him the same day, for a hanged man is cursed by God” (Deuteronomy 21:22–23).4 A crucified body hanging on a Roman cross was, on the face of the text, a body under the visible curse of God. So “crucified Messiah” was not merely an unhappy phrase; it was very nearly a contradiction in terms. The Messiah was the LORD’s anointed, the son of David who would break the nations and reign — not a condemned criminal dying under God’s own curse at the hands of the pagan empire he was supposed to overthrow. A crucified Messiah looked less like a vindicated king than like a blasphemous joke.

Here a note of care is owed, because the point is easily overstated. It is not that no strand of ancient Judaism ever contemplated a suffering righteous figure — the Servant of Isaiah 53 was read by some in an individual, even messianic key, as our essay on messianic prophecy discusses, and later tradition knew of a suffering “Messiah son of Joseph.” The claim is narrower and firmer: the dominant hope was for a triumphant deliverer, and a crucified Messiah — executed as a criminal, hung under the Deuteronomic curse, by Rome — was the opposite of what that hope expected and, to most first-century Jews, an offense to be resisted rather than a promise to be welcomed.

Why the scandal is evidence

Now set beside that a simple historical observation: inventors do not sabotage their own inventions. A group fabricating a messianic hero from scratch, or gilding the memory of a beloved teacher, has every reason to leave out the detail that most discredits the claim — and crucifixion was that detail. That the earliest Christians did the opposite, placing the offense at the dead center of their preaching, is not what fabrication looks like. It is what looks like a community stuck with a fact it could not deny and did not choose.

This is why the crucifixion is, by wide agreement, the most secure single fact about Jesus of Nazareth. Its bedrock status rests first on plain attestation: it is reported not only by Christians but by non-Christian writers with no motive to flatter the movement — the Roman historian Tacitus, and (in a notice whose core is widely accepted as authentic) Josephus — and even determinedly sceptical scholars grant it, John Dominic Crossan calling it “as sure as anything historical can ever be.”5 The scandal adds a second, converging consideration. Strictly, the point is not that the crucifixion embarrassed the Christians who preached it — as the next section shows, they gloried in it — but that no one setting out to invent a Messiah, and no audience they hoped to win, would ever have chosen this detail; a claim so damaging to its own cause is not the kind a movement fabricates. That reasoning is a historian’s tool of probability, not a proof, and it has its critics; here it does not stand alone but reinforces the attestation.

The sharpest illustration is a single man. Before he was the apostle Paul, Saul of Tarsus found the crucified-Messiah claim so intolerable that he set out to destroy the people making it (Galatians 1:13–14).6 He of all people understood the Deuteronomic curse; a cursed Messiah was, to the Pharisee he was, the ultimate blasphemy. That the movement’s most capable enemy became its most relentless missionary — and never once tried to soften the scandal he had once found damning — is a fact demanding explanation, and it is the subject of our essay on Paul’s conversion.

The weight of this argument must be placed carefully. What the scandal establishes is that the crucifixion is authentic bedrock — too damaging to have been invented, present at the movement’s foundation, believed by people who had every earthly reason to wish it away. What it does not do, by itself, is prove that Jesus rose. The offensiveness of the cross explains why no one would fabricate it; it does not explain why anyone would then celebrate it. For that a further event is needed — the vindication the first Christians claimed had occurred — and that resurrection case is argued on its own evidence elsewhere. The scandal clears the ground; it does not, alone, raise the dead.

The gospel that refused to hide it

What is most striking is not that the first Christians admitted the cross but that they gloried in it. A movement merely coping with an embarrassing fact would minimize it. Paul does the reverse. “I decided to know nothing among you except Jesus Christ and him crucified” (1 Corinthians 2:2); “far be it from me to boast except in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ” (Galatians 6:14).7 The offense was not an awkwardness to be managed on the way to the message; it was the message. “The word of the cross is folly to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God” (1 Corinthians 1:18). Paul takes the very feature that made the claim contemptible and names it the point.

That is a remarkable rhetorical posture, and it is hard to explain as invention or salesmanship. People selling a fabricated hero do not stake everything on his most humiliating hour. People convinced that the most humiliating hour had been reversed — and had turned out to mean something — might.

The answer the New Testament gives

So the first Christians did not merely concede the curse; they claimed to understand it. Their answer runs straight through the offending text. Paul writes: “Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for usWhat “becoming a curse for us” meansIs sayingAt the cross Christ willingly bore, in our place, the curse and death that sin incurs — the plain sense of Galatians 3:13 quoting Deuteronomy 21:23. Christians across the traditions confess that Christ’s death reconciles us to God.Not sayingNot that the Father hated or was divided from the Son, nor that the divine nature could die or be cursed. And not the endorsement of any one theory of how the cross saves — the church has explained the mechanism in several orthodox ways, and this essay does not adjudicate between them. — for it is written, ‘Cursed is everyone who is hanged on a tree’” (Galatians 3:13).8 The move is audacious: the exact verse (Deuteronomy 21:23) that made a crucified Messiah a scandal becomes the key to what the crucifixion accomplished. The curse was real, Paul grants — and it was borne, willingly, in the place of those who deserved it. The scandal is not denied; it is absorbed and turned inside out.

The same reversal is read back into Israel’s Scriptures. The Servant of Isaiah 53 is “pierced for our transgressions… and with his wounds we are healed” — a suffering that is not meaningless but substitutionary, the theme our messianic-prophecy essay treats (and whose contested reading it treats honestly). Christ is called “our Passover lamb” who “has been sacrificed” (1 Corinthians 5:7), placing the cross within the story of the exodus.9 Across these images runs one confession: the death that looked like God’s curse on a failed pretender was in fact God’s appointed means of rescue.

How exactly the cross saves — the mechanism of the atonement — is a question on which sincere Christians have long differed, and this essay deliberately does not settle it. Some traditions emphasize Christ bearing the penalty of sin in our place; others, his victory over the powers of sin and death; others, the satisfaction of divine honor, or the recapitulation of humanity, or the transforming power of self-giving love. These are not all equal, and each tradition argues its case — but they are the family’s internal debate, held within a shared confession the New Testament states plainly and this essay is content to state with it: “Christ died for our sins” (1 Corinthians 15:3), and in that death the curse was borne and God and sinners reconciled.

What this establishes, and what it does not

Two honesties keep the case sound. The first is about the argument’s reach. The scandal of the cross is powerful evidence that the crucifixion is historical bedrock — the kind of fact a movement is stuck with, not the kind it invents — and it shows that the earliest Christians met that fact not with denial but with a developed theology of the curse borne. It does not, on its own, demonstrate that the theology is true, nor that Jesus rose; those are further steps, resting on further evidence. An honest critic can grant every word of this essay and still ask whether the crucified man stayed dead.

The second is about the answer. That the New Testament reads the cross as saving is beyond dispute; how it saves is a question the Christian traditions have answered in more than one orthodox way, and nothing here is meant to crown one of them. What can be said plainly is the thing the first Christians said plainly, at the cost of every advantage: that the Messiah was crucified, that they did not hide it, and that they staked everything on the claim that his most shameful hour was, in the strange economy of God, his most decisive work. “For the word of the cross is folly to those who are perishing, but to us who are being saved it is the power of God” (1 Corinthians 1:18) — a claim made, then as now, best “with gentleness and respect” (1 Peter 3:15).

Frequently asked questions

Wasn’t a crucified Messiah just an embarrassment the first Christians had to explain away?

They did the opposite of explaining it away — they made it the center. “We preach Christ crucified” (1 Corinthians 1:23); “I decided to know nothing among you except Jesus Christ and him crucified” (1 Corinthians 2:2); “far be it from me to boast except in the cross” (Galatians 6:14). And that is exactly why historians treat the crucifixion as bedrock: a movement does not invent the one fact most damaging to its own claim — a Messiah executed as a criminal, under the Deuteronomic curse, by Rome.

Didn’t Jews expect a suffering, even a crucified, Messiah?

The dominant hope was for a triumphant deliverer, a son of David who would reign — not a condemned man dying under God’s curse (Deuteronomy 21:23) at the hands of the pagan empire he was meant to overthrow. It is true that some strands of ancient Judaism read the Servant of Isaiah 53 in an individual key, and later tradition knew of a suffering “Messiah son of Joseph.” But a crucified Messiah — the specific scandal of a Roman cross — was the opposite of what the mainstream expected, which is why it was resisted rather than welcomed.

How can a cross — a curse and an execution — be good news?

Paul answers with the very verse that made it a scandal: “Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us — for it is written, ‘Cursed is everyone who is hanged on a tree’” (Galatians 3:13, quoting Deuteronomy 21:23). The curse was real, and it was borne, willingly, in the place of those who deserved it — the death that looked like God’s judgment on a failed pretender was God’s appointed means of rescue (Isaiah 53; “Christ our Passover lamb,” 1 Corinthians 5:7). How the cross saves is explained in more than one orthodox way across the Christian traditions; the shared confession this essay states is that “Christ died for our sins” (1 Corinthians 15:3).
