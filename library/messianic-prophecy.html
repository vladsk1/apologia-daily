The Witness of the Prophets: How the Hebrew Scriptures Converge on Jesus

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

Short on time? Read the quick answer →

An Argument as Old as the Church Itself

On the first Easter evening, two disconsolate disciples walked the road to Emmaus, and a stranger fell in beside them. "Was it not necessary," he asked, "that the Christ should suffer these things and enter into his glory?" Then, Luke tells us, "beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself."1 The risen Jesus did not first appeal to the empty tomb. He appealed to the prophets.

His followers never forgot it. When Peter stood before the Pentecost crowd, he reached for the Psalms (Acts 2:25–31). When Philip met the Ethiopian official puzzling over Isaiah 53, he "told him the good news about Jesus" from that very page (Acts 8:35). Paul "reasoned with them from the Scriptures" in synagogue after synagogue (Acts 17:2–3). The argument from prophecy is not a modern apologetic invention; it is the oldest Christian argument there is, and it deserves to be made well — which means made honestly.

This essay sets aside a popular but flawed version of the argument (the "statistical" case, treated below) and turns instead to the prophecies themselves: what they say, where the New Testament finds them fulfilled, and — crucially — which carry real evidential weight and which do not. A serious case is one that can survive its own footnotes.

How to Read Prophecy Honestly

The first thing a careful reader must grasp is that the New Testament writers use the word "fulfill" (Greek plēroō) in more than one sense, and not always to mean "a prediction came true." Sometimes it means exactly that — a future-tense forecast realized in a specific event. But often it means something closer to "filled full": an earlier pattern, person, or institution reaches its intended completion in Christ. This is what scholars call typology, and a related idea, sensus plenior — a "fuller sense" God intended in a text beyond what its human author consciously foresaw.

The classic example is Hosea 11:1, "Out of Egypt I called my son." In Hosea the verse is plainly about the past — Israel's exodus — and contains no future-tense verb at all. When Matthew applies it to the infant Jesus' return from Egypt (Matthew 2:15), he is not claiming Hosea predicted it. He is drawing an analogy: Jesus relives and succeeds in Israel's story.2 To call that a "fulfilled prediction" would be to misread Matthew himself.

This honesty matters enormously, because the strength of the prophetic argument is uneven, and pretending otherwise discredits the whole. So throughout what follows, prophecies are flagged: strong ones concern matters outside any individual's control or staging (birthplace, lineage, the manner and timing of an execution under Roman law); weaker or typological ones are real and meaningful but could in principle be arranged, or were not predictions in their original setting. The cumulative case rests chiefly on the strong ones.

A Catalogue of the Prophecies

Lineage and Birth

Long before any town or tribe is named, the Hebrew Scriptures open a single thread and slowly draw it tight. In the garden, God tells the serpent that the woman’s offspring will one day strike the serpent’s head even as the serpent strikes his heel (Genesis 3:15) — words that Christian readers from the earliest centuries took as the first, faint announcement of a deliverer to come. This is a trajectory, not yet a falsifiable prediction; its force is only that the promise then narrows, generation by generation, from the whole human family toward a single line.

The expected deliverer is mapped genealogically with increasing precision. He is the "offspring" of Abraham, through whom "all the families of the earth shall be blessed" (Genesis 12:3; cf. 22:18) — applied to Christ in Galatians 3:16. He comes from the tribe of Judah: "The scepter shall not depart from Judah... until tribute comes to him" (Genesis 49:10), echoed in Revelation 5:5. He is of the line of David, to whom God promised an everlasting throne (2 Samuel 7:12–16; Isaiah 9:7; Jeremiah 23:5), a claim the New Testament presses hard (Matthew 1:1; Romans 1:3). Strength: lineage is outside an individual's control, and the New Testament's confident appeal to it — in a culture that took genealogical descent with great seriousness — is notable (the two Gospel genealogies, Matthew 1 and Luke 3, differ in ways that are a genuine difficulty, not merely a matter of scholarly debate—the proposed harmonizations, such as Luke giving Mary’s line or a levirate-marriage explanation, are possible but contested—but the datum that carries the weight here, a public claim to Davidic descent, does not depend on resolving them).

Micah names the town: "But you, O Bethlehem Ephrathah... from you shall come forth for me one who is to be ruler in Israel" (Micah 5:2), fulfilled in Matthew 2:1–6. The Hebrew adds that the ruler's "coming forth is from of old, from ancient days" — a phrase debated between "ancient lineage" and something more.3 Strength: a specific, falsifiable birthplace — though it rests on the infancy narratives of Matthew and Luke alone, and so carries less weight against a “prophecy historicized” account than the crucifixion does.

The most contested birth text is Isaiah 7:14: "the almah shall conceive and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel." Here we must be candid. The Hebrew ‘almah means a young woman of marriageable age; it does not by itself specify virginity (the more precise word would be betulah). The Jewish translators of the Septuagint, however (Isaiah was rendered into Greek in the second century BC), translated it parthenos, "virgin" — and it is the Septuagint Matthew quotes (Matthew 1:23).4 Moreover, the verse's immediate context is a sign to King Ahaz about a child born in his own lifetime. Many scholars therefore read Isaiah 7:14 as having a near-term referent in Isaiah's day and a fuller messianic sense Matthew discerns in hindsight — a "dual fulfillment." This is a contested, largely typological case, not a knockdown prediction, and it should be presented as such.

Mission and Ministry

A forerunner prepares the way: "Behold, I send my messenger" (Malachi 3:1) and "a voice cries: In the wilderness prepare the way of the LORD" (Isaiah 40:3), both applied to John the Baptist (Mark 1:2–3). The light dawns in the north: "Galilee of the nations... the people who walked in darkness have seen a great light" (Isaiah 9:1–2), cited in Matthew 4:14–16. The Deliverer is a "prophet like Moses" (Deuteronomy 18:15; cf. Acts 3:22). His ministry brings healing — eyes of the blind opened, the lame leaping (Isaiah 35:5–6) — which Jesus invokes as his credential (Matthew 11:4–5). He teaches in parables (Psalm 78:2; Matthew 13:34–35), and zeal for God's house consumes him (Psalm 69:9; John 2:17). He enters Jerusalem "humble and mounted on a donkey" (Zechariah 9:9; Matthew 21:1–9) — though this is the clearest example of a stageable prophecy, one the Gospels themselves present Jesus as deliberately arranging. Strength: mixed. The forerunner and the geography are reasonably firm; healing and teaching-style are the kind of thing that could be shaped in the telling, so they carry less independent weight.

Rejection and Passion

Here the texts grow startling. The Suffering Servant of Isaiah 52:13–53:12 is "despised and rejected," "pierced for our transgressions," "like a lamb that is led to the slaughter" who "opened not his mouth," whose "soul makes an offering for guilt," and who is "with a rich man in his death." Isaiah 53:9 even pairs a criminal's death ("with the wicked") and a wealthy burial — answered in Jesus' crucifixion between two thieves and burial in Joseph of Arimathea's tomb (Matthew 27:57–60). The Servant's silence before accusers (Isaiah 53:7) meets Jesus' silence before Pilate (Matthew 27:12–14).

Psalm 22 opens with the cry Jesus echoed from the cross — "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Psalm 22:1; Matthew 27:46) — and goes on to describe mockery (vv. 7–8), thirst and exhaustion (vv. 14–15), and lots cast for clothing (v. 18; John 19:24). Then comes verse 16, one of the most disputed lines in the Hebrew Bible. The traditional Masoretic Text reads ka'ari — "like a lion" — yielding the awkward "like a lion my hands and my feet." But a fragment from Nahal Hever (5/6HevPs, 1st century AD) reads ka'aru, and the ancient Septuagint reads ōryxan, "they dug/pierced." The Septuagint (pre-Christian) and a disputed 1st-century AD fragment together suggest a "pierced/dug" reading is at least old and not a Christian invention — though the Nahal Hever fragment is 1st-century AD rather than pre-Christian, and its reading is contested; scholars such as Brent Strawn defend the Masoretic "lion," and early published photographs of the fragment were faint. This verse is genuinely contested; honesty requires saying so, and the broader Psalm does not depend on it. The point about the manner of Jesus' death and burial should therefore rest not on the disputed Psalm 22:16 but on the textually secure Isaiah 53:9—a criminal's death paired with a rich man's burial—which the manuscripts place well before the crucifixion.5

Other passion details: betrayal by an intimate, "my close friend... who ate my bread" (Psalm 41:9; John 13:18); thirty pieces of silver thrown "to the potter" (Zechariah 11:12–13), eerily matching Judas's betrayal price spent on a potter's field (Matthew 27:3–10 — which, candidly, attributes the quotation to Jeremiah, a long-noted difficulty usually explained by scroll-naming convention or a composite citation).6 No bone is broken (Psalm 34:20; cf. the Passover lamb of Exodus 12:46; John 19:33–36); his side is pierced (Zechariah 12:10, "they look on me, on him whom they have pierced"; John 19:34–37).

And there is Daniel 9:24–27, which foretells that "an anointed one shall be cut off" after a span of "sixty-two weeks" following a decree to restore Jerusalem — language Christians have read as timing the Messiah's death before the Second Temple's destruction (the text says the city and sanctuary will be destroyed afterward, which happened in AD 70). But the dating is heavily contested: which decree starts the clock (Cyrus c. 538 BC, or Artaxerxes in 458 or 445 BC?), and whether the "anointed one" is the Messiah, a high priest, or Onias III, are all live scholarly disputes. The passage is suggestive but should not be overclaimed.7

Vindication and Resurrection

The Scriptures do not end at the grave. "You will not abandon my soul to Sheol, or let your holy one see corruption" (Psalm 16:10) — which Peter (Acts 2:25–31) and Paul (Acts 13:35) argue David could not have meant of himself, since David died and decayed. The vindicated one is enthroned: "The LORD says to my Lord, Sit at my right hand" (Psalm 110:1), the Old Testament verse the New Testament quotes most often. And in Daniel 7:13–14, "one like a son of man" comes "with the clouds of heaven" and receives "an everlasting dominion." "Son of Man" is the very title Jesus chose for himself.

The Honest Objections

1. The retrojection worry

The sharpest objection is this: the Gospel writers knew the Hebrew Scriptures intimately, so what looks like fulfillment may be the writers shaping their narratives to match the texts. This must be conceded for the "stageable" prophecies — a man could deliberately ride a donkey into Jerusalem (Zechariah 9:9), and an author could narrate a detail to echo a psalm.

The reply is to lean on the prophecies outside anyone's control or staging: where one is born, what tribe and lineage one belongs to, and the manner and timing of one's death under an occupying empire that alone held the power of capital crucifixion. A first-century Jew could not arrange to be born in Bethlehem of David's line, nor engineer a Roman execution among criminals, followed by a rich man's burial, the pairing the textually secure Isaiah 53:9 describes centuries earlier. As for the charge that the evangelists wrote the details in: first, the central fact—the crucifixion of Jesus under Pilate—is among the best-attested events in ancient history, reported by the Roman historian Tacitus (Annals 15.44) and the Jewish historian Josephus (Antiquities 18.3.3), not derived from any psalm. Second, a crucified messiah was not a story first-century Jews were predisposed to invent; crucifixion signified a curse (Deuteronomy 21:23), and a humiliated, executed deliverer ran against the grain of messianic expectation, which is why Paul calls the message a “stumbling block” (1 Corinthians 1:23). One does not fabricate the most embarrassing possible claim to win converts. And the texts are demonstrably pre-Christian: the Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran (1QIsaᵃ) — containing Isaiah 7, 9, 35, 40, and all of chapter 53 — is dated by paleography and radiocarbon to roughly 125 BC, well over a century before Jesus.8 No one shaped Isaiah after the fact.

2. The "not originally messianic / out of context" objection

Jewish counter-missionary scholars — Rabbi Tovia Singer foremost among them — argue that many of these texts were not predictions of an individual Messiah at all in their original setting. Isaiah 7:14 addresses Ahaz; Hosea 11:1 looks backward to the Exodus. Most pointedly, the "Servant" of Isaiah is in several nearby passages explicitly named as the nation Israel (Isaiah 41:8; 49:3), so Isaiah 53, on this reading, depicts suffering, faithful Israel among the nations — not a crucified Messiah.

First, on Isaiah 53: within the song the Servant is "stricken for the transgression of my people" (53:8) — distinguished from the people, which fits awkwardly with a purely collective reading, though its defenders appeal to a righteous remnant suffering for the wider nation. Second, the messianic-individual interpretation is not a Christian imposition: both individual-messianic and collective readings are attested in ancient Judaism — the Targum on Isaiah 52:13 names "my servant the Messiah," evidence that an individual-messianic reading of the Servant was live in ancient Judaism, even though the Targum itself redirects the Servant's sufferings to Israel and the nations — and the collective-Israel reading, itself attested as early as Origen (Contra Celsum 1.55, c. AD 248), became dominant in medieval Jewish exegesis, a shift many associate with Rashi.9 Christians should hold this point gently. The collective reading is sincere, rooted in Isaiah's own servant-Israel language, and was sharpened in centuries when Jewish communities were suffering at Christian hands. Isaiah 53's referent is genuinely contested, but the objection does not hold: this Servant “had done no violence, and there was no deceit in his mouth” (53:9), while servant-Israel elsewhere in Isaiah is “blind” and “deaf” (42:19) and is told, “you have burdened me with your sins” (43:24); even the song’s own speakers confess, “All we like sheep have gone astray” (53:6). The individual reading is the better one, and Jesus fits it. The honest move is to let it strengthen the cumulative pattern, not to wield it as a club.

3. The “unfinished work” objection: peace, the ingathering, and the Temple

The weightiest Jewish objection is not about any single text. It is that the Messiah has a work to do, and Jesus did not do it. The prophets promise that in the messianic age the nations “shall beat their swords into plowshares” and “neither shall they learn war anymore” (Isaiah 2:4); that God will gather the scattered people of Israel “from all around,” set one king of David’s line over them, and “set my sanctuary in their midst forevermore” (Ezekiel 37:21–28); and that “the earth shall be full of the knowledge of the LORD as the waters cover the sea” (Isaiah 11:9), when “they shall all know me, from the least of them to the greatest” (Jeremiah 31:34). Twenty centuries after Jesus, wars go on, the Temple has not been rebuilt, and the knowledge of God does not cover the earth. The classic statement of the test is Maimonides’ in his twelfth-century law code, the Mishneh Torah: a king of David’s line who restores the observance of the Torah and “fights the wars of God” may be presumed to be the Messiah; if he then “builds the Temple in its place, and gathers the dispersed of Israel, he is definitely the Mashiach”; but “if he did not succeed to this degree or was killed, he surely is not the redeemer promised by the Torah.”10 On that test Jesus fails twice over: the tasks are unfinished, and he was killed. The objection is raised sincerely, from the prophets’ own pages, and it must be answered, not waved away.

The answer begins with a question the objection does not ask: in what order do the prophets say these things happen? The tasks are real, but they are only half of the portrait. Read as this essay has read them, the same prophets describe a figure who is rejected and killed before he triumphs. Isaiah’s Servant, on the individual reading defended above, is “despised and rejected by men” (53:3), “poured out his soul to death,” and was “numbered with the transgressors” (53:12) — and after that death “he shall see his offspring; he shall prolong his days,” and God will “divide him a portion with the many” (53:10, 12). Earlier in the same songs the Servant is “one deeply despised, abhorred by the nation,” to whom God promises, “Kings shall see and arise” (49:7). Zechariah’s king comes “humble and mounted on a donkey” (9:9) before “he shall speak peace to the nations” and rule “from sea to sea” (9:10); the same prophet has Jerusalem mourning over “him whom they have pierced” (12:10). And Daniel’s “anointed one shall be cut off and shall have nothing” (9:26), whatever one concludes about that passage’s disputed chronology. The prophets do not describe a Messiah who arrives and finishes the work in one unbroken stroke. They put rejection and suffering first and the reign of peace after. A test that rules out any claimant who “was killed” is therefore stricter than the prophets themselves: it excludes in advance the very sequence Isaiah 53 describes.

Nor have Christians alone seen a death in the messianic portrait. Later tradition knew of a suffering “Messiah son of Joseph.” The Babylonian Talmud — whose redaction ran into the sixth century AD, so it is not evidence of what Jews expected in Jesus’ day — records a dispute over the mourning of Zechariah 12:10, and one view holds that it is for “Messiah ben Yosef who was killed,” whose death, a later passage on the same page implies, comes before Messiah ben David is revealed (the other view refers the mourning to the evil inclination).11 Two cautions keep this honest. The Talmud divides the portrait between two different men, where Christians see one person in two comings; and nothing in the passage presents his death as an offering for sin. So this tradition does not concede the Christian reading. What it does show is that rabbinic readers of the same prophets could find in them a messianic figure who is killed before the final redemption arrives.

That two-stage shape is exactly what the New Testament claims, and it is the claim of the apostles’ preaching as Acts records it, not a late repair. Peter tells a Jerusalem crowd that Jesus is the Christ “whom heaven must receive until the time for restoring all the things about which God spoke by the mouth of his holy prophets long ago” (Acts 3:21). Hebrews says that Christ, “having been offered once to bear the sins of many, will appear a second time, not to deal with sin but to save those who are eagerly waiting for him” (Hebrews 9:28). When the disciples asked the risen Jesus about restoring the kingdom to Israel, he answered that the times were the Father’s to fix (Acts 1:6–7). So the peace of Isaiah 2 and an earth full of the knowledge of the LORD are, on the Christian claim, not cancelled but still to come, at his return. Christians differ among themselves over how some of these promises will be fulfilled — the ingathering, the land and the Temple above all — and that end-times question is not settled here; what they share is the confession that the kingdom the prophets promised was inaugurated at his first coming and will be completed at his second.

And the work that belongs to the first, suffering stage was done. The Servant is to bear “the sin of many” (Isaiah 53:12) — a claim received by faith, and the one Hebrews 9:28 echoes. But his commission also reaches past Israel in a way anyone can check: “It is too light a thing that you should be my servant to raise up the tribes of Jacob … I will make you as a light for the nations, that my salvation may reach to the end of the earth” (Isaiah 49:6), words Paul and Barnabas applied to their own mission (Acts 13:47). In the very chapter the objection cites, the root of Jesse stands “as a signal for the peoples—of him shall the nations inquire” (Isaiah 11:10), a verse Paul applies to the Gentiles’ hope in Christ (Romans 15:12); and Isaiah 2 itself pictures “all the nations” streaming to learn the ways of “the God of Jacob” (2:2–3). That has begun to happen. Across the nations, people with no Jewish ancestry pray Israel’s Psalms and worship the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob — and it happened through a crucified Jew from Nazareth. The objection’s own classic source grants the fact while rejecting the conclusion. In the full text of the same ruling, long omitted from printed editions by censors, Maimonides names “Jesus of Nazareth who aspired to be the Mashiach and was executed by the court,” and he counts Christianity an error. Yet he writes that through it, and through Islam after it, “the entire world has already become filled with the mention of Mashiach, Torah, and mitzvot”: “These matters have been spread to the furthermost islands to many stubborn-hearted nations.” For Maimonides this is a way the Creator prepares for a Messiah still to come, not a fulfilment, so he is no witness for the Christian claim. He is a witness that knowledge of Israel’s Scriptures and of its hope of a Messiah reached the nations through the movement Jesus began.12 The new covenant of Jeremiah 31, whose promise is “I will forgive their iniquity” (31:34), Jesus declared opened “in my blood” (Luke 22:20); that “they shall all know me” in full awaits the same completion as the rest.

So the objection does not hold. It is right that the Messiah must bring peace and fill the earth with the knowledge of God, and Christians confess that Jesus will. But the objection assumes a one-stage Messiah who does all of it in a single appearance and is never killed — and that is not the Messiah the prophets describe on the reading of Isaiah 53 defended above. Isaiah, Zechariah, and Daniel place rejection and death before triumph; the work tied to that first, suffering stage — bearing sin and bringing Israel’s God to the nations — is the work Jesus did; and what remains is what the New Testament says his return will bring. This answer does not by itself prove that Jesus is the Messiah; that rests on the cumulative case this essay lays out. What it removes is the disqualification: “he has not yet finished” is no evidence that he is not the one, when the prophets themselves describe a Messiah who suffers first and reigns after. The case should be made to Jewish friends “with gentleness and respect” (1 Peter 3:15), never as a triumph over them: “where is the peace?” is a question their own prophets taught them to ask, and the Christian answer is that it comes with the Messiah who has already come once.

4. The statistical (Stoner) argument

Here is the reframe this essay was built around. In Science Speaks (first published in 1944 as From Science to Souls, retitled in 1952, and revised across later editions), mathematician Peter Stoner argued that the odds of one man fulfilling just eight prophecies were one in 1017 — a figure he reached by taking a raw product of roughly 1028 and dividing by the estimated number of people who have ever lived; he put forty-eight prophecies at 1 in 10157. The image is vivid — Texas knee-deep in silver dollars, one of them marked — and it has circulated for more than seventy years.

It should be retired. The probabilities are not real measurements; they are numbers assigned by guesswork (Stoner puts the chance of being born in Bethlehem at 1 in 280,000, and so on), then multiplied as if independent. A reviewer in The Quarterly Review of Biology charged Stoner with "using only these facts which bolster his hypothesis"; even Stoner's own grandson now concedes the book is of more historical than evangelistic value.13 The method is pseudo-mathematics: precise-looking figures resting on arbitrary inputs. The case for the prophecies does not rest on it, and never needed it. Convergence, not a manufactured probability, is the real argument.

What the Argument Does — and Does Not — Establish

So what is left when the weak links are removed and the contested texts are flagged as contested? A great deal, but stated precisely.

What the prophetic argument does establish is a striking cumulative convergence: a single first-century figure who fits a constellation of expectations — birthplace, lineage, a rejected and suffering deliverer, a death among criminals and a rich man's burial, a vindication beyond the grave — drawn from documents provably older than he was. The most evidentially weighty of these lie outside any human power to arrange. That convergence is not nothing; it is, in the proper sense, remarkable, and it is why the apostles preached it.

What the argument does not establish is a single, knock-down, mathematical proof. It is a cumulative, probabilistic case, the way a verdict is built from many testimonies rather than one. Some "fulfillments" are typological, not predictive. Several key texts — Isaiah 7:14, Psalm 22:16, the dating in Daniel 9, the referent of Isaiah 53 — are genuinely disputed among honest scholars, and a reader is entitled to know that before being asked to weigh them.

The prophets, then, do not hand us a proof to end argument; they hand us a portrait, sketched across a thousand years by many hands, that comes startlingly into focus on one face. That focus is not coincidence but providence: Jesus of Nazareth is the one the prophets were pointing to. This is a cumulative case, not a proof; it rests on the texts’ pre-Christian date and on the independently anchored facts of his death, and it is a strong case, to be made “with gentleness and respect” (1 Peter 3:15).

Frequently asked questions

Didn't the Gospel writers just invent fulfillments to match Old Testament texts?

For prophecies that could be staged, like riding a donkey into Jerusalem, this must be conceded. The reply leans on prophecies outside anyone's control: birthplace, tribe and lineage, and the manner and timing of a death under Roman power. A first-century Jew could not arrange to be born in Bethlehem of David's line, nor engineer a Roman crucifixion. Nor is the central fact written in to fit: the crucifixion under Pilate is reported by Tacitus and Josephus, and a crucified messiah — a curse under Deuteronomy 21:23, a 'stumbling block' in Paul's words — is not a story anyone invents to win converts. And the texts are demonstrably pre-Christian: the Great Isaiah Scroll is dated to roughly 125 BC.

Was the virgin birth prophecy in Isaiah 7:14 a mistranslation of 'young woman'?

The essay is candid here. The Hebrew almah means a young woman of marriageable age and does not by itself specify virginity; the Septuagint, translated by Jews before Christ, rendered it parthenos, virgin, and that is what Matthew quotes. The verse's immediate context is a sign to King Ahaz in his own day. Many read it as having a near-term referent plus a fuller messianic sense, a contested, largely typological case, not a knockdown prediction.

Aren't the odds of Jesus fulfilling prophecy proof he's the Messiah?

The essay actually retires that statistical argument. Peter Stoner's famous figures are not real measurements but numbers assigned by guesswork, then multiplied as if independent; a reviewer charged him with using only facts that bolstered his case. The prophetic argument does not rest on it and never needed it. The real case is convergence: a single figure fitting a constellation of expectations from documents provably older than he was. It is a cumulative case, not a mathematical proof.

Jesus didn’t bring world peace or rebuild the Temple, so doesn’t that rule him out as the Messiah?

No. That test assumes a Messiah who completes every task in a single appearance, and on the Christian reading of Isaiah 53 the prophets do not describe one. Isaiah’s Servant is despised, rejected and ‘poured out his soul to death’ before he is exalted (Isaiah 53); Zechariah’s humble king on a donkey comes before he ‘shall speak peace to the nations’ (Zechariah 9:9–10); Daniel’s anointed one is ‘cut off’ (Daniel 9:26). The New Testament claims two comings: at his first, Jesus bore sin and became a ‘light for the nations’ (Isaiah 49:6), as the worldwide worship of Israel’s God among Gentiles shows; the peace and the universal knowledge of God the prophets promise come at his return (Acts 3:21). That does not by itself prove he is the Messiah, but ‘he has not yet finished’ does not disqualify him.
