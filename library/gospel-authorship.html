Deep Dive Essay

Who Wrote the Gospels? Were They Really Anonymous? The Early Evidence for the Four Names

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

Open a Bible to the second book of the New Testament and the heading reads, in some form, “The Gospel according to Mark.” Then a documentary, a textbook, or a well-read friend says that those names were added later and that nobody really knows who wrote the Gospels. For many readers that sentence lands like a demolition. If the books are anonymous, how can anyone say they go back to the people who were there?

The question deserves a careful answer, because the two easy answers are both wrong. The Evangelists did not sign their books, and the evidence does not show the names being invented generations later. The truth sits between those claims, and it is stronger than many readers have been told. This essay asks the authorship question directly: what does “anonymous” mean here, who does the earliest evidence say wrote each Gospel, and how much weight can that evidence bear? A companion essay, Were the Gospels Based on Eyewitnesses?, takes up a different question, whether the Gospels contain eyewitness testimony, from the named minor characters to the fit of their personal names to first-century Palestine. Those arguments are not repeated here.

Stating the Claim Carefully

Begin with what this essay does not claim. No Evangelist names himself within the body of his text, and the familiar titles—“The Gospel according to Matthew,” and so on—may well, in their present form, be later than the books themselves. So the claim being defended here is not that each Evangelist signed his work, still less that we can prove a particular apostle held the pen. The claim is narrower. The names attached to the four Gospels are attested comparatively early (Mark’s and Matthew’s by Papias, John’s before or alongside Irenaeus, Luke’s by the end of the second century), consistently, and with no rival tradition of authorship, and on that evidence the case that the attributions are early, and probably original, is strong and live.

It is not a consensus, and that should be said at the outset: most scholars still regard the Gospels as originally anonymous. Nor would a secure attribution settle everything. An early attribution is not proof of authorship, and authorship is not truth. Who wrote a book is one question; whether its reports are accurate is a second; whether its central claims are true is a third. This essay is about the first.

The argument can be put as a simple structure. (1) When an ancient work does not name its author in its text, its author was normally identified by the label it carried and by external testimony; and when the testimony to a name is comparatively early, consistent, and unrivalled, the name probably travelled with the work from the start, though other explanations remain possible. (2) The names of the four Evangelists are attested comparatively early, consistently, and with no rival. (3) Therefore the traditional attributions are probably original, and deserve to be weighed as such, not dismissed as late guesses. The logic is valid; the contest is over the premises: whether the names really are early and unrivalled, and whether that pattern is better explained by names that were there from the start than by names fixed later.

What “Anonymous” Means—and What It Does Not

Two facts usually stand behind the word, and both should be granted. First, no Evangelist names himself in his text. Second, the familiar titles (“The Gospel according to Matthew,” and so on) may well, in their present form, be later than the books themselves. The Cambridge New Testament scholar Simon Gathercole, who has recently made a detailed case against the majority view, grants both facts; what he disputes is their significance.1 Neither observation is the issue. The question is what follows from them.

Take the first fact. The fact is granted, but the inference usually drawn from it is weaker than it looks. Most ancient biographies and many ancient histories did not name their authors in the text either; Plutarch’s Lives and Philostratus’s Life of Apollonius are examples, as are Livy’s and Sallust’s histories, because an author’s name normally travelled with the title on the copy rather than inside the prose.2 Plutarch does not name himself in the Lives, any more than the Evangelists name themselves in the Gospels. Plutarch’s authorship is also secured in other ways—by his many other writings, and by personal asides in the Lives about his own family and town—so for the Gospels the external testimony carries more of the weight. But the comparison shows that an unsigned ancient book was not, for that reason, an authorless one. Its author was identified by the material around the text, such as a title at the head or the end of the roll or a label attached to it, rather than by a signature inside it. That is why Gathercole argues that calling a work anonymous because the author’s name is absent from its body mistakes the text for the label that identified it.

Now the second fact. The full titles in the form we know—“The Gospel according to…”—probably took that shape in the second century. The German scholar Martin Hengel argued that titles were attached early, when the books first had to be identified, but his case for the originality of the titles has won few followers, as Gathercole himself notes.3 The question that matters, though, is not when the formula took its present shape. It is whether the name Mark was attached to that book from early on. A title in a manuscript is one witness to that, but our manuscripts begin after a gap of one to several generations from the autographs, and so this essay leans instead on the writers who name the Evangelists.

The Witnesses, Gospel by Gospel

What follows sets out, for each Gospel, the earliest surviving sources that name its author and how far each one reaches. One caution governs all of it. The testimony is early, named, and widespread—from Asia Minor, Antioch, and Gaul, and from a Gnostic teacher the church opposed as well as from its own bishops—though not all of it is demonstrably independent of Papias, and later writers may repeat earlier ones.

Mark

The earliest testimony comes from Papias of Hierapolis, preserved by the church historian Eusebius; Papias wrote in the first half of the second century (most current proposals run from about AD 95 to about 140). He preserved a tradition he traced to “the Elder”: that “Mark, having become the interpreter of Peter, wrote down accurately, though not in order, whatsoever he remembered of the things said or done by Christ.”4 If Papias is right that he is passing on what the Elder used to say, the link between Mark and Peter is older than his own book. The same report says that Mark “neither heard the Lord nor followed him, but afterward, as I said, he followed Peter.” The tradition presents Mark not as an eyewitness but as the recorder of one.

Around AD 180 Irenaeus of Lyons gives the same account: “Mark, the disciple and interpreter of Peter, did also hand down to us in writing what had been preached by Peter.”5 Early in the third century Tertullian makes the distinction explicit, writing: “Of the apostles, therefore, John and Matthew first instil faith into us; while of apostolic men, Luke and Mark renew it afterwards.”6 Irenaeus may well depend on Papias here, so the weight rests where it began: on Papias’s own report, Mark’s name reaches back behind Papias’s book to the Elder.

Matthew

Papias also reports that “Matthew wrote the oracles in the Hebrew language, and every one interpreted them as he was able.”7 What Papias meant by Matthew’s “oracles” in “the Hebrew language” is disputed—our Matthew is in Greek—so his report about Matthew is less secure than his report about Mark, and the Mark–Peter link does not depend on it. Irenaeus, in the same passage quoted above, repeats the tradition: “Matthew also issued a written Gospel among the Hebrews in their own dialect.” He may be drawing on Papias, so the two are not clearly independent. The honest summary for Matthew is an early name, recorded by the same writer who gives us Mark, with a real puzzle about exactly what that writer was describing.

Luke

Here the evidence is thinnest, and it must be stated plainly. No surviving writer names Luke as the author of the third Gospel before Irenaeus and the Muratorian Fragment, late in the second century. Irenaeus, in the same passage, writes that “Luke also, the companion of Paul, recorded in a book the Gospel preached by him.” The Muratorian Fragment, an anonymous list that most scholars date to roughly AD 170–200 (a minority place it in the fourth century), says, in Bruce Metzger’s translation, “The third book of the Gospel is that according to Luke,” and calls him “the well-known physician.”8

Yet Luke is, on Gathercole’s argument, the Gospel least likely to have been anonymous at its origin, because of how it begins. Luke opens with a formal preface in the first person: many have compiled narratives of these events, and so “it seemed good to me also, having followed all things closely for some time past, to write an orderly account for you, most excellent Theophilus” (Luke 1:3). Its second volume opens the same way: “In the first book, O Theophilus, I have dealt with all that Jesus began to do and teach” (Acts 1:1). And at points Acts shifts into “we,” as the “we” passages (e.g., Acts 16:10–17; 27:1–28:16) present the narrator travelling with Paul.9 If, as the formal address suggests, Theophilus was a real person, then a book written in the first person and dedicated to him was a book whose author was known to at least one reader from the day it was delivered.

Three cautions keep this honest. Whether the “we” passages mean that the author was himself present is debated, and none of this shows that the author’s name was Luke; the name still rests on the later testimony. Nor does a name known to Theophilus guarantee that it travelled with every later copy; that is the gap the softer version of the objection, below, presses. What the preface does show is that the work was not composed as a faceless community product: its author speaks in his own voice, to a named reader, about his own method. Like any such claim, it is evidence of how the author understood his own work, not proof that his account is true.

John

John is the most widely named of the four, and the witnesses come from opposite sides of the second-century church. The Valentinian Gnostic Ptolemy (or a teacher of his school), whom Irenaeus quotes in order to refute and who wrote before him, attributed the Fourth Gospel’s opening words to “John, the disciple of the Lord.”10 Theophilus of Antioch, writing at about the same time as Irenaeus, introduces the Gospel’s first line the same way: “the holy writings teach us, and all the spirit-bearing [inspired] men, one of whom, John, says, In the beginning was the Word.”11 Irenaeus himself, in the passage already quoted, writes that “John, the disciple of the Lord, who also had leaned upon His breast, did himself publish a Gospel during his residence at Ephesus in Asia.” And in the 190s Polycrates, bishop of Ephesus, writing to Victor of Rome, names among the church’s great dead in Asia “John, who was both a witness and a teacher, who reclined upon the bosom of the Lord.”12 That phrase identifies a John with the Beloved Disciple, the one who leaned on Jesus at the supper (John 13:23–25), whom the Gospel’s closing verses identify as the one who bears witness to these things and has written them (John 21:24).

But an early name “John” does not settle which John. Church tradition came to identify him with the apostle John, son of Zebedee, and Irenaeus is generally read as meaning him, though Richard Bauckham disputes this. Bauckham, writing as a historian, argues instead for “John the Elder,” a disciple of Jesus outside the Twelve; others have proposed Lazarus, whom John says Jesus loved, or read the figure as an idealized representative of the community.13 This essay does not settle the question, and its argument does not depend on settling it. The witnesses do not establish which disciple he was; what they do establish is that the Fourth Gospel was tied to a disciple named John before the end of the second century, by writers on both sides of the church’s quarrel with the Gnostics.

Irenaeus Was Not the First

This matters, because a common telling of the story makes Irenaeus the moment the names appear. Irenaeus, around AD 180, names all four Evangelists and ties each name to the opening of its Gospel, but he was not the first after Papias to name one.14 He insists “it is not possible that the Gospels can be either more or fewer in number than they are,” comparing them to the four zones of the world and the four winds—a strange-sounding argument to modern ears, and one aimed at groups he names who used one Gospel or more than four; but it takes the four books and their names as already in the churches’ hands, not as something he is introducing. Irenaeus is a witness to what the churches already held, not the origin of the names.

The Two Silences

Two further considerations come not from what the sources say but from what they never say. Both are arguments from silence, and they should be weighed as such.

The first is that no surviving second-century writer treats the four Gospels as books whose authors were unknown.15 The silence cuts both ways: early writings such as Ignatius’s letters, Polycarp’s letter and the Didache cite Gospel material as what ‘the Lord’ said, or as ‘the Gospel’, without naming any Evangelist; on its own that is compatible with either view. The second is that wherever the surviving sources attribute the Gospels, they attribute them to Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John, with no rival tradition of authorship (the nearest thing to an exception, a disputed report that some opponents of John’s Gospel ascribed it to the heretic Cerinthus, is taken up below). (Two further possible exceptions, Justin Martyr’s way of describing the Gospels and Marcion’s nameless Gospel, bear on the first silence and are also taken up below.)

Uniform names can still be mistaken: the anonymous Epistle of Barnabas acquired a single, uniform attribution that most scholars think mistaken.16

Even so, the agreement sits awkwardly with the idea that the Gospel names were late guesses: Gathercole presses the point that the later the Gospel names are supposed to have been attached, the harder it becomes to explain why the surviving witnesses agree on them, and shared clues in the texts explain some of that agreement more easily than all of it (nothing in Mark’s text points to Mark). The absence of a rival tradition is an argument from silence (a skeptic can reply that the names were fixed once, when the four were first gathered together, which would explain the uniformity without making them original), and most scholars still regard the Gospels as originally anonymous; the question remains open, and Gathercole has made a detailed case that the names were probably attached from the start.17

The Strongest Objections, Stated at Full Strength

The case above has to meet the objections in their best form, and several of them are serious.

1. The names were attached when the four were collected

Most New Testament scholars hold that the Gospels were first issued without names, a view represented by Michael Wolter’s study of the anonymous writings of the New Testament; Armin Baum has argued that this silence is a deliberate stylistic device, modelled on the anonymous historical books of the Old Testament and the ancient Near East rather than on Greco-Roman historiography.18 On the common reconstruction, the titles that now name the Gospels were added later, once the four circulated together and had to be told apart. A softer version, which Bart Ehrman reports as one some have held (without adopting it himself), grants something to the other side: the first readers may have known who wrote these books, but the names were not attached as copies spread, the authors’ identities were soon lost, and later readers linked the four to two of Jesus’ disciples and two companions of apostles.19 Nor need uniform names be original: later guesses can converge when they follow the same clues in the texts (the tax collector called Matthew in Matthew 9:9, Paul’s companion Luke [Colossians 4:14] behind the “we” passages, the disciple whom Jesus loved) or when later writers follow one early authority. And the skeptic has a sharp reply to the silences: the names may have been fixed once, when the four were first gathered together, which would explain the uniformity without making them original. A single editorial decision could account for much of the agreement the previous section relied on.

2. Gospels did circulate without names

A second objection points to evidence that a Gospel could circulate without a name. In the middle of the second century Marcion, who severed Christianity from Judaism entirely, used a single Gospel, and Tertullian reports: “Marcion, on the other hand, you must know, ascribes no author to his Gospel.”20 Justin Martyr, writing at about the same time, describes the Gospels collectively as “the memoirs of the apostles” and, in the passages where he describes them, does not name the individual Evangelists.21 If the names were fixed from the start, why do two of the best-known Christian figures of the mid-second century leave them out?

3. There was a rival after all

A third objection points to a rival attribution. A disputed report found chiefly in the fourth-century Epiphanius says that some opponents of John’s Gospel ascribed it to the heretic Cerinthus.22 If even one early group gave John’s Gospel a different author, the claim that there was no rival tradition is weaker than it sounds.

4. Papias is a fragile foundation

A fourth targets the earliest witness. To build on Papias is to lean on a single second-century figure whom Eusebius himself, centuries on, judged to have been “of very limited understanding.”23 If Papias is unreliable, the oldest link in the Mark chain is weak, and much of the later testimony may simply repeat him. A skeptic can press further: what Papias describes need not be our Gospels. His Matthew is a Hebrew collection of “oracles,” which our Greek Matthew is not, and his Mark is a record of Peter’s recollections written “not in order.”

5. Apostolic names were cheap

The last is the broadest. Early Christians did attach the names of Jesus’ followers to books those followers did not write: the Gospel of Peter and the Gospel of Thomas borrow such names, and the Gospel of Judas trades on another. And Ehrman presses a further point: several canonical books are pseudonymous by the judgment of most critical scholars—the Pastoral Epistles, 2 Peter, perhaps Ephesians—so the church enforced a criterion it could not actually verify and sometimes got wrong.24 Why trust its attributions of the Gospels any more?

The Reply—and What It Can and Cannot Carry

Take the collection hypothesis first, because it is the strongest. The evidence cannot rule it out, but it has costs. On Papias’s own report, at least one of the names—Mark’s—was already being passed on by the Elder before Papias wrote. If so, whoever first gathered the four did not invent Mark’s name but inherited it, unless the gathering came earlier still, which would itself place that name very early. Matthew’s case is less clear, since it is disputed whether Papias’s Matthew is our Greek Gospel; and for Luke and John, whose earliest named witnesses fall in the second half of the second century, Papias does not touch the hypothesis; there its main cost is that a decision made at one moment left no record of anyone saying that the authors were unknown, though that too is an argument from silence. None of this refutes the hypothesis; it makes it more expensive than it first appears. The softer version Ehrman reports meets the same difficulty in a sharper form. If the first readers knew who wrote these books and the knowledge was later lost, the later attributions would be guesses; yet the surviving attributions of the Gospels did not scatter. That the later guesses converged by following shared clues cannot be excluded, but such clues explain some of that agreement more easily than all of it. Nothing in Mark’s text points to Mark, a figure the earliest tradition (Papias) presents as neither an apostle nor an eyewitness (some suggest 1 Peter 5:13, “Mark, my son,” supplied the link; since Papias knew that letter, this cannot be ruled out, but on Papias’s report the link was already in place in the Elder’s time, so any such inference was made early, while the first readers’ knowledge was nearer to hand, which makes a guess replacing a forgotten name harder, though not impossible, to credit).

The Marcion objection rests on a real datum, and it cuts both ways. It shows that a Gospel could circulate without a name in the middle of the second century. But Tertullian, writing some sixty years later, treats the omission as a mark against Marcion, not as normal practice, and Irenaeus, writing around 180, reports that Marcion “mutilates the Gospel which is according to Luke.” Some scholars contest that account; Joseph Tyson’s reconstruction rests on a contested premise—that Marcion used an earlier form of Luke which our Gospel revised. On the ancient reports, however, the case is one of a single radical editor who, as Tertullian tells it, would not put a name to a Gospel the church called Luke’s; it is not evidence of a church that never had one, though reports written a generation or more later cannot by themselves show that the name was already attached in Marcion’s day.25 As for Justin, his description of the memoirs as “drawn up by His apostles and those who followed them” does not name the Evangelists, but it is consistent with the later attributions, which credit two Gospels to apostles and two to their followers.26 Justin’s silence is weak evidence in itself: in his surviving works he never names Paul either; and in one passage (Dialogue 106) he cites a detail found only in Mark, the sons of Zebedee called “Boanerges” (Mark 3:17), from what he calls “his memoirs,” often read as Peter’s.

The Cerinthus report, the nearest thing to an exception, describes a polemical rejection of that Gospel, not a rival tradition. Its aim was to discredit the book, not to report who had written it; and even that ascription, to a man Irenaeus makes a contemporary of John, placed the Gospel around the end of the first century. It is also uncertain in itself: T. Scott Manor, in a specialist study of these opponents, questions both the second-century dating of that opposition and the authenticity of the Cerinthus attribution.27

As for Papias, Eusebius’s disdain was aimed chiefly at his millenarian eschatology rather than at his reliability about Mark, and what Papias passes on is not his own speculation but what he had from “the Elder”—so, on Papias’s own report, the link between Mark and Peter is older than his writing. The further objection, that Papias need not be describing our Gospels, has already been granted for Matthew. For Mark it is weaker: “not in order” is often read as a defence of a book whose arrangement had been criticised, which fits a Gospel rather than counting against one, and many scholars take Papias to be describing our Mark or something very close to it. Nor is Papias the only early witness to the Gospels’ names, though he is the earliest for Mark. And the charge that the names stand on Papias alone is weakest for John, who is named as the Gospel’s author by a Gnostic teacher and a bishop of Antioch as well as by Irenaeus, while a bishop of Ephesus names a John as the disciple who reclined on the Lord’s breast.

The pseudonymity objection is answered in part by chronology. The Gospel of Thomas as we have it, the Gospel of Peter, the Gospel of Judas, and the rest are second-century or later compositions, written generations after the apostles whose names they borrow. And the church tested such books rather than simply accepting the names they bore. Around AD 200 Serapion, bishop of Antioch, who had at first let the Gospel of Peter be read at Rhossus without examining it, later obtained and read it and stated the principle he was applying: “we receive both Peter and the other apostles as Christ; but we reject intelligently the writings falsely ascribed to them, knowing that such were not handed down to us.”28 The existence of forgeries shows that a name could be borrowed; it does not show that every name was, and Serapion’s verdict shows that the church knew the difference mattered. On the disputed canonical letters, it must be acknowledged that the church’s judgments about authorship were made with the limited tools of antiquity and are regarded as insecure by many modern critics—though the critics’ verdicts are themselves contested by other scholars, many of them evangelical and some Catholic.29 The case for the Gospel names does not rest on the church’s having judged every attribution rightly. It rests on how early, how consistent, and how unrivalled these particular attributions are, and that is evidence any historian can weigh.

Here, for a moment, the last word should go to the critic. Ehrman would say: even granting all of this, you have shown that the church believed early that these men wrote these books, not that they did; early belief can be early error, and a uniform tradition can be uniformly wrong. Part of that is simply true, and it cannot be waved away: an early attribution is not proof of authorship. That is fair, and nothing above claims more. What it does not show is that early, uniform testimony counts for little: by the ordinary standards for assigning ancient works to their authors this is comparatively early and named testimony; for many classical works the formal evidence of authorship is later and thinner.30

The Honest State of Scholarship

The debate is live, and it should be described accurately. Most New Testament scholars hold that the Gospels were published anonymously; Gathercole himself documents that majority, and it is represented in the specialist literature by Wolter, and, as a deliberate stylistic choice modelled on Old Testament historiography, by Baum. Against it stands a minority whose case has grown more detailed: Hengel’s argument that the titles were attached early; Richard Bauckham’s historical argument that Mark stands very close to Peter and that John’s Gospel was written by a disciple of Jesus; and Gathercole’s 2018 study, which argues that the names are probably original while stressing that this does not by itself show who wrote the books.31 The dissenters have not won the field, and a defender should not pretend otherwise. In this essay’s judgment, what they have done is sharpen the question. The live issue is not whether a missing name in the text proves anonymity, since the ancient comparisons tell against that, but whether the uniform early names are best explained as original, or as fixed later, whether by a single decision when the four were first gathered or by later guesses that converged.

Conclusion: What This Does and Does Not Establish

What can be said with reasonable confidence is this. The evidence does not fit the picture of the names as late, scattered guesses. Mark and Matthew are named by Papias in the first half of the second century, and on Papias’s own report Mark’s link with Peter reaches back to the Elder before him. John is named, before or alongside Irenaeus, by a Gnostic teacher the church opposed and by a bishop of Antioch, and soon after a bishop of Ephesus names a John as the Beloved Disciple. Luke is named late, but his is the Gospel least likely ever to have been authorless to its first reader. No rival set of names appears. On that evidence, the case that the names were attached early, and probably from the start, is strong and live, though not settled. In the popular sense of ‘anonymous’—that nobody in the early church knew or agreed who wrote them—the description does not fit the evidence. In the scholarly sense—that the books were issued without names which were attached later—the question remains open.

What it does not establish is just as important to say. It does not prove that the four men named wrote the books that bear their names; an early attribution is not proof of authorship; nor does it answer the internal objections to the traditional authors, such as Matthew’s use of Mark, which bear on whether the names are right rather than on how early they are. It does not settle which John stands behind the Fourth Gospel, or whether Papias’s Matthew is our Greek Matthew. It does not turn the minority view into the majority one: most scholars still regard the Gospels as originally anonymous, and the question remains open. And it does not, by itself, show that what the Gospels report is accurate, still less that their central claims are true. Those are further arguments, taken up elsewhere in this Library: whether the Gospels rest on eyewitness testimony, how early they were written, and, decisively, whether Jesus rose from the dead. What this evidence earns is narrower and still real: the four names deserve to be weighed as serious historical testimony, not waved away as late inventions.

Frequently asked questions

Were the Gospels written anonymously?

In one sense, yes: no Evangelist names himself in his text, and the full titles are probably later in their present form. But that does not mean nobody knew who wrote them. Most ancient biographies did not name their authors in the text either. The names are attested comparatively early: Papias names Mark and Matthew in the first half of the second century, and John is named before or alongside Irenaeus by a Gnostic teacher and by Theophilus of Antioch. Luke is not named before about AD 180. No rival tradition of authorship appears in the surviving sources, though uniform names can still be mistaken. That is an argument from silence, and most scholars still regard the Gospels as originally anonymous, so the question is not settled; but the case that the names were attached early, and probably from the start, is strong.

Didn't Irenaeus invent the Gospel names around AD 180?

No. Irenaeus names all four Evangelists around AD 180, but he was not the first. Papias, writing in the first half of the second century, names Mark and Matthew, and on his own report he was passing on what an earlier figure, the Elder, said about Mark and Peter. The Valentinian teacher Ptolemy (or a teacher of his school), whom Irenaeus quotes in order to refute, and Theophilus of Antioch both attribute the opening of the Fourth Gospel to John. Irenaeus is a witness to what the churches already held, not the origin of the names.

If we know who wrote the Gospels, does that prove they are true?

No. An early attribution is not proof of authorship, and authorship is not truth. Who wrote a book is one question; whether its reports are accurate is a second; whether its central claims are true is a third. The early names, with no rival (an argument from silence), earn the Gospels a serious hearing as testimony. Whether that testimony is accurate, and whether Jesus rose from the dead, are argued on their own evidence.
