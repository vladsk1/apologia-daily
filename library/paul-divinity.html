Did Paul Invent Jesus's Divinity?

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

It is one of the most repeated moves in the popular case against Christianity: the divine Christ, it is said, is not the Jesus who taught in Galilee but a figure the apostle Paul built. On this telling a Jewish teacher was executed, his followers remembered him, and then Paul — an educated Pharisee who never walked with the earthly Jesus, at home in the Greek-speaking world — turned the man into a pre-existent, worshipped, cosmic Lord. Christianity, the slogan runs, is the religion about Jesus that Paul founded, not the religion of Jesus.

There is a great deal to test in that claim; this essay tests the narrow, load-bearing piece of it. Did Paul invent the confession of Jesus’s divinity? The answer the evidence returns is no — and it is Paul himself, more than anyone, who rules it out. He tells his readers, in so many words, that he received the confession rather than composed it. He never sets out to argue for Christ’s divine status; he everywhere assumes it, which is a stronger kind of evidence. When he reaches for language to describe Jesus, he reaches for the Old Testament’s own words for Yahweh. And he had been, by his own unembarrassed admission, the confession’s violent persecutor before he was its apostle. A man does not invent, at the cost of everything he had, the very belief he was travelling to destroy.

The claim, stated fairly

The idea has a serious scholarly ancestor. A century ago the history-of-religions school argued that a “high” view of Jesus — pre-existent, divine, hailed as Kyrios (“Lord”) — could not have arisen among monotheistic Jews in Palestine; it must have drifted upward later, as the church moved out into the Greek world and absorbed the vocabulary of the pagan mystery cults and their dying-and-rising lords.1 Its popular descendant today is the developmental thesis associated with Bart Ehrman: the earliest layers show an exalted human being, and full-blown incarnation is a later development, cresting in John, the latest Gospel.2 On either version Paul stands near the head of the trajectory — the earliest and most influential engine of the change.

Grant what is true in this, because a fair reply concedes the facts and refuses only the inference. The oldest datable Christian documents we possess are the letters of Paul, generally dated to the 50s of the first century — earlier than any of the Gospels. Paul did not walk with the earthly Jesus. And his Christology really is very high. None of that is in dispute. The question is whether Paul originated that Christology, or inherited and expressed it.

He says he received it, not invented it

Begin where Paul is most explicit that he is not the author. Around the year 55, in a letter to a quarrelsome church in Corinth, Paul paused to remind his readers of something he insists he did not invent. “For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received,” he writes, “that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures, and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve” (1 Corinthians 15:3–5).3 The verbs are technical. Paul “delivered” (Greek paredōka) what he “received” (parelabon) — the precise idiom used in Judaism for the transmission of authoritative tradition, the equivalent of the rabbinic masar and qibbel.4 He is not narrating his own experience; he is handing on something handed to him.

And the tradition he hands on is old. Reckoning backward from the fixed points of Paul’s biography — his conversion within a few years of the crucifixion, his visit to Cephas and James in Jerusalem — the formula must predate Paul’s own receipt of it, which pushes its origin into the first half of the 30s, within roughly two to five years of the crucifixion.5 This is not an apologetic estimate; it is held across the spectrum. The atheist New Testament scholar Gerd Lüdemann judged that “the elements in the tradition are to be dated to the first two years after the crucifixion… not later than three years”;6 the critical historian James Dunn writes that the formula “was formulated as tradition within months of Jesus’ death.”7 As N. T. Wright argues, this primitive tradition of the mid-30s reaches behind all our written Gospels to the foundation of the church’s faith.8 The full case for the date is laid out in our companion essay on the early creed of 1 Corinthians 15. What matters here is the posture: the confession Paul carries is one he tells us he inherited from those who were witnesses before him.

He assumes the divine confession — he never argues for it

The received creed of 1 Corinthians 15 is about the resurrection. Turn to the divinity itself, and the same pattern holds, more tellingly still. Across some fifteen years of letters written to scattered churches to settle other questions — a collection here, a quarrel there, a word of comfort — Paul never sets out to prove that Christ is divine. He everywhere assumes it, and a thing presupposed offhandedly by a writer making some other point was not up for debate. In Colossians he can call Christ “the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation,” the one by whom “all things were created,” and say flatly that “in him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily”9 — not as a thesis to be defended but as common ground from which to correct a local error. (Colossians’ own authorship is debated; but the same presupposing-not-arguing pattern runs through the undisputed letters — Philippians, 1 Corinthians, Romans — so the observation does not stand or fall with it.) This is Gordon Fee’s central exegetical observation about Paul: the startlingly high Christology is everywhere presupposed, never argued.10

And the pattern reaches behind Paul’s own letters. In Philippians he quotes what most scholars regard as a pre-Pauline hymn — material older than the letter itself — describing one who was “in the form of God” and did not count “equality with God” a thing to be grasped, and who is finally given “the name that is above every name.”11 (The pre-Pauline dating is itself debated — Gordon Fee argues Paul composed the hymn — but the point stands either way: composed or quoted, the conviction it expresses is not one Paul argues into being.) Earlier still, Paul preserves an Aramaic prayer left untranslated in his Greek-speaking churches: Marana tha, “Our Lord, come!”12 That the invocation survives in Aramaic is the point. It carries us back behind Paul’s Greek to the Aramaic-speaking Judean church — the very first community, already calling on the risen Jesus as the Lord who will come. The highest devotion is not a late overlay Paul introduced; it is bedrock beneath him.

He didn’t invent a new god — he put Jesus inside Israel’s God

Here the derivation-from-paganism thesis meets its hardest problem, and it is a problem of direction. When Paul reaches for language to describe the exalted Jesus, he does not reach outward to the mystery cults; he reaches into the Old Testament’s most jealously monotheistic texts — and applies to Jesus the words reserved for Yahweh alone. The climax of the Philippians hymn, “every knee should bow… every tongue confess,” is lifted from Isaiah 45, where the LORD swears that to him alone every knee shall bow — one of the most fiercely monotheistic passages in all of Scripture — and Paul applies it to Jesus. In Romans 10, having written that “if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord… you will be saved,” he clinches the point by quoting Joel — “everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved” — a passage in which the Lord one calls upon is Yahweh himself.13

This is what the British scholar Richard Bauckham calls including Jesus within the unique identity of the one God of Israel: Jesus is placed on the Creator’s side of the line that Jewish monotheism drew between God and everything else.14 And it is the opposite of a pagan borrowing. A man importing a second god from the mystery religions does not reach for the chapter of Isaiah that most insists there is no god beside the LORD. The matrix is Jewish monotheism, not Hellenistic syncretism — which is why the older derivation-from-paganism model has largely collapsed under closer study. As Martin Hengel argued, more happened in Christology in the few years between the crucifixion and Paul’s letters than in the seven centuries of doctrinal development that followed.15 The timeline is too fast, and the vocabulary too Jewish, for a slow drift out of paganism.

Exhibit A is Paul himself

There is one witness the inventor thesis has to explain, and it is the supposed inventor. Before he was the apostle Paul, he was Saul of Tarsus, and he was the confession’s enemy. “I persecuted the church of God violently and tried to destroy it,” he reminds the Galatians; to the Philippians he lists his credentials with a kind of bitter pride: “as to zeal, a persecutor of the church” (Galatians 1:13; Philippians 3:6).16 This is not a hostile source describing Paul; it is Paul describing himself, in letters whose authenticity even the most skeptical critics accept. And Saul was no confused bystander. He grasped the claim that a crucified man was Israel’s Messiah, and he found it a scandal — for a hanged man was under God’s curse (Deuteronomy 21:23), and a crucified Messiah was a contradiction in terms to a first-century Pharisee.17

Then something reversed him. A movement’s enemies do not usually defect to it, at ruinous personal cost, on the strength of nothing. And the reversal is fatal to the thesis that Paul is the inventor, because it puts the causation backwards. You do not invent, at the cost of your standing and your safety and your inherited convictions, the very belief you had been trying to stamp out — a belief whose divine shape, as the Aramaic Marana tha and the hymn behind Philippians show, already reached back behind him. Whatever happened on the Damascus road, Paul did not build the confession of the risen, divine Christ. He joined it — and then went, in person, to check the story with the men who had started it before him.

The reply, and the concession

Honesty about scope keeps the argument sound, so state the concessions plainly. The developed creedal vocabulary came laterWhat “developed later” meansIs sayingThe technical creedal vocabulary and systematic formulation (Nicaea’s “one essence,” Chalcedon’s “two natures”) came later, as the church’s articulation of what was already in the texts.Not sayingNot that the deity of Christ was a later human invention. The data — Jesus sharing God’s throne, name, and worship — is present from the earliest layers we can reach.: the fourth- and fifth-century language of “one essence” and “two natures” is a real achievement, the church’s articulation of what was already in the texts, not a first-century phrase. Paul is, indeed, our earliest extensive Christian writer. And this essay does not, by itself, prove that the confession Paul received is true; that Paul handed on the belief that Jesus is the risen Lord establishes that the claim was made — at the movement’s foundation, by a formerly hostile witness — not that the claim is correct. Whether it is correct is the work of the resurrection evidence and of Jesus’s own words, argued elsewhere in this library.

What the evidence does close off is the specific charge that Paul invented the divinity. The developmental thesis faces the harder problem: it must explain why our earliest reachable material — the pre-Pauline creed, the Aramaic Marana tha, the Philippians hymn, the reshaped Shema of 1 Corinthians 8:6 — already presupposes a Christ included in God’s identity and worship, on the divine-identity reading of Bauckham and Hurtado, rather than arriving there late. Bauckham sharpens the point: the earliest Christians did not slowly climb a ladder from a low Christology to a high one; they expressed the highest possible claim — inclusion in the divine identity — from the start, using the very resources of Jewish monotheism.18 The words for it were refined over centuries; the reality is bedrock, and it is there in the earliest layer we can reach. Paul stands inside that layer, not above it.

Conclusion

So the honest answer to the question is no. Paul did not invent Jesus’s divinity. He is the single most important early witness to it — but a witness is precisely what he is: a man who received a confession, assumed it rather than argued it, clothed it in Israel’s own language for God, and embraced it only after it had overturned everything he believed. The one sentence that frames his whole testimony is the one he wrote to Corinth: “I delivered to you… what I also received.” The divine Christ was not Paul’s invention. He was the faith Paul was given — and had once tried to destroy.

Frequently asked questions

Didn’t Paul invent the idea that Jesus is God?

No. Paul is our earliest extensive Christian writer, but he tells his readers he received the core confession rather than composing it: “I delivered to you… what I also received” (1 Corinthians 15:3). He never argues that Christ is divine; he everywhere assumes it, even quoting an early hymn (Philippians 2) and the Aramaic prayer Marana tha — the latter reaching back to the first Aramaic-speaking Judean church. And when he describes the exalted Jesus he applies the Old Testament’s own texts for Yahweh (Isaiah 45; Joel 2:32). He expresses the confession; he did not originate it.

Isn’t Paul’s “Lord” borrowed from pagan mystery religions?

That was the history-of-religions thesis of a century ago, and it has largely collapsed. A monotheistic Jew importing a second god from paganism does not reach for the most fiercely monotheistic chapter in Isaiah and apply it to that god — but that is exactly what Paul does with Isaiah 45 in Philippians 2. The matrix of his Christology is Jewish monotheism, not Greek syncretism, and the timeline is too fast for a slow drift: as Martin Hengel put it, more happened in Christology in the years between the crucifixion and Paul’s letters than in the seven centuries that followed.

Does this prove the confession is true?

No, and it is not meant to. This essay establishes the narrower point that Paul received rather than invented the divine confession — that it is early, pre-Pauline, and rooted in the first Jewish believers. Whether that confession is true is a further question, argued on separate grounds: the resurrection evidence and Jesus’s own claims, treated in their own essays. That Paul handed on the belief shows the claim was made at the movement’s foundation by a formerly hostile witness, not that the claim is correct.
