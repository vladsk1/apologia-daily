Where Did the Idea of Progress Come From?

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

“The arc of the moral universe is long, but it bends toward justice.” The line was Martin Luther King Jr.’s favourite, and he reached for it again and again—on picket lines, in sermons, in the last speech of his life. It has since been woven into an Oval Office rug and quoted by presidents. Most people who hear it nod, because it says out loud something they already feel in their bones: that history is going somewhere, that the future can be better than the past, that cruelties once thought normal can be named as evils and left behind. We assume that time has a direction, and that the direction is up.

Here is the surprising thing. That assumption is not a human universal. It is not what most people who have ever lived believed. And the man who first shaped the sentence King made famous knew it was a claim of faith, not of sight. The abolitionist minister Theodore Parker wrote it in 1853, and his original words are more honest than the compression: “I do not pretend to understand the moral universe; the arc is a long one… I cannot calculate the curve and complete the figure by the experience of sight; I can divine it by conscience. But from what I see I am sure it bends towards justice.”1 King trimmed the hesitation and kept the hope, quoting it as an adage already in the air.2 This essay is about where that hope came from—and about the fixed point it needs in order to make sense.

The wheel, not the arrow

Step outside the modern West and the confidence that history improves largely evaporates. The oldest and most widespread picture of time is not a line but a circle—or, worse, a slope running downhill.

The Greek poet Hesiod, writing around 700 BC, gave the classic version of the downhill slope. Humanity began in a Golden Age, when people lived like gods, free of toil and sorrow; then came a Silver race, then a Bronze, then the heroes, and finally the Iron race—his own—doomed to labour and grief, and destined to grow worse until the gods abandoned it altogether. The story runs from gold to iron, and the arrow, if there is one, points down.3 Where the golden age lies behind you, the task of the wise is not reform but memory: to recover what has been lost, not to build what has never been.

Others saw not a slope but a wheel. The Stoics taught that the cosmos passes through vast cycles, each ending in a universal fire and beginning again—and that when it begins again it repeats exactly: the same Socrates marries the same wife and is tried by the same accusers, endlessly, without remainder.4 On such a view nothing is ever finally gained or lost, because everything comes round again. The goddess Fortune turned her wheel, lifting the low and casting down the high, and the only wisdom was to expect the turn. There was no destination toward which the whole was moving, because there was no whole that moved. None of this is to say the ancients imagined no improvement at all: Greek and Roman writers could celebrate advances in the arts and sciences, and note that humanity had climbed from rude beginnings. What the dominant pictures lacked was the further idea this essay is tracing—that history as a whole is a story with a plot, morally judged, headed toward a goal it has not yet reached.

This is worth dwelling on, because it is easy to mistake our own outlook for common sense. The idea that a society could look at its own past, judge it wanting, and set out deliberately to be better—that history is a story with a plot, headed toward a goal—is a specific and unusual conviction. Scholars have mapped its pedigree, and they do not all agree. Karl Löwith argued that the modern philosophy of progress is a secularized form of biblical eschatology—Judeo-Christian hope in worldly dress; it is a contested reading, and worth naming as one, for Hans Blumenberg replied that the idea of progress grows from within history, out of the open-ended experience of science and modern self-assertion, not from any borrowed eschatology. Robert Nisbet, unlike Löwith, credited the Greeks and Romans with real ideas of progress of their own. What is not seriously disputed is narrower, and enough for the point here: the biblical narrative gave the West a distinctive and load-bearing picture of time as a line with a destination—a picture the dominant ancient images of the cycle and the decline did not supply.5

The Bible gave history an arrow

What the ancient world lacked, the Hebrew Scriptures supplied: a story that runs in a straight line and is going somewhere. Creation, a call to Abraham, a rescue from slavery in Egypt, a journey to a promised land, prophets who summon the nation back to its covenant, and—stretching over the horizon—a promised kingdom in which the wrongs of history are finally set right. Time is not a wheel and not a decline; it is a road with a destination, walked under the eye of a God who acts within it.

Crucially, that road is judged—measured against a standard that lies ahead of it rather than behind. The prophets do not tell Israel to return to a golden age; they hold up a future in which justice arrives, and indict the present for falling short of it. “But let justice roll down like waters,” thunders Amos, “and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream.”6 Isaiah sees a day when the nations “shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruning hooks,” and learn war no more.7 The law itself builds in the rhythm of reform: every fiftieth year is a Jubilee, when debts are released, slaves freed, and land restored—liberty proclaimed “throughout the land to all its inhabitants.”8 Here is a moral universe with an arc, and the arc bends because Someone is bending it.

This is why King’s cadences sound the way they do. When he told the crowd in Washington that he would not be satisfied “until justice rolls down like waters and righteousness like a mighty stream,” and dreamed of the day when “every valley shall be exalted,” he was not decorating a political speech with religious ornament. He was quoting Amos and Isaiah, preaching the prophets’ own vision of a history moving toward a justice that has not yet arrived.9 The civil-rights movement’s language of the arc was biblical prophecy in a Sunday suit.

The secular borrowing

The remarkable thing is how much of that inheritance survives even in those who have set the faith aside. Consider Steven Pinker, the Harvard psychologist whose Enlightenment Now (2018) is perhaps the most confident recent defence of progress—and who writes as a thoroughgoing atheist and humanist. When he wants to celebrate the dismantling of nuclear weapons, he reaches for the image of swords beaten into plowshares. When he explains the retreat of the death penalty, he writes that it has “made it seem as if there really is a mysterious arc bending toward justice.” When he states the principle behind the century’s moral gains, he puts it as plainly as any liturgy: “life is sacred.”10 A committed secularist, straining to describe human progress, cannot help but talk like a prophet.

There is nothing dishonest in this, and nothing to sneer at. It is simply what happens when you keep the fruit and let go of the root. The modern idea of progress is, to a striking degree, the biblical arrow with the source filed off: the shape of the hope remains, its origin forgotten. But filing off the source leaves a real question exposed, and it is fairer to state it than to score off it. An arc has to bend toward something. Parker could name what: a justice fixed by conscience, which for him meant fixed by God. Pinker offers instead what he calls a more objective measure—the long tables of declining violence and rising health.11 Those tables are real, and this essay does not dispute a line of them. But a measurement of where we have gone cannot, by itself, tell us which direction counts as forward. For that you need a fixed point standing above history to be measured against—a standard the story cannot generate purely from within itself. That the moral law needs such a ground is the argument of a companion essay, and it is not re-run here; the point here is only historical, and narrower: the confidence that history bends toward justice was born believing in that fixed point, and has not obviously found a replacement for it.

Progress without a pole star

That is not merely an academic worry, because the twentieth century tested it. The word “progress” names a movement but not a destination; it says forward without saying toward what—and a century that believed fervently in progress discovered that “forward” can be made to point almost anywhere. Cut loose from any standard above history, the belief that one is riding the tide of the future can curdle into its own justification. The philosopher Isaiah Berlin, in a lecture he delivered in 1953 and published the following year, originally titled “History as an Alibi,” warned precisely against this: the notion that history has a guaranteed direction, he argued, becomes a way of excusing whatever is done in its name, dissolving the moral responsibility of the people who do it.12 When the tide of history is thought to sanctify the deed, the deed no longer has to answer to anything higher than the tide.

What is telling is where the century turned when it recoiled from its own worst chapters. It reached backward for a footing it could no longer supply from within. The framers of the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights did not open by deducing human dignity from progress or from nature; they opened by naming the pit they meant to climb out of—“barbarous acts which have outraged the conscience of mankind.”13 It is one of the most important documents of the modern world, and it works by pointing at the darkness rather than by grounding the light. It states, with magnificent clarity, what we must never do again; it says far less, from its own resources, about why—why this creature carries a dignity that no majority and no future may override.14 Whether a secular account can supply that why from within is the question of a companion essay, not this one; the historical observation here is only that the drafters reached for the language of conscience outraged, not for a derivation from progress or from nature.

What the genealogy shows—and what it doesn’t

Two honest limits keep this argument in its lane. The first is that none of it is evidence that Christianity is true. That a conviction has biblical roots tells you where it came from, not whether the faith that carried it is right about God; a tradition can seed a good idea and still be mistaken in its central claims, and the case for the faith’s truth is made elsewhere in this library, resting above all on the resurrection. The second is that Christians have repeatedly been on the wrong side of the very reforms this hope demands—defending what later Christians, reading the same Scriptures, would fight to abolish. The point being made is genealogical, not congratulatory: the belief that history can bend toward justice is an inheritance, not a self-evident fact, and—this is the whole of it—an inheritance still leaning on the fixed point it was given.

So the closing image is not a boast but an invitation. On his last night, in Memphis in 1968, King preached that he had “been to the mountaintop.” He was standing, deliberately, in the sandals of Moses—the old prophet shown the promised land from the summit of Nebo, who would not himself cross into it.15 It is worth noticing the shape of that hope. The Christian arc is not a smooth upward line; it runs down into suffering before it rises, its clearest emblem a cross before an empty tomb. Progress, on this telling, is not the automatic drift of history but a road that plumbs the depths before it climbs—and that can be walked with confidence only because the destination is secured by the One who set it there. The person who believes the arc bends toward justice is, whether they know it or not, keeping faith with an old and specific hope. The honest next step is not to file the source off, but to go and ask whether the source is real.16

Frequently asked questions

Didn’t every culture believe things get better over time?

No—that belief is unusual, not universal. The oldest and most widespread pictures of time are cyclical or declining. Hesiod described history running downhill from a Golden Age to his own wretched Iron Age; the Stoics taught that the cosmos repeats in endless identical cycles; the wheel of Fortune simply turned. The conviction that history is a story headed toward a goal, and that the future can be deliberately made better than the past, entered the West largely through the biblical narrative—creation, exodus, the prophets, and a promised kingdom—which runs in a line rather than a circle.

Does this prove Christianity is true?

No, and the essay is careful to say so. Showing that the idea of progress has biblical roots is a claim about where the idea came from, not about whether the faith that carried it is true. A tradition can seed a valuable idea and still be wrong about God; and Christians have often been on the wrong side of the reforms this hope demands. The case for the faith’s truth rests elsewhere—above all on the historical evidence for the resurrection. The point here is genealogical: the confidence that history bends toward justice is an inheritance, not a self-evident fact.

Why can’t a secular humanist just believe in progress on their own terms?

They can, and many do—Steven Pinker’s data on declining violence and rising health are real and worth taking seriously. The honest tension is narrower. An arc has to bend toward something, and a record of where we have gone cannot by itself tell us which direction counts as “forward.” For that you need a fixed standard above history to measure against—which is exactly what the idea was born believing in. The observation is not that secular progress is impossible, but that it is living on inherited capital, and owes an account of the fixed point it still assumes.
