How Early Was the New Testament Written? The Case for Closeness to the Events

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

Imagine a rumor trying to spread through a city while the people it is about are still alive to deny it. A story that grows wild in the telling needs two things above all: time, and distance from anyone who was there. Take away the time and the eyewitnesses, and a legend has nowhere to grow. This is the quiet, unglamorous engine behind one of the most important questions in the study of the New Testament: not whether its claims are true—that is a separate fight—but how close its documents stand to the events they report.

It is a question that cuts both ways, and honesty requires saying so at the outset. If the Gospels were composed a century or two after Jesus, by people far removed from Galilee, then a critic’s suspicion that we are reading developed legend would at least have room to work. But if the central claims were already circulating, in fixed verbal form, within a few years of the crucifixion—while hostile witnesses in Jerusalem could still object—then the “legend had time to grow” explanation loses much of its grip. The early-dating argument is the case that the second picture is the true one—firmly for the central proclamation, and as a contested but defensible position for the narrative Gospels.

Stating the Argument Carefully

The argument is usually run in three moves. First: a document produced within the lifetime of eyewitnesses is too early to be a fully developed legend that could circulate unchallenged. Second: the core New Testament material falls inside that eyewitness window—and its earliest layers fall startlingly early. Therefore: the New Testament must be assessed not as late mythology but as first-generation testimony.

Two cautions are needed before we go further, because the argument is often overstated. The conclusion is modest: it tells us the material is early, not that it is accurate. And the second premise does not require the most aggressive possible dating of the four Gospels. As we will see, the strongest version of the case rests on the layers that the great majority of scholars—believer and skeptic alike—already grant to be early: Paul’s undisputed letters of the 50s, and the pre-Pauline tradition embedded inside them.

How the Argument Developed

For much of the twentieth century, the regnant view—shaped by the form critics, above all Rudolf Bultmann—treated the Gospels as the deposit of decades of anonymous community storytelling, with the historical Jesus all but unrecoverable beneath the layers. The most dramatic challenge to that consensus came from an unexpected quarter. In 1976 the liberal Anglican bishop John A. T. Robinson—no friend of conservative apologetics, and famous for the radical theology of Honest to God—published Redating the New Testament, in which his fresh examination of the internal evidence led him to the startling conclusion that the entire New Testament may have been written before the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70.1 Robinson’s specific dates remain a minority position, but his deeper point landed: the textbook datings, he argued, rested on a foundation thinner and more circular than their confidence suggested.2 In parallel, work on the pre-Pauline creeds (reaching back to Joachim Jeremias) and, more recently, Richard Bauckham’s case for eyewitness sources behind the Gospels, has pushed the conversation toward closeness rather than distance.

Premise One: An Early Date Starves a Legend

Why should earliness matter at all? The classic statement comes from the Oxford Roman historian A. N. Sherwin-White, who in his 1960–61 Sarum Lectures (published 1963) turned the historian’s eye on the Gospels. Working from how legends actually behave in the ancient sources—he used Herodotus as his test case—Sherwin-White argued that even two full generations was too short an interval for legend to wipe out a solid historical core; the mythologizing tendency, he observed, takes longer than that to triumph over hard fact.3 For the Gospels the interval is shorter still. Where living memory remains, an invented account does not float free; it collides with people who were present.

That collision was not hypothetical. According to Acts, the earliest preaching of the resurrection happened in Jerusalem, weeks after the crucifixion, to crowds that included the very authorities who had engineered Jesus’s death. Claims about an empty tomb or public events could in principle have been falsified on the spot. Early dating keeps that adversarial check in force, which is exactly what a slow-growing legend needs to escape.

The premise should not be oversold, and critics press it. The skeptic Richard Carrier and others note that Sherwin-White’s “two-generation rule” was an observation drawn from a particular body of Greek material, not a demonstrated law of folklore—and that much is accurate; rapid legendary embellishment is not impossible, and the rate at which stories mutate varies enormously.4 The honest reply is that Premise One does not need an iron law. It needs only the comparative point that more time and more distance make undetected legend easier, and that the New Testament window is unusually tight by the standards of ancient history—where our best surviving biographies of Alexander the Great, by Arrian and Plutarch, were written some four centuries after him and are still mined for a historical core.

Premise Two: The New Testament Really Is Early

The Creed Behind 1 Corinthians 15

Here the case finds its firmest ground, and it must lead the way—because the popular objection that “the Gospels were written decades later” quietly assumes the narrative Gospels are the earliest evidence we have. They are not. In 1 Corinthians 15:3–7, writing in the mid-50s, Paul hands on what he himself had “received”: that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures, and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the Twelve, then to more than five hundred, to James, and to all the apostles. The technical language of “delivering” and “receiving” tradition, the non-Pauline vocabulary, the rhythmic structure, and the Aramaic name Cephas all mark this out as a pre-formed oral creed that Paul is quoting, not composing—a formula already circulating before he wrote it down.5

How early? Paul’s own conversion is usually placed within a few years of the crucifixion, and in Galatians 1:18–19 he describes going up to Jerusalem to meet Cephas (Peter) and James—two of the named witnesses in the creed—three years later. The tradition he relays must predate that meeting. This is why the dating crosses ideological lines. The German New Testament scholar Gerd Lüdemann—who emphatically does not believe Jesus rose bodily and explains the appearances as visions—nonetheless concluded that “the elements in the tradition are to be dated to the first two years after the crucifixion… not later than three years.”6 Gary Habermas, who has surveyed the literature most exhaustively, reports that a wide range of scholars across the spectrum who address the question place the formula within the first few years—though his survey should be cited with care, since it is a count of published positions assembled by one researcher rather than a randomized poll.7

That meeting did more than fix a date; it turned Paul into a checkable link back to the eyewitnesses. Three years after his conversion he lodged for a fortnight with Cephas — the first name in the creed — and, as C. H. Dodd famously quipped of those fifteen days, “we may presume they did not spend all the time talking about the weather.”8 Fourteen years later he went up to Jerusalem again and this time deliberately “set before” its leaders — James, Cephas, and John, the reputed “pillars” — the gospel he had been preaching, “in order to make sure I was not running or had not run in vain” (Galatians 2:2). The dispute in view there was about the terms on which Gentiles could be included, not a line-by-line audit of the tradition; so when Paul reports that the leaders “added nothing” to his message and gave him “the right hand of fellowship” (Galatians 2:6, 9), his point is that they required nothing further of him — no circumcision, no law — and recognized his gospel as one with their own. We should not overstate what this shows: in the same letter Paul is at pains to say that the gospel he preached was “not man’s gospel” but came “through a revelation of Jesus Christ” (Galatians 1:11–12), so this is not a report of Paul being taught the tradition by Peter, nor of the eyewitnesses auditing his memory against theirs. What it shows is narrower and still weighty — that the message Paul preached independently was set before the leading men of the Jerusalem church, those who had known Jesus, and was endorsed by them rather than corrected. The line from the events to the creed does not vanish into anonymous hands; it runs through named people who had access to one another and to the events, and who publicly recognized that Paul preached the same gospel they did.

The force of this is considerable, and it reframes the whole debate. Whatever one decides about the date of Mark, the proclamation of Jesus’s death, burial, and resurrection appearances is not a late accretion that grew over generations. It sits at the very foundation, traceable to the believing community in Jerusalem within a handful of years of the events—and naming individuals, some still alive, who could be questioned.

That alone places the load-bearing claims well inside living memory.

A second clue concerns the book of Acts. Luke–Acts is a two-volume work, and Acts ends with remarkable abruptness: Paul under house arrest in Rome, awaiting trial, the gospel preached “with all boldness and without hindrance” (Acts 28:31). What it never narrates is striking. It does not report the martyrdom of Paul (c. AD 64–67), nor of Peter, nor the death of James the brother of Jesus (AD 62, recorded by Josephus9), nor the Neronian persecution, nor—most conspicuous of all—the destruction of Jerusalem and its Temple in AD 70. Luke, who narrates the martyrdom of Stephen at length and records that of James son of Zebedee (Acts 12:2), and who returns again and again to the theme of the Temple, would have had every reason to mention these catastrophes had they already happened. On this reading, Luke wrote Acts in the early 60s, which places his Gospel (and Mark, one of its sources) earlier still. This is the heart of Robinson’s case, and it was pressed in great detail by the classicist Colin Hemer.10

Not every argument for an early Acts is an argument from silence. The New Testament scholar David Seccombe has pressed two further considerations, neither of which turns on what Acts fails to mention. The first is one of political climate: from the outbreak of the Jewish revolt in AD 66, an intense anti-Jewish mood settled over the Roman world, and in that atmosphere the opening chapters of Luke read as dangerous material to own or circulate—a coming deliverer who “has brought down the mighty from their thrones” (Luke 1:52), the promised rescue “from our enemies and from the hand of all who hate us” (1:71), those “waiting for the redemption of Jerusalem” (2:38), a disciple remembered as “Simon who was called the Zealot” (6:15), and a narrative that ends with Paul in chains “because of the hope of Israel” (Acts 28:20). Salvation cast so thoroughly in the terms of Israel’s national hope, Seccombe argues, fits far more naturally before the war than in the long aftermath of Jewish defeat. The second consideration concerns the book’s purpose: Acts devotes whole chapters (10–11 and 15) to arguing that Gentiles may be received without circumcision—a debate whose very shape presupposes a Jewish church in the position of strength, being asked to make room for Gentiles. After 70, with Judea crushed and the church increasingly Gentile, that argument had—on Seccombe’s reading—largely lost its occasion. Both points are contested: scholars who date Luke–Acts later read the same features as compatible with a post-70 setting, and Seccombe himself presents his case as a set of further, cumulative reasons rather than a proof. But unlike the silence of Acts, they point to positive features of the text rather than gaps in it (David Seccombe, “Dating Luke-Acts: Further Arguments for an Early Date,” Tyndale Bulletin 71.2 [2020]: 207–227).

Patristic Testimony

Finally, the early church remembered who wrote the Gospels and traced them to the apostolic circle. The earliest such testimony comes from Papias of Hierapolis (writing c. AD 110–130), preserved by the church historian Eusebius. Papias reports, on the authority of “the Elder,” that Mark became Peter’s “interpreter” and accurately wrote down what Peter recounted of the Lord’s words and deeds, though “not in order,” and that Matthew wrote “the oracles in the Hebrew language.”11 Later writers—Irenaeus around AD 180—name all four Evangelists. This testimony is external corroboration that the church tied its Gospels to eyewitnesses and their associates, not to anonymous late authors.

The Latest Possible Date

Dating can also be approached from the other end: not how early a writing could be, but how late it can be. A document cannot be later than the first author who quotes it, and several Christian writers around the turn of the first and second centuries already treat New Testament writings as known and authoritative. The letter of the Roman church to Corinth known as 1 Clement, commonly dated around AD 95–96, tells its readers, “Take up the epistle of the blessed Paul the Apostle,” and recalls what Paul had written to them about Cephas and Apollos—an unmistakable reference to 1 Corinthians (1:12).12 Ignatius of Antioch, writing on his way to martyrdom (traditionally c. AD 110), asks, “Where is the wise man? Where the disputer?” and so echoes 1 Corinthians 1:20, and when he says that Jesus was baptized by John “in order that all righteousness might be fulfilled by Him,” he repeats wording found only in Matthew 3:15.13 Polycarp of Smyrna, writing to the Philippians, quotes 1 Peter (“who did no sin, neither was guile found in His mouth”), cites 1 Corinthians 6:2 “as Paul teaches,” and reminds the Philippians of the letter Paul had written to them.14

These dates need the same care as everything else in this essay. 1 Clement’s usual date rests on reading its opening reference to “sudden and repeated calamities” as persecution under Domitian; Kirsopp Lake judged it safest to place the letter “between 75 and 110 A.D.,” while Thomas Herron has argued for about AD 70 and Laurence Welborn for a window reaching as late as 140. Timothy Barnes has argued that Ignatius wrote in the 140s rather than under Trajan, and P. N. Harrison that Polycarp’s letter joins a short note of about 110 to a longer letter (chapters 1–12) of the 130s.15 Even on the latest of these proposals, Paul’s letters and 1 Peter were already circulating and being cited as authorities, and Matthew was very probably known, by the first half of the second century. What this shows is a ceiling, not a floor. For these writings it rules out the picture sketched at the start of this essay, composition two centuries after Jesus, and on the usual dates even composition one century after him: they were known within about eighty years of the crucifixion, and even the latest proposals push that only into the 130s and 140s. It does not decide between the mainstream dates and the earlier ones, since even the earliest proposed date for these writers is no earlier than about 70. And, like every argument in this essay, it shows that the writings existed and were valued early, not that what they say is true.

The Strongest Objection, Stated at Full Strength

The mainstream of critical scholarship does not accept the early-60s dating of the Gospels, and its case must be stated fully. The standard consensus, articulated by Bart Ehrman among many others, dates Mark to around AD 70, Matthew and Luke to roughly 80–85, and John to 90–95.16 The reasoning is layered: Matthew and Luke used Mark as a source, which requires Mark to have circulated first; and—the decisive point—Mark 13 (the “Olivet Discourse”) and its parallels have Jesus predicting the encirclement and destruction of Jerusalem. The simplest historical explanation, on this view, is that the Evangelist wrote after AD 70 and shaped the prophecy in light of what had occurred—a vaticinium ex eventu, a “prophecy after the fact.”

This is not special pleading, and it is important not to caricature it. Historians as a rule do not assume miracles or genuine foreknowledge when an ordinary explanation is available, and the correspondence between Jesus’s words and the events of 70 is real. The great historian of Judaism E. P. Sanders judged the Gospels probably composed between 70 and 90.17 Luke 21:20, with its talk of Jerusalem “surrounded by armies,” reads to many scholars as more specific than Mark—suggesting an author writing with the siege in view. To a critic, the early-dater looks like someone willing to multiply improbabilities (genuine prediction, a string of coincidental silences) to avoid the obvious.

A Reply—and What It Can and Cannot Carry

The reply begins with a concession. The vaticinium ex eventu reading is not special pleading as a method, and it cannot be refuted simply by insisting that Jesus could predict the future—that would smuggle the conclusion into the premise. But the inference is far less decisive than it sounds, for three reasons.

First, the prediction in Mark 13 is conspicuously unspecific about the manner of Jerusalem’s fall. It speaks of the Temple’s stones thrown down, of wars and rumors of wars, of flight to the mountains—but says nothing of the fire that gutted the Temple, the months-long siege, or the legions of Titus, the very details a writer narrating after the fact would find hard to omit. This is why E. P. Sanders, for all his post-70 dating of the Gospels, judged Mark’s prediction of the Temple’s destruction “probably pre-70,” since a prophecy written after the event would likely have mentioned the fire.18 The discourse’s most vivid imagery is drawn from Scripture, not from the events of 70. The “abomination of desolation” (Mark 13:14) is Daniel’s phrase (Daniel 11:31; 12:11), already applied in 1 Maccabees 1:54 to the profaning of the Temple in 167 BC; the darkened sun and the moon that “will not give its light” (Mark 13:24) echo Isaiah’s oracle against Babylon (Isaiah 13:10); and the Son of Man “coming in clouds” (Mark 13:26) comes from Daniel 7:13. N. T. Wright and R. T. France have both stressed how thoroughly the discourse is built from such Old Testament texts, above all Daniel 7.19 Even Luke’s “surrounded by armies” (21:20) is the stock language of any siege, and C. H. Dodd argued that Luke’s picture is drawn from the Old Testament accounts of Jerusalem’s fall in 587 BC rather than from Titus’s campaign (C. H. Dodd, “The Fall of Jerusalem and the ‘Abomination of Desolation,’” Journal of Roman Studies 37 [1947]: 47–54). Nor does a prediction of the Temple’s fall require foreknowledge to be early: Josephus reports another Jesus, son of Ananias, crying woe on the city and the sanctuary from AD 62, years before the war (Jewish War 6.300–309), and many critical historians, Sanders among them, count Jesus’s action and words against the Temple among the most secure facts about him (E. P. Sanders, Jesus and Judaism [London: SCM, 1985], 11 and ch. 1). Second, the silences in Acts remain hard to explain on a late date; a convergence of expected-yet-absent events (Paul’s death, James’s death, the fall of Jerusalem) is more telling than any single omission. Third, and most importantly, the early-dating case for the central claims does not stand or fall with Mark’s date at all. Even on Ehrman’s own chronology, the creed of 1 Corinthians 15 and Paul’s letters carry the death, burial, and resurrection proclamation back into the 30s and 50s. Whether Mark is 65 or 72 changes little here.

And here the limits must be stated plainly, in the critic’s favor. The argument from silence in Acts—as distinct from the political and apologetic arguments noted earlier, which do not depend on it—is genuinely an argument from silence, and such arguments are only as strong as the certainty that the author would have mentioned the missing event—a judgment about which reasonable scholars differ. Luke may have ended Acts where his narrative purpose was complete, with the gospel reaching Rome. The dissenting expert here is the consensus itself: most New Testament specialists are not persuaded that the silences outweigh the source-critical and Olivet-Discourse considerations, and they place the Gospels a decade or two later than Robinson did. The early-dating of the narrative Gospels to the pre-70 period is therefore a serious, defensible, but genuinely contested position—not a settled result.

So let the critic have the last objection before the reply. Ehrman would say: you have rescued the early creed, granted, but you have not rescued the early Gospels, and it is the Gospels that contain the miracles, the empty tomb, the detailed narratives—so the gap you most needed to close, you have left open. The first half is accurate: the creed does not date the Gospels. The second is not, because the argument never promised to close that gap. What the creed establishes is that the explanatory core—a crucified, buried, and risen Jesus, as the creed proclaims him, attested by named witnesses—is too early to be slow legend. The narrative details are a further question, addressed by other lines of evidence, including the case that the Gospels themselves rest on named eyewitness testimony, and the finding — from a scholar who set out to disprove it — that the Gospels were composed in the recognizable genre of Graeco-Roman biography (bios), a literary form whose very purpose is to convey historical information about a real person. That is a claim about genre, not a guarantee of accuracy: ancient bioi still selected and shaped their material around a theme. But it means the Gospels ask to be read as testimony about a real life, not as free-floating myth.20

The Opposite Challenge: Was Acts Written in the Second Century?

A smaller group of scholars challenges the mainstream from the other side, placing Acts not in the first century at all but in the early second. The fullest statement is Richard I. Pervo’s Dating Acts (2006), which places Acts around AD 115 on three main grounds. First, Pervo argues that Luke used Josephus’s Antiquities of the Jews, completed in AD 93/94, and that some of Acts’ historical tangles—above all Gamaliel’s mention of the rebels Theudas and Judas the Galilean (Acts 5:36–37)—are best explained as misreadings of Josephus. Second, he argues that the author knew a collection of Paul’s letters and drew on their characteristic language. Third, he finds that Acts shares vocabulary, themes and outlook with writings he places in the early second century—the Pastoral Epistles, Polycarp, the apologists.21 Joseph B. Tyson (2006) supplies a setting, arguing that Acts and the canonical form of Luke were completed around 120–125 as a response to Marcion, who rejected the Jewish Scriptures and set Paul apart from the other apostles; Luke–Acts, on this reading, insists on continuity with Israel and pairs Peter and Paul so closely that neither can be played against the other.22 It is a minority position, but one argued in detail by specialists in Acts, so it needs an answer.

Its supports are weaker than they look. The Josephus case leans hardest on a passage where Acts and Josephus disagree. Josephus places Theudas under the governor Fadus, in the mid-40s, and a few lines later mentions Judas the Galilean only through the execution of his sons, adding that Judas had led his revolt “when Cyrenius came to take an account of the estates of the Jews”—some four decades earlier. Acts has Judas come “after” Theudas, and gives Theudas “about four hundred” followers, a figure Josephus does not supply. Pervo and Steve Mason, a leading Josephus specialist, reply that Luke wrote from memory, not with Josephus open, and that Josephus’s own sequence—Theudas, then the sons of Judas—could explain the reversal; but Acts itself dates Judas correctly, “in the days of the census.” The real difficulty is that Gamaliel, speaking in the 30s, sets Theudas before Judas, whereas Josephus’s Theudas rose a decade or more later. It is a recognised historical problem: F. F. Bruce and others have proposed that Gamaliel meant an earlier rebel of that name, one of the many who rose after Herod’s death, though some doubt the name was common enough to make this likely; others suspect Josephus’s chronology. However it is resolved, Josephus’s sequence does not explain it. As Jonathan Bernier has argued, using a place where Luke diverges from Josephus to prove that Luke used Josephus exposes how weak the hypothesis is; the overlap is public Judean history that both men could know independently.23 The question is not closed—Mason thinks Luke probably did know Josephus’s work—but even if he did, that would place Acts after the mid-90s, not establish a date around 115. The argument from Paul’s letters cuts both ways. Acts never says that Paul wrote letters and never quotes one, and at points it tells the story differently from them (compare Paul’s Jerusalem visits in Galatians 1–2 with Acts 9, 11 and 15), which is why the more common judgement has been that its author did not use them; and where Paul in Acts does sound like the Paul of the letters, a writer who had heard Paul preach—as the “we” passages (e.g., Acts 16:10–17; 27:1–28:16) present the narrator—would not need a letter collection to know how he talked. Shared vocabulary with the Pastorals, Polycarp and the apologists may mean that they drew on Acts—Pervo himself allows that Polycarp may have—or simply that one church idiom ran across a few decades; it cannot by itself move Acts a generation later. And the themes Tyson reads as anti-Marcionite already have a first-century occasion in the disputes over Gentile inclusion that Paul describes in Galatians 2 and that Acts 10–11 and 15 narrate. Tyson’s reconstruction also rests on a contested premise—that Marcion used an earlier form of Luke which our Gospel revised—whereas Irenaeus, writing around 180, reports that Marcion cut down the Gospel according to Luke.24

Our verdict is that the case for a second-century date is not made. Its central link to Josephus is disputed at its strongest point, its argument from Paul’s letters is at least as well explained by an author who knew Paul, and its affinities and its Marcionite setting fit at least as well with a book written earlier and used in the second century. The evidence leaves Acts where most scholars place it: in the first century. The live question is the one already discussed—whether Acts was written in the early 60s or in the decades after 70.

The Honest State of the Scholarship

Two things are true at once, and a responsible presentation holds them together. On the dating of the four Gospels, the mainstream consensus—Mark c. 70, the others in the 80s and 90s—remains the majority view, and the pre-70 dating championed by Robinson and Hemer—and restated more recently by Alexander Mittelstaedt (2006), Karl Armstrong (2017), and David Seccombe (2020)—is a respected minority position, not the field’s settled judgment; a centrist commentator like Craig Keener, no partisan of the early date, still places Luke–Acts in the early 70s, with the 60s and 80s among the live possibilities (Craig S. Keener, Acts: An Exegetical Commentary, vol. 1 [Grand Rapids: Baker Academic, 2012], 400; Karl L. Armstrong, “A New Plea for an Early Date of Acts,” Journal of Greco-Roman Christianity and Judaism 13 [2017]: 79–110; Alexander Mittelstaedt, Lukas als Historiker [Tübingen: Francke, 2006]). But on the dating of the earliest tradition—the 1 Corinthians 15 creed and the undisputed Pauline letters—there is broad agreement across the ideological spectrum, skeptics included, that the material is remarkably early. The live debate is not about whether the central proclamation is early (it is) but about how much follows from that, and about where exactly the narrative Gospels sit within the eyewitness generation.

Conclusion: What Early Dating Does and Does Not Establish

What the argument establishes is real and worth holding onto. The foundational Christian claims about Jesus’s death and resurrection appearances are not the residue of generations of mythmaking; they are demonstrably early, fixed in creedal form within a few years of the events, named to specific witnesses, and circulated while hostile parties in Jerusalem could have objected. Even on the mainstream (later) dating, the Gospels fall within living memory. The “legend had time to grow” hypothesis, in its strong form, simply does not fit the evidence.

What it does not establish is equally important, and pretending otherwise would forfeit the reader’s trust. Early does not mean true. An early date rules out one explanation—slow, undetected legendary development—but it does not rule out sincere error or deliberate fabrication; people can and do invent things quickly. Nor does it prove the pre-70 dating of the narrative Gospels, which remains contested. The early-dating argument is best understood as removing a whole class of objection and clearing the ground—handing the central claims over to be assessed as first-generation testimony, where the harder questions of honesty, accuracy, and the resurrection itself can be taken up on their own terms (on the last, this library defends elsewhere the inference that Jesus rose bodily from the dead). It does not win the war. It just makes sure the right battle gets fought.

Frequently asked questions

If the Gospels were written decades after Jesus, isn't that plenty of time for legends to grow?

That objection quietly assumes the Gospels are our earliest evidence. They are not. The creed in 1 Corinthians 15, which Paul says he received, carries the death, burial, and resurrection appearances back into the 30s, within a few years of the crucifixion, naming witnesses who could still be questioned. Even Gerd Lüdemann, who rejects a bodily resurrection, dates it to within two to three years. Whatever Mark's date, the core proclamation isn't slow legend.

Doesn't an early date for the New Testament prove that what it says is true?

No, and the essay insists on this. Early does not mean true. An early date rules out one specific explanation, slow undetected legendary development, but it does not rule out sincere error or deliberate fabrication, since people can invent things quickly. The argument removes a whole class of objection and hands the central claims over to be assessed as first-generation testimony, where honesty, accuracy, and the resurrection are argued on their own terms.

Wasn't Mark written after AD 70 because Jesus "predicts" the destruction of Jerusalem?

Not necessarily, and the central claims don't depend on it. The post-70 reading is the majority view and isn't special pleading, but it is far less decisive than it sounds: Mark 13 is conspicuously unspecific, saying nothing of the fire, the siege, or Titus' legions. And crucially, the case for the central claims doesn't stand or fall with Mark's date: the 1 Corinthians 15 creed and Paul's letters are early regardless.
