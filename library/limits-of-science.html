Does Science Disprove God? The Scope and Limits of Science

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

“Science has buried God.” It is one of the most confident slogans of the age, and it is delivered less as an argument than as a weather report—a simple statement of where the educated world now stands. The picture behind it is vivid and familiar. Once, we are told, people reached for God to explain the thunder, the harvest, the plague, and the stars; now we have meteorology, agronomy, germ theory, and astrophysics, and the gaps where God used to live have been filled in one by one. On this telling, religious faith is a pre-scientific hangover, and the only reliable road to truth runs through the laboratory. Whatever cannot be weighed, measured, or tested is, at best, private feeling—and at worst, superstition.

This essay asks whether that confident verdict is earned. Its thesis is deliberately modest, and it is worth stating plainly at the outset so that no one mistakes it for a bigger claim. The thesis is not that science points to God, still less that science disproves atheism; those are separate arguments, taken up elsewhere. The thesis is only this: science neither does nor can disprove God, because the claims usually said to do so—“science is the only path to truth” and “nature is all there is”—are not scientific findings at all but philosophical commitments, and because explaining the mechanism of a thing never removes the agent behind it. The reach of science is one of the glories of the human mind. The question is whether it reaches all the way to the conclusion that God does not exist. It does not—and showing why is the work of three distinct moves.

First, let the case be put at its strongest. Its ablest modern champions—the chemist Peter Atkins, the biologist Richard Dawkins—hold that science faces no barrier it cannot in principle cross, that there is no question worth asking which the methods of science cannot eventually settle, and that to appeal to anything beyond nature is simply to give up on explanation.1 This is not a straw position; it is held by serious people, and it has the attraction of a clean, unified picture of the world. The reply is not to belittle science but to notice three things the picture quietly assumes.

One: Scientism Refutes Itself

The engine of the whole slogan is a claim the slogan rarely states out loud—that science is the only genuine route to knowledge. Bertrand Russell, no friend of religion, put it as bluntly as anyone ever has in Religion and Science (1935): “Whatever knowledge is attainable, must be attained by scientific methods; and what science cannot discover, mankind cannot know.”2 This view is usually called scientism—not science itself, but a philosophy about science that promotes it from one powerful way of knowing into the only one.

And here the ground gives way beneath its own feet. Consider the sentence again: “what science cannot discover, mankind cannot know.” Is that claim something science can discover? Could any experiment, any measurement, any instrument establish it? Plainly not. There is no telescope one can point at the proposition “science is the only road to truth” and read off its confirmation. The claim is a statement of philosophy, not a result of physics or biology—and so, by its own standard, it fails to qualify as knowledge. It saws off the branch it is sitting on. A rule that says “only scientific statements count as knowledge” is itself not a scientific statement, so if it is true, it is not knowledge; and if it is not knowledge, there is no reason to believe it. Either way, it collapses.

This is not a clever debating trick; it is the same fate that overtook an earlier, more rigorous version of the same idea. The logical positivists of the early twentieth century proposed a “verification principle”: a statement is meaningful only if it can be empirically verified or is true by definition. For a generation it was wielded to dismiss metaphysics and theology as literal nonsense—until philosophers noticed that the verification principle is itself neither empirically verifiable nor true by definition, and so, by its own terms, meaningless. The principle quietly died, and scientism is its less careful heir, carrying the same self-destruct mechanism inside it.3

A defender might protest that this topples a straw man—that no careful person really holds the exceptionless claim that only science yields knowledge, but merely the milder thesis that science is by far our most reliable guide to how the world is. That milder thesis is reasonable, and nothing here disputes it. But notice what it costs the slogan it was meant to rescue. A scientism modest enough to survive the self-refutation charge is also too modest to carry the atheist conclusion: “science is our most reliable method” simply does not entail “nature is all there is,” any more than “the microscope is the best instrument for studying cells” entails “only what a microscope can see is real.” The strong version refutes itself; the version that survives no longer disproves God.

The deeper point was made by someone who cannot be accused of hostility to science: Sir Peter Medawar, Nobel laureate in medicine and one of the twentieth century’s great essayists on scientific method. In The Limits of Science, Medawar insisted that science is “the most successful enterprise human beings have ever engaged upon,” and then, precisely as a scientist, marked its boundary. There are questions, he wrote, “that science cannot answer, and that no conceivable advance of science would empower it to answer”—the childlike yet inexhaustible questions, “How did everything begin? What are we all here for? What is the point of living?” The temptation, he noted, is to follow “doctrinaire positivism” and dismiss these “as nonquestions or pseudoquestions such as only simpletons ask and only charlatans profess to be able to answer.” But that dismissal, Medawar held, leaves us dissatisfied, because the questions are perfectly real to those who ask them. They are simply not the kind of question a laboratory is built to settle.4

Notice carefully what this does and does not show. It does not show that science is false, or small, or untrustworthy. Nothing here disparages the real triumphs of physics, chemistry, and biology, or suggests that the believer may wave away a scientific result that is inconvenient. The claim is only that science is incomplete—magnificent within its domain, and silent beyond it. To say that a thermometer cannot measure justice is not an insult to thermometers. It is simply to know what a thermometer is for.

Two: From a Method to a Metaphysics

The second move distinguishes two things that wear almost the same name, and whose confusion does most of the real work in the “science has buried God” slogan. Working scientists operate by methodological naturalism: when investigating the natural world, they look for natural causes and do not reach for “and then a miracle occurred” as a line in a research paper. This is an enormously fruitful working rule. It is not the same as metaphysical naturalism—the philosophical claim that nature is all there is, that the physical universe is a sealed box with nothing outside it. The first is a method; the second is a worldview. And the slogan trades on sliding, without argument, from the one to the other: because science looks only for natural causes, it is quietly assumed that only natural causes exist.

That this slide is a slide, and not a discovery, is best shown not by a believer but by a committed materialist describing his own mind with unusual candor. The Harvard geneticist Richard Lewontin, reviewing Carl Sagan’s The Demon-Haunted World in The New York Review of Books in 1997, wrote:

Our willingness to accept scientific claims that are against common sense is the key to an understanding of the real struggle between science and the supernatural. We take the side of science in spite of the patent absurdity of some of its constructs … because we have a prior commitment, a commitment to materialism. It is not that the methods and institutions of science somehow compel us to accept a material explanation of the phenomenal world, but, on the contrary, that we are forced by our a priori adherence to material causes to create an apparatus of investigation and a set of concepts that produce material explanations, no matter how counter-intuitive, no matter how mystifying to the uninitiated. Moreover, that materialism is absolute, for we cannot allow a Divine Foot in the door.5

Read fairly and in full—and Lewontin deserves to be read in full, because he is not confessing a vice but describing, with his eyes open, a framework he thinks justified—the passage is remarkable. Materialism, he says, is not where the evidence led; it is the prior commitment that decides in advance what sort of evidence will be allowed to count. The door is shut before the inquiry begins. That is a philosophical stance dressed in a lab coat, and Lewontin is honest enough to say so.

He is not alone in the candor. The distinguished cancer researcher George Klein, pressed by a theologian, said that his atheism was not based on science at all but was a matter of conviction—a childhood faith grown into an adult pillar of security, as he described it, not a conclusion forced on him by the laboratory.6 And the Nobel biologist Christian de Duve, himself a thoroughgoing naturalist about the origin of life, was clear-eyed that his program rested on a postulate: he proceeded on the working assumption that every event must have a natural explanation, treating “rational” and “natural” as interchangeable—a methodological rule he adopted rather than a theorem he had proved.7 In each case a thoughtful scientist grants the very distinction the slogan needs to blur: the method is one thing, the metaphysics another.

Honesty now requires the other half of the picture, and it is not a small concession. It would be a serious overreach to conclude from Lewontin that methodological naturalism is covert atheism, or that every scientist who uses it is a dogmatist smuggling in a worldview. The philosopher of science Ernan McMullin—a Catholic priest, no enemy of faith—argued exactly the opposite, and he must be given his full weight. For McMullin, methodological naturalism “does not restrict our study of nature; it just lays down which sort of study qualifies as scientific,” and it says nothing whatever about whether nature is all there is.8 A Christian biologist can—and routinely does—look for natural mechanisms all day in the lab precisely because she believes God made an orderly, intelligible world, and then worship that God on Sunday without the slightest contradiction. On McMullin’s view the method is religiously neutral, a fence around what counts as science, not a creed.

So the honest verdict is narrower than the slogan and sharper than a shrug. Methodological naturalism can be—and in Lewontin’s case openly is—a metaphysical commitment smuggled in under the respectable name of a method. But it need not be, and for many working scientists it is not. The error to expose is not the method; it is the unargued leap from “science studies natural causes” to “only natural causes are real.” That leap is never performed by any experiment. It is performed by the philosopher hiding inside whoever makes that leap—and it needs to be argued for, in the open, not presupposed.

Three: Mechanism Is Not Agency

The third move corrects what is perhaps the most common confusion of all—the quiet assumption that a scientific account of how something works competes with, and crowds out, a personal account of who is responsible for it. It does not. The two answers belong to different categories, and treating them as rivals is what philosophers call a category mistake.

Take the plainest example. Suppose you ask how a motor car moves, and I explain the internal combustion engine—the spark, the compressed fuel-air mixture, the expanding gases driving the piston, the crankshaft, the wheels. That explanation can be complete, exhaustive, correct down to the last valve. Does it show that Henry Ford had nothing to do with the car? Of course not. The engineering of the engine and the agency of the designer are not competing explanations fighting over the same slot; they are answers to different questions. One tells you the mechanism; the other tells you the mind behind the mechanism. To say “we understand combustion, therefore there was no designer” is exactly as confused as saying “we understand the chemistry of ink and paper, therefore Hamlet had no author.”

The same distinction is often put as the difference between how and why. Imagine a cake, and imagine the world’s greatest scientists given unlimited time to analyze it—the chemistry of its ingredients, the physics of its baking, the biology of the wheat and the eggs. They could in principle tell you how the cake is constituted in perfect and exhaustive detail. But not one of their instruments could tell you why it was made—that it was baked for a friend’s birthday, as an act of love. For that you must ask the baker, who is free to tell you or to stay silent, and whose answer no amount of further chemistry could ever supply or overturn.9 The “why” of purpose is not a gap in the “how” of mechanism, waiting to be filled by better science; it is a different kind of question altogether.

This is the right way to read the most famous anecdote in the whole debate. When the astronomer Pierre-Simon Laplace presented his great work on the mechanics of the solar system to Napoleon, the emperor is said to have observed that the book never mentioned the author of the universe. Laplace replied: “Sire, I had no need of that hypothesis.”10 The line is endlessly quoted as though it were science dismissing God. Read in context, it is nothing of the kind. Laplace was answering a narrow technical question—whether the equations describing the planetary orbits require a term for divine adjustment—and the answer was no: the orbits are stable on their own mechanics. But God was never a term inside the equation in the first place, any more than Henry Ford is a term inside a description of combustion. To find no need for God in the mathematics of orbital motion is no more evidence against God’s existence than finding no mention of the baker in a chemical assay of the cake is evidence that the cake baked itself. Laplace had simply answered the question he was asked. He had not answered—and his remark cannot answer—the question of whether the whole law-governed system has an Author.

Here, too, the fence must be set honestly. This third move does not, by itself, prove that a Creator exists. Showing that mechanism and agency are compatible is not the same as showing that there is in fact an Agent; it only clears away the illusion that the one rules out the other. A convinced atheist can grant every word of this section and still hold that, as a matter of fact, there is no mind behind the mechanism of the universe—that the cosmos has a “how” and no “why.” What the section establishes is that he cannot reach that conclusion merely by pointing to the success of science at describing mechanisms. The question “is there an Author?” remains open, to be argued on its own ground—not closed by the fact that physics can describe the machinery without naming him.

The State of the Question

It would be false to suggest the matter is tidily settled in the believer’s favor, and the mission of this essay is not served by pretending otherwise. The relationship of science and religion is a large and genuinely contested scholarly field, and able thinkers line up on several sides. The old “conflict thesis”—the idea that science and religion have been locked in perpetual warfare—is, interestingly, now regarded by most professional historians of science as a nineteenth-century myth, popularized by polemicists such as John William Draper and Andrew Dickson White and quietly abandoned by serious historiography; the real history is one of complex entanglement, not a simple war.11 But the collapse of the conflict thesis has not ended the philosophical dispute, and it should not be oversold as if it had. Confident defenders of scientism remain—Atkins and the late debates around the “new atheism” keep the stronger claim in play—and the live argument now is less about any particular scientific result than about this prior question of scope: how far does science reach, and where does philosophy begin?

On that question the honest observation is simply that the three moves above are not fringe apologetics but mainstream distinctions. The self-refutation of a strong scientism is widely treated in philosophy as a serious problem for it. The difference between methodological and metaphysical naturalism is standard in the philosophy of science—which is exactly why McMullin and Lewontin can both be quoted: they agree the distinction exists and disagree about whether the method carries the metaphysics. And the category difference between mechanism and agency is as old as Aristotle’s distinction among kinds of cause. None of this is a trump card. All of it together makes one limited, defensible point: the claim that science has disproved God is not itself a scientific claim, and it does not survive inspection as a philosophical one.

Conclusion: What This Does and Does Not Establish

The argument of this essay removes a roadblock; it does not complete a journey. It is worth being scrupulous about the difference, because a case that claims too much is a case that will not hold.

What has been shown is this, and only this: science does not and cannot disprove God. The slogan to the contrary rests on three mistakes—a scientism that fails by its own standard, a slide from a fruitful method to an unproven metaphysics, and a confusion of mechanism with agency. Expose the three, and the confident verdict that “science has buried God” is seen to be not a finding of science at all, but a philosophy that was smuggled in alongside it.

What has not been shown is just as important. Nothing here proves that God exists; nothing here disproves atheism; nothing here even establishes that the universe points to a mind. Those are positive arguments, and they must be made positively and on their own evidence—from the beginning of the universe, from its fine-tuning (where, it must be said carefully, atheist physicists concede the remarkable data while vigorously contesting the inference to design), from the existence of objective moral obligation, and—decisively for the Christian—from the historical resurrection of Jesus. And even if those arguments succeed in pointing to a transcendent Mind, that Mind is not yet the Father of Jesus Christ. A Creator or Designer reached by argument is still a long way from the Triune God of the Nicene Creed, who is known not by inference alone but by self-revelation in Christ. Clearing the ground of a false obstacle is a real service, but it is only the clearing of the ground.

Still, the ground is worth clearing, and the result is more liberating than it first appears. The believer need not fear that the next discovery will finally crowd God out of the last gap, because God was never a gap in the first place—never a term inside the equation, to be deleted when the equation is solved. He is the reason there is an equation to solve at all. To the Christian, then, science rightly understood does not bury God; it reads, line by careful line, a book that Someone else wrote. Those who wish to press on from “science has not closed the door” to the positive case that there is Someone on the other side of it will find it taken up in the companion essays on whether reason itself can be trusted in a godless universe, on where the laws of nature come from, and on whether miracles are even possible.

Frequently asked questions

Hasn’t science made God unnecessary by explaining things we once credited to him?

No—because that argument confuses mechanism with agency. Discovering how something works does not remove the mind behind it, any more than understanding the internal combustion engine shows that Henry Ford never designed the car. A scientific description of a mechanism and a personal explanation of a purpose answer different questions; they do not compete for the same slot. God was never a “gap” in our knowledge to be filled in, but the reason there is an orderly world to understand at all.

Isn’t science the only reliable path to truth?

That claim—scientism—refutes itself. The statement “only science delivers knowledge” is not itself a scientific finding; no experiment could establish it. So if it were true, it would not count as knowledge by its own standard. It is a philosophical claim about science, not a result of science—the same flaw that sank the logical positivists’ verification principle. Science is a magnificent way of knowing; it is simply not the only one, as the Nobel scientist Peter Medawar freely granted.

Does this essay prove that God exists?

No, and it does not try to. Its only claim is that science neither does nor can disprove God, because “science is the only path to truth” and “nature is all there is” are philosophical commitments rather than scientific findings. Removing that roadblock is not the same as proving the destination. The positive case—and the further step from a Creator to the Triune God of Christianity—is argued separately, from the beginning and fine-tuning of the universe, the moral law, and above all the resurrection of Jesus.
