Can Miracles Happen? Answering Hume and Spinoza

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

Imagine a courtroom in which the verdict is fixed before the first witness is sworn. However many people testify, however sober and consistent and unmotivated to lie, the judge has ruled in advance that their testimony cannot be heard, because it concerns a kind of event that—he has decided beforehand—simply does not happen. That is precisely the posture many take toward the central claim of the Christian faith. When the resurrection of Jesus is raised, a great many thoughtful people do not weigh the evidence and find it wanting; they decline to weigh it at all, because they hold that a miracle is either impossible or, whatever the testimony, never something a reasonable person could believe.

If that verdict is sound, the rest of the case is moot. There is no point examining an empty tomb or an early creed if resurrection has been ruled out at the door. So before any historical question can be honestly opened, one prior question must be faced: is there a good in-principle argument that miracles are impossible, or never rationally believable? This essay is about that question, and only that question. Its thesis is deliberately modest: the two classic attempts to shut the door—Baruch Spinoza’s argument that miracles are impossible, and David Hume’s that they are never credible—both fail. If they fail, the door is open. Whether any particular miracle actually walked through it is a separate, historical inquiry, taken up elsewhere.

Stating the Question Carefully

Two questions must be kept rigorously apart. The first is a question of possibility: could a miracle happen at all? The second is a question of actuality: did this or that specific miracle happen? These are answered by different means. Possibility is settled by philosophy—by asking whether the very concept is coherent and whether the universe is the kind of place open to such an event. Actuality is settled by evidence—by testimony, documents, and the ordinary tools of history. Spinoza and Hume both attempt to settle the actuality question a priori, in advance of any evidence, by reasoning from the nature of law itself. That is the move this essay contests.

It is worth stating plainly what will not be argued here. Nothing below shows that any miracle has occurred; nothing below establishes the resurrection, or that miracles are common, or that every wonder reported in Scripture happened as told. The claim is narrower and, precisely because it is narrow, more secure: the a priori bar is illegitimate, so miracle claims must be examined case by case on their evidence rather than dismissed at the outset. Removing the roadblock is not the same as completing the journey—but you cannot begin the journey while the roadblock stands.

What a Miracle Is—and Is Not

Careless definitions have done more to confuse this debate than any argument. A miracle, in the classical sense, is not simply a very strange or very rare event; a stunning coincidence is not a miracle, and neither is a phenomenon we cannot yet explain. Nor is it best described as nature “breaking down.” The most defensible definition is that a miracle is a special act of God who transcends the natural order—an event that would not have occurred had nature been left to its own resources. Strikingly, the atheist philosopher Antony Flew, no friend of theism for most of his career, framed a miracle in just such terms: as something that would never have happened if nature had been left to its own devices.1 The concept, then, is not incoherent even to those who reject it; the dispute is over whether such an act is possible and whether it ever occurs.

One further point of care, kept deliberately light and shared across the Christian traditions: a miracle in the biblical sense is not a bare display of power but a sign. This essay does not adjudicate which miracles are genuine, nor when or whether they occur today; those are questions on which sincere Christians differ. The point here is only conceptual: a miracle is God acting purposively within a world he made, not a random rupture in the fabric of things.

Descriptions, Not Prescriptions

The decisive move in the whole debate is a distinction about what a law of nature is. (The metaphysics of laws is treated at length in a companion essay;2 here only one facet matters.) A law of nature, on the most defensible account, is a description of how things regularly behave, not a prescription that dictates what must happen—and even those who hold that laws genuinely govern take them to govern only how things go when nothing interferes. The law of gravity does not compel a stone; it tells us, accurately, how unsupported bodies do in fact move when nothing interferes. That last clause is everything. The regularities hold when nothing interferes—and agents interfere constantly.

Consider an outfielder catching a falling ball. He does not repeal the law of gravitation; the law describes exactly what the ball would do left to itself, and it goes on describing that even as his glove intervenes. A finite agent, by introducing a new causal factor, produces an outcome the regularity alone would not have produced, without abolishing the regularity in the slightest. If a finite mind and hand can do this within nature, an infinite Mind who authored nature is hardly barred from doing the same on rare occasion and for a purpose. A miracle, so understood, is not the violation of a law but the introduction of a cause the law never claimed to exclude. This is why the loaded word “violation”—which both Spinoza and Hume lean on—quietly smuggles in the conclusion it is meant to prove.

The objection in its plainest form is simple and has real pull: a miracle, by definition, breaks the laws of nature, and what breaks the laws of nature cannot happen. The reply is not a modern evasion invented to rescue miracles from science; its core is ancient. Augustine, writing in The City of God in the early fifth century, met the objection in the form his own age gave it—that a wonder runs “contrary to nature.” Discussing a portent recorded by the Roman scholar Varro—a change in the appearance and course of the planet Venus—he denied the premise: “For we say that all portents are contrary to nature; but they are not so. For how is that contrary to nature which happens by the will of God, since the will of so mighty a Creator is certainly the nature of each created thing? A portent, therefore, happens not contrary to nature, but contrary to what we know as nature.”3 Augustine was not writing about laws of nature in the modern scientific sense, and he should not be read as settling the modern debate over what such laws are. But his point is a close cousin of the one just made: what a miracle runs against is our knowledge of nature’s ordinary course, not the order that the Creator himself gives to each thing he has made. So the charge that miracles “violate nature” does not establish that they are impossible; it assumes it, by treating nature as an order that stands over against its own Maker.

The Closed Box and the Open One

Underneath Spinoza’s argument—and the popular “science has disproved miracles” slogan—lies an assumption rarely stated: that the physical universe is a causally closed system—a sealed box in which nothing acts except other things inside the box. (Hume’s subtler, purely epistemic version does not depend on this; it is taken up in the next section.) On that assumption, of course, no miracle can occur, because there is no one outside to act. But whether the box is closed is not a finding of science; it is a metaphysical commitment. Naturalism assumes closure; theism holds that the universe is open to the God who made it. And here is the pivot: whether the universe is a closed system is exactly the question in dispute between the theist and the naturalist. To assume closure and conclude that miracles are impossible is to argue in a circle—to help oneself to the very premise the theist denies. If a God capable of creating a universe exists at all, the possibility of his acting within it follows almost trivially; the one who made the box is not trapped inside it.

Spinoza: Miracles as an Absurdity

The bolder of the two arguments is the older. Benedict de Spinoza, writing in his Tractatus Theologico-Politicus of 1670, held that miracles are not merely unbelievable but impossible. His reasoning, reconstructed fairly, runs like this: the laws of nature are the eternal, necessary decrees of God; whatever happens follows necessarily from those laws; therefore an event contrary to the laws of nature would be an event contrary to God’s own nature, which is absurd. Spinoza states it without flinching: “Nothing, then, comes to pass in nature … in contravention to her universal laws, nay, everything agrees with them and follows from them, for whatsoever comes to pass, comes to pass by the will and eternal decree of God.”4 He concludes that “a miracle, whether in contravention to, or beyond, nature, is a mere absurdity.”5

Stated at full strength, the argument has real force: if the laws of nature genuinely are immutable and inviolable, then by definition nothing can violate them, and a miracle so defined is a contradiction in terms—like a married bachelor. One cannot simply wave this away.

But it does not survive inspection, for two reasons. First, and fatally, it begs the question by definition. Spinoza builds inviolability into the meaning of “law of nature” and then discovers, unsurprisingly, that laws cannot be violated. But whether the regularities of nature are absolutely immutable—impervious even to their Author—is precisely what is in dispute. Define laws as descriptions of nature’s ordinary course, and the argument evaporates: a description of what usually happens is not breached by an unusual act, any more than the outfielder breaches gravity. Second, Spinoza’s conclusion was already contained in his metaphysics. He was a pantheist who famously identified God with Nature itself—Deus sive Natura, “God, or Nature”—so that there simply is no God who transcends the natural order and could act upon it from without.6 Given that God, miracles are indeed impossible; but that is not a discovery about miracles, it is a restatement of pantheism. Against a theist who affirms a transcendent Creator, the pantheist premise has no purchase; and the worry that an exception would set God against his own eternal decree fails once the miracle is seen as itself part of what God eternally wills, not an afterthought correcting it.

Hume: Miracles as Never Credible

The argument that actually shapes the modern mind is subtler, and more formidable. David Hume, in Section X of his Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding (1748), conceded the ground Spinoza had over-claimed. He did not try to prove miracles impossible. He argued instead that no testimony could ever make belief in one reasonable—an epistemological argument, not a metaphysical one.

Hume begins with a principle few would reject: “A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence.”7 He then defines a miracle as “a violation of the laws of nature” and presses the consequence: “and as a firm and unalterable experience has established these laws, the proof against a miracle, from the very nature of the fact, is as entire as any argument from experience can possibly be imagined.”8 The whole weight of human experience of nature’s regularity stands, he says, against any report of its breach: “as an uniform experience amounts to a proof, there is here a direct and full proof, from the nature of the fact, against the existence of any miracle.”9 From this he draws his celebrated maxim, offered at the close of Part I as “a general maxim worthy of our attention”: “That no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish.”10 In Part II, Hume argues that in practice such testimony never arrives; the witnesses to miracles are too few, too credulous, too eager, and too apt to found new religions, for their falsehood ever to be more astonishing than the wonder they report; and the wonders claimed by rival religions, he adds, cancel one another out.

This argument should be felt before it is answered. It appeals to the same evidential caution that makes us doubt a tabloid headline; it does not deny God or the possibility of the supernatural outright; and it seems only to ask the believer to be consistent about weighing evidence. It has persuaded many philosophers ever since, and any reply that treats it as foolish has not understood it.

Where Hume’s Argument Breaks Down

Yet for all its polish, the argument fails—and it fails in three distinct places.

First, the premise that the evidence for the regular is always greater than the evidence for the rare is simply false. A single well-attested, unrepeatable event can be rationally believed on adequate testimony; the frequency of an event and the strength of the evidence for it are two different things. We accept, on good testimony, that a particular golfer once made a hole-in-one, without demanding that he repeat it, and without weighing against his witnesses the countless times balls have not gone in. More tellingly, the naturalist’s own worldview affirms events believed to have happened exactly once—the origin of the universe and the origin of life among them. These are, on the standard scientific picture, unrepeated and in some sense unrepeatable; yet no one thinks it irrational to believe they occurred. If unique events can be rationally believed elsewhere, Hume’s blanket rule cannot be right. The Humean will reply that a miracle is not merely rare but contrary to the whole uniform run of experience—a stronger charge than mere infrequency. But that objection was met above: a miracle introduces a new cause into the ordinary course of things; it does not breach the regularity that holds when nothing interferes, and how low its prior should be set depends on whether there is a God with reason to act—a metaphysical prior, not a finding of experience.

Second, on one common reading the argument argues in a circle. Hume’s claim that “a firm and unalterable experience” stands against every miracle assumes what it needs to prove. How could one know that experience is uniformly against miracles unless one already knew that every miracle report, past and present, is false? But that is the very conclusion in question. C. S. Lewis pressed exactly this point: if we assume in advance that all reports of miracles are false, then of course experience is uniform against them—but we are then reasoning in a circle, having smuggled the conclusion into the premise.11 To label experience “uniform” is not to report a finding; it is to prejudge every disputed case at once. Hume’s modern defenders, such as Robert Fogelin, read Part I instead as showing how very high the bar for miracle testimony is set, with Part II arguing that no report has cleared it; on that reading the circle disappears, and the dispute moves to the third point—how the weighing is done.

Third, the argument weighs the evidence the wrong way. Hume, in effect, piles the entire mass of ordinary experience against the specific testimony for a specific event, and concludes the testimony must lose. But that is not how rational assessment of a particular claim works. The right method is not to add up how often the extraordinary fails to happen, but to weigh the specific evidence for this claim—the number, independence, and reliability of the witnesses, their access to the facts, their motives. Here the most striking witness is not an apologist at all. John Earman, a secular philosopher of science, subjected Hume’s essay to a rigorous probabilistic (Bayesian) analysis in a study pointedly titled Hume’s Abject Failure. Earman concludes that Hume’s argument, where it is original, is largely without merit, and that Hume overlooked how multiple independent witnesses can, in principle, make even a highly improbable event credible.12 Hume’s defenders, Fogelin among them, have replied to that verdict.

Hume’s Part II charges—credulous witnesses, eager believers, rival wonders—are real reasons for caution, but they are reasons to examine each report, not to rule all of them out in advance: they show how testimony often fails, not that it always must, and rival claims are to be weighed on their own evidence too, not cancelled en bloc.

Can a Historian Even Consider a Miracle?

A third objection grants everything so far and moves the bar to a new place. Suppose miracles are possible, and suppose the testimony for one is strong; still, the skeptic says, the historian—working strictly as a historian—can never affirm that one happened. The reason usually given is the principle of analogy, associated with Ernst Troeltsch: the historian reconstructs the unknown past by its likeness to present experience, so an event with no analogy in what we ordinarily observe falls outside what history can credit.13 Applied to the resurrection the argument runs: people do not rise from the dead today, we have no analogy for it, so the historian must set the claim aside whatever the evidence.

The principle is sound as a working tool and fails as a rule. As a guide—that we reasonably read the past in the light of the regularities we know—it is simply good method, and nothing here quarrels with it. But pressed into a law that nothing lacking a present analogy may ever be affirmed to have happened, it proves far too much. That law would equally forbid the historian, or the scientist, from affirming the genuinely singular events our own account of the past already includes—the first living thing from non-living matter, or the beginning of the universe itself, events no one has watched happen and for which present experience offers no straightforward analogy. A defender of the rule may reply that these are reconstructed by extrapolating familiar processes backward, so they are not strictly analogy-less; but that answer only moves the question to the evidence in each case, which is just where the believer is content to have it. A rule strict enough to bar a resurrection would, applied consistently, also bar the beginning of the cosmos—and no one treats that as sober history.

Used as a bar against miracles in particular, the principle also quietly assumes the very thing in dispute. To say “no event unlike our ordinary experience may be affirmed” is only another way of saying “no miracle may be affirmed,” since a miracle simply is an event unlike the ordinary course of nature. The rule, so used, does not discover that no miracle occurred; it stipulates it in advance—the closed-box reasoning met earlier, now dressed as historical method. This is why the theologian Wolfhart Pannenberg argued that excluding the resurrection on principle imports a metaphysical assumption about what reality can contain, rather than rendering a historical verdict;14 it is C. S. Lewis’s circularity objection again, moved from the philosopher’s chair to the historian’s desk. A historian may, of course, weigh the evidence for any particular miracle and judge it weak. What a historian may not do is declare the question closed before the evidence is heard—and then call that history.

A sharper form of the objection concedes that the historian may investigate but forbids the verdict. Even after the most thorough inquiry, it says, a historian may report only that no natural cause is known, that the setting was charged with religious expectation, and that the witnesses sincerely believed a miracle had occurred; to add that God caused it is to step out of history and into theology. The reply begins by granting what is true here: naming God as the cause is a theological inference, not a bare historical datum, and the companion case for the resurrection says as much—the step from “best historical explanation” to “and therefore God acted” is one the evidence may invite but cannot compel, and it does not force the conclusion on anyone whose worldview assigns no room to the act of God.15 But two things follow, and they cut against the objection rather than for it. First, it touches only the cause, never the event. Historians constantly settle what happened while leaving how or why open: they are confident Carloman died in 771 without a settled account of how he died. A historian could therefore in principle conclude that an event occurred—that a man was dead and was later alive—while handing the question of its ultimate cause to the reader’s wider view of reality. Second, part of the objection’s force rests on a narrower worry—that a historian may infer only what can be directly observed—and that worry would wreck ordinary science along with it: no one has ever seen a quark or a black hole, yet both are affirmed as the best explanation of their effects. That alone does not license the further step to a divine cause; a transcendent agent is not simply one more unobserved entity, and the inference to God remains the worldview-dependent step conceded a moment ago. What it does show is narrower but still to the point: inferring an unseen cause is not, as such, outside a discipline’s reach, so the bar against a cause beyond nature has to be argued for, not merely assumed—and to assume it is once again to declare the box closed.16

A last objection trades method for sociology: scholars do not all agree that any miracle has happened, and—the argument runs—a properly historical conclusion ought to command assent across the field, whatever a scholar’s private convictions. But this sets a bar ordinary history never has to clear. Professional historians disagree permanently about questions with no religious charge at all—the causes of the First World War, the character of the French Revolution—because every historian brings a framework of values and assumptions to the evidence, a point philosophers of history concede without embarrassment.17 If the mere absence of consensus disqualified a conclusion, a great deal of ordinary history would fall with it. The demand is applied unevenly, besides: a handful of writers deny that Jesus existed at all, yet that dissent does not stop the agnostic New Testament scholar Bart Ehrman—or the field—from affirming, as securely as anything in ancient history, that Jesus lived and was crucified. The lesson is about the principle, not the headcount: a conclusion need not command unbroken assent to count as historical. That does not make the sceptics about miracles a fringe—they plainly are not, and the resurrection does not enjoy anything like the agreement the crucifixion does—but it does retire the demand that only a verdict the whole field shares may be called history. Nor was consensus ever the same thing as truth: whole communities of experts have agreed on claims later shown false. A historian may judge a hypothesis probably true without waiting for the entire guild to raise its hand.

The State of the Scholarship

It would be triumphalist, and false, to say the debate is over. Hume’s essay remains one of the most discussed pieces in the philosophy of religion, and it retains able defenders. J. L. Mackie, in The Miracle of Theism, restated Hume’s reasoning and judged that the balance of probability will almost always lie against accepting a miracle report—the strongest contemporary case for Hume’s side, and one any responsible treatment must reckon with.18 The answer is the one already given: how low a miracle’s prior should be set turns on whether there is a God with reason to act, so it cannot be fixed near zero without first settling the question of God—and enough independent testimony can, as Earman argued, overcome even a low prior. What stays the skeptic’s hand is a metaphysical prior, not a gap in the evidence. The live dispute is not whether miracles are worth taking seriously as a concept—that much is now widely granted—but how to weigh testimony for extraordinary events, and how high the prior improbability of a miracle should be set.

What has genuinely shifted is the collapse of the confident a priori dismissal. Richard Swinburne’s work argued decades ago that there is no good reason to rule out miracles in advance and that historical evidence for them can carry real weight.19 And the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s survey of the field, by Timothy McGrew and Robert Larmer (both defenders of the rationality of belief in miracles), records that Hume’s critique “has itself come under heavy fire and is now viewed in some quarters as requiring defense.”20 The center of gravity has moved from “miracles are impossible or never believable” to “let us look at the evidence in each case”—which is exactly where the honest inquirer wanted the conversation to be.

Conclusion: What This Does and Does Not Establish

The argument of this essay is a door-opener, not a proof. It does not show that God exists—that case is argued elsewhere, from the beginning of the universe, its fine-tuning, and the moral law. It does not show that any miracle has occurred. It certainly does not show that the resurrection happened; that is a historical claim, to be tested by the ordinary evidence of the empty tomb, the early creed, and the transformed witnesses.21 What it establishes is this and only this: neither Spinoza, nor Hume, nor the appeal to historical method has given a sound in-principle reason to refuse the evidence a hearing. Spinoza’s argument begs the question and presupposes a pantheism no theist accepts; Hume’s rests on a false premise, a circle (on its common reading), and a mistaken method of weighing; and the principle of analogy, pressed into a bar, either proves too much or quietly assumes what it set out to show.

If God exists, miracles are possible; if miracles are possible, then whether one occurred is a question for the evidence, not for a rule laid down in advance. The believer need not claim more than that—and need not claim less. The courtroom that fixes its verdict before hearing the witnesses is not being rigorous; it is being closed. The reasonable thing, and the honest thing, is to unbar the door and let the testimony be heard. The decisive case is the resurrection of Jesus; those wishing to press on to it will find it taken up in the companion essays on the minimal facts of the resurrection and the empty tomb.

Frequently asked questions

Doesn’t modern science prove that miracles can’t happen?

No. Science describes how nature regularly behaves when nothing intervenes; it does not, and cannot, prove that nothing outside nature ever intervenes. That the universe is a closed system, sealed against any Creator, is a philosophical assumption, not a scientific finding—and it is exactly the point in dispute. A law of nature is a description of nature’s ordinary course, not a decree that God cannot act.

Isn’t it always more likely that the witnesses were mistaken than that a miracle occurred?

This is Hume’s argument, and it does not hold as a blanket rule. Frequency and evidence are two different things: we rationally believe unique, unrepeatable events—including, on the naturalist’s own view, the origin of the universe and of life—on adequate evidence. The right method is to weigh the specific evidence for the specific claim, not to pile all of ordinary experience against it. The secular philosopher John Earman argues on probabilistic grounds that Hume’s reasoning simply fails.

Does this essay prove that the resurrection happened?

No, and it does not try to. Its only claim is that miracles are possible and worth investigating—that the a priori arguments of Spinoza and Hume do not succeed in ruling them out. Whether any particular miracle, including the resurrection, actually occurred is a historical question, settled by evidence and taken up in separate essays. Removing the roadblock is not the same as finishing the journey.
