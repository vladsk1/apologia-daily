Is Jesus God in the New Testament? The Whole Witness, Surveyed

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

Short on time? Read the quick answer →

In a locked room a week after the crucifixion, a hard-headed fisherman who had refused to believe his friends' reports put his hand toward the wounds of a man he had watched die. He did not say, "So it is really you." He said something no devout Jew said to anyone but the LORD of Israel: "My Lord and my God."1 John, who built his entire Gospel around the conviction that there is one God, lets the line stand without correction. It is the climax of the book.

That confession is one thread. The question this essay asks is what happens when you pull on all of them at once. Pick up any individual verse and a determined reader can propose another reading. But the New Testament is not a single verse; it is a library of some twenty-seven documents, written by perhaps nine or ten authors across roughly half a century, in two languages, to scattered communities who never coordinated their theology. The striking thing is what they converge on. This is the wide-angle essay — the survey that ties the threads together. The close work on individual texts lives in the companion pieces, and I will point you to them as we go rather than repeat their detail.

The thesis, stated carefully

Here is the claim, and its limits. The New Testament writers do not hand us the later vocabulary of "one essence in three persons" or "two natures in one person." Those are fourth- and fifth-century formulations — the church's attempt to systematize, against rival readings, what it found already in these texts. What the New Testament does, repeatedly and across independent authors, is something the British scholar Richard Bauckham has called including Jesus within the unique identity of the one God of Israel.2 Jesus is placed on the Creator's side of the line that Jewish monotheism drew between God and everything else: he shares God's throne, God's acts of creation, God's eternal name, and — most tellingly — God's worship.

So the thesis is not "the New Testament teaches the Nicene Creed in so many words." It is the more modest and more defensible claim that the raw data the creeds later organized is already here, distributed across the whole canon, and that the developed doctrine is the faithful articulation of it rather than a later imposition upon it.

The spread of the evidence

Begin with John, where the case is most explicit. The Gospel opens by reaching behind Genesis: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God."3 Jehovah's Witnesses render that last clause "a god" — a reading that, on the mainstream grammatical analysis, misreads the grammar — and the focused treatment of why the standard translation holds lives in the companion essay on John 1:1. The same Gospel has Jesus take the divine self-designation of Exodus and Isaiah onto his own lips — "before Abraham was, I am"4 — and closes, as we saw, with Thomas. Whatever one makes of any single line, John's frame is unmistakable: the Word who was God became flesh and was confessed as God.

Paul, writing decades earlier, is often said to be quieter on this. He is not. In Colossians he calls Christ "the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation," the one "by him all things were created," and then says flatly that "in him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily."5 In Philippians he quotes what most scholars regard as a pre-Pauline hymn — material older than the letter itself — describing one who was "in the form of God" and did not count "equality with God" a thing to be grasped, and who is finally given "the name that is above every name," at which every knee shall bow.6 That last phrase is lifted from Isaiah 45, where the LORD swears that to him alone every knee shall bow — and Paul applies it to Jesus. The hymn has its own deep-dive: see the Philippians 2 hymn. Nor is Isaiah 45 the only divine-name text Paul redirects. In Romans 10, having just written that "if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord... you will be saved," he clinches the point by quoting Joel — "everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved" — a passage in which the Lord one calls upon is Yahweh himself.7 On the most natural reading of Paul's argument, the Old Testament's own language for calling on God is applied, without apology, to Jesus. Two further texts in the Pauline letters — Romans 9:5 and Titus 2:13 — appear to call Jesus "God" directly, though both turn on Greek grammar and punctuation, and I will concede below that they are disputed.

Hebrews is, if anything, bolder. Its first chapter strings together Old Testament passages and aims them at the Son. God says to the Son, "Your throne, O God, is forever and ever."8 Then the author quotes Psalm 102 — a psalm addressed to the LORD, the unchanging Creator — and applies it to the Son: "You, Lord, laid the foundation of the earth in the beginning, and the heavens are the work of your hands... but you are the same, and your years will have no end."9 A text about Yahweh's eternity and creative work is, without apology, read as words to Christ.

Revelation completes the circle. The risen Christ tells John, "Fear not, I am the first and the last,"10 and at the book's end, "I am the Alpha and the Omega, the first and the last, the beginning and the end."11 "The first and the last" is precisely how the LORD identifies himself in Isaiah — "I am the first and I am the last; besides me there is no god."12 The title God reserves for himself, the exalted Jesus claims.

Called God outright — and which texts are secure

Step back and notice a category the survey has scattered. A handful of texts do not merely place Jesus on the Creator’s side of the line; they call him theos, “God,” directly. Three of them turn on Greek grammar, and I will concede below that they are contested — Titus 2:13 and 2 Peter 1:1, which hinge on the Granville Sharp construction, and Romans 9:5, where a comma the original lacked can be placed so that “God” becomes a separate doxology to the Father. But two of the direct predications depend on neither the Granville Sharp rule nor a disputed comma, and they are the ones worth resting weight on.

The first is where John’s Gospel ends. Seeing the risen Jesus, Thomas “said to him” — the narrator’s own words — “My Lord and my God.”1 A sceptic answers that this was an astonished cry to heaven, an “oh my God” rather than a confession of Jesus. But the reply is cumulative and rests on no single word. The narrator frames the words as spoken to Jesus (“said to him”); the phrase is an articular nominative that functions as a vocative of direct address, the ordinary Greek way of calling someone “Lord” and “God”; and Jesus, far from correcting the confession, accepts it and blesses those who will believe without seeing (John 20:29), while John lets it stand as the climax of the book, immediately before he states why he wrote (John 20:30–31). It is no accident that the same Gospel opens with “the Word was God” and closes here: the deity of Christ is the frame around the whole work, not a stray remark inside it.

The second is in Hebrews, and it is spoken to the Son: “Your throne, O God, is forever and ever” (quoting Psalm 45:6).8 Most translations and commentators read “O God” (ho theos) as a vocative of address — the Father addressing the Son as God. A minority render it “God is your throne,” taking the same words as a statement about the Son rather than an address to him, and they find their foothold in the next verse, where “God, your God, has anointed you” plainly means the Father. That alternative is grammatically possible; it simply sits awkwardly in a chapter whose entire argument is the Son’s superiority to the angels — a chapter that enthrones and anoints him — which is why the vocative is the majority reading. Concede the alternative and the point is softened, not lost.

So the honest picture is this. Grant, for the sake of argument, every grammatical concession on the contested three, and two direct predications still stand: John’s confession, which no grammatical alternative touches, and Hebrews’ “O God,” which rests on the reading its own chapter’s argument favours. The book-length study of exactly this question, weighing text by text every place the New Testament calls Jesus theos, is Murray Harris’s Jesus as God. The “called God” case does not live or die on a disputed comma; it has a floor — John 20:28 at the least — and the floor is firm.

The evidence that isn't a title

Names and texts can be argued. Harder to argue away is what the earliest Christians did. Here the work of Larry Hurtado is central. In his major study Lord Jesus Christ, Hurtado documents a pattern of devotional practice — what he calls a "binitarian" shape of worship — that emerged "amazingly early," within the first years of the movement, among Jewish believers who would have regarded worshiping any creature as blasphemy.13 They sang to Christ, baptized into his name, called on his name, broke bread in his presence, and prayed to him. And they did this while insisting, with the Shema, that God is one.

Consider three strands. First, Jesus receives worship — the devotion due God alone — and accepts it, where in the same literature angels twice refuse it (Revelation 19:10; 22:9). Second, Jesus forgives sins, prompting the objection, "Who can forgive sins but God alone?"14 — an objection the narrative does not deny but answers by demonstration. Third, and perhaps most telling, the earliest Christians prayed to Jesus. Paul preserves an Aramaic prayer untranslated into his Greek-speaking churches: Marana tha, "Our Lord, come!"15 That the invocation survives in Aramaic is the point. It carries us back behind Paul's Greek to the Aramaic-speaking Judean church — to the very first community, calling on the risen Jesus as the Lord who will come. The highest devotion is not a late Hellenistic overlay. It is bedrock.

For the framework that organizes all this — divine titles, divine attributes, divine throne, divine works, divine worship, and the divine name — see the companion essays on the HANDS framework and the Titles of Jesus.

The strongest objections, fairly stated

Two communities deserve to be heard at their best. Jehovah's Witnesses and biblical Unitarians read this same evidence within an "agency" framework drawn from the Hebrew Bible. There, an emissary can bear the sender's name and authority — the angel of the LORD speaks as God, Moses is made “like God to Pharaoh” (Exodus 7:1) — without being the LORD. On this reading, Jesus is God's supreme agent, given the divine name and even a kind of worship as the one who perfectly represents God, while remaining a distinct and subordinate being. John 1:1c, they argue, should read "the Word was a god" or "was divine"; the worship Jesus receives is the relative homage due an exalted vice-regent; and the loftiest texts describe a created firstborn raised to glory, not the Creator. In its academic form the reading is subtler still: the New Testament historian James F. McGrath, contesting Bauckham’s and Wright’s “divine-identity” category, grants that John’s language about Jesus is genuinely exalted yet argues that Second Temple Judaism could let God share his name and functions with a supreme agent without a second God arising — and points to John 17:3, where Jesus addresses the Father as “the only true God” and himself as the one “whom you have sent” (James F. McGrath, The Only True God: Early Christian Monotheism in Its Jewish Context [Urbana: University of Illinois Press, 2009], esp. ch. 4). The agency motif these readings appeal to is genuinely present in the Hebrew Bible; the question is whether it can bear the weight they put on it.

On the disputed proof-texts, candor is required — and it costs the case nothing. A few verses appear to call Jesus "God" directly: Titus 2:13 ("our great God and Savior Jesus Christ") and 2 Peter 1:1 turn on the Granville Sharp rule, which Daniel Wallace argues holds without exception in native Koine when its conditions are properly met;16 Romans 9:5 turns on where one places a comma the original lacked. Translators have not always agreed, and the grammar is genuinely contested. But this is the weakest leg of the argument, not its foundation. The case does not rest on it — the weight falls on worship and divine identity, to which the objectors' strongest reading must now answer.

The most formidable academic challenge is Bart Ehrman's developmental thesis in How Jesus Became God. Ehrman argues that the historical Jesus did not claim divinity, that his earliest followers held an "exaltation" Christology — God raised the man Jesus and adopted him to divine status — yet he also finds a pre-existent Christ already in material older than Paul, a figure he takes to have been conceived at first as a subordinate divine being — God’s chief angel, or a divinity of a lesser order — whose elevation to the co-equal God confessed at Nicaea in 325 is the later step, reaching its New Testament height in the latest Gospel, John.17 On this view what developed was the divine figure’s rank, not the date of pre-existence itself.

The reply — and the concession

Take the agency reading first, since of the two it engages the biblical text most directly. It is true that in the Hebrew Bible an emissary can bear the sender's name and authority. But there is one thing no agent — not the angel of the LORD, not Moses made “like God to Pharaoh” (Exodus 7:1), not the most exalted king, not even the great angels who were given God’s own name, such as Yahoel in the Apocalypse of Abraham — ever receives: the cultic devotion reserved for God alone. This is where even McGrath’s version meets its limit — grant that God’s name and functions could be entrusted to a supreme agent, and the worship itself still could not be. The point needs care, because a gesture of homage was not itself the line: Israelites could bow before a king as well as before the LORD — 1 Chronicles 29:20 uses a single verb of prostration for both. What Jewish monotheism guarded jealously for God was not the posture of homage but the substance of worship — sacrifice, invocation, prayer. Angels, prophets, and kings could be honored; but the intermediary figures of Second Temple Judaism characteristically refuse worship and redirect it to God. And that is exactly the line the earliest Christians crossed with Jesus. In the very books that record it, an angel twice refuses the worship John offers, redirecting it to God (Revelation 19:10; 22:9); Jesus receives it without a word of protest. Marana tha is not the homage due a vice-regent — it is an invocation, calling on the risen Lord to come; that is, prayer, offered by the first Aramaic-speaking church. A figure you worship and pray to, while confessing in the same breath that God is one, has already outrun the category of "supreme agent." John 17:3 excludes the idols, not the Son; five verses later the same Jesus asks for the glory he had “before the world existed” (John 17:5)—glory that Isaiah says YHWH shares with no other (Isaiah 42:8). The one by whom every created thing was made cannot himself be one of the created things — firstborn over creation, not first item within it. Grant the framework the titles for the sake of argument; it still breaks at the altar. So the agency reading does not hold.

The developmental thesis fails for a different reason. The disagreement is not really about dates — Ehrman himself finds a pre-existent Christ in material older than Paul’s letters — but about what kind of being the earliest Christians took him to be. And the evidence answers that question with the one thing his developmental thesis cannot easily accommodate: the earliest worship. Marana tha is Aramaic, pre-Pauline, rooted in the Jerusalem church of the 30s. The Philippians hymn, widely regarded as pre-Pauline, already applies an exclusive-worship text of YHWH to Jesus (the pre-Pauline dating is itself debated — Gordon Fee argues Paul composed the hymn — but the worship-of-Jesus datum stands either way) — and the pattern of worship Hurtado documents is in place within a decade or two of the crucifixion, not a century after. Bauckham sharpens the point: the earliest Christians did not slowly climb a ladder from a low Christology to a high one; they expressed the highest possible claim — inclusion in the divine identity — from the start, using the very resources of Jewish monotheism.18 John is not the invention of a new doctrine but the most explicit articulation of an old one. The development is in the clarity, not in the substance.

There is a further reply, and it presses on the premise rather than the timeline. Ehrman’s model requires the Synoptic Gospels to carry no pre-existence — on his reading they know an exaltation or adoption Christology, not an incarnation. Yet a serious line of scholarship — argued most fully by the Cambridge New Testament scholar Simon Gathercole — contends that Matthew, Mark, and Luke already speak of a Jesus who came from somewhere. Again and again the Synoptic Jesus frames his mission in a distinctive idiom: “I have come” (Greek ēlthon) followed by a statement of purpose — “I came not to call the righteous, but sinners” (Mark 2:17); “I have not come to abolish [the Law or the Prophets]… but to fulfill” (Matthew 5:17); “I came to cast fire on the earth” (Luke 12:49).19 In the Jewish writings of the period, that same formula — “I have come” plus a stated purpose — is characteristically how a heavenly messenger announces the mission that has brought him down to earth, from the angel of Daniel onward.20 If the idiom carries its native force on Jesus’ lips, the Synoptics are already presenting one who came from heaven for a purpose — the very preexistence the developmental thesis says they lack.

The argument is contested rather than settled, and its strongest objection must be granted a hearing: ēlthon plus a purpose is also perfectly ordinary Greek and need imply no heavenly origin at all — at least once the Synoptic Jesus uses “came” simply of moving on from one town to the next (Mark 1:38). Gathercole’s own claim is measured to match: the pattern may strengthen the case for a Synoptic preexistence rather than clinch it. But the distribution of the idiom is a checkable fact, and — if Gathercole is right about what it signals — it tells against the tidy picture in which the Synoptics know no pre-existent Son. Jesus speaks in the idiom of one who came from heavenWhat the angelic parallel does and does not claimIs sayingThe form of these sayings matches how a heavenly messenger in Jewish literature announced a mission he had come to earth to accomplish — so the idiom on Jesus’ lips implies he too came from somewhere.Not sayingNot that Jesus is an angel. The parallel is to the form of the announcement, never the nature of the speaker — whom these same Gospels present as the Lord the angels serve, not one of their number. Reading it as “Jesus is a created, angelic being” is precisely the Arian error the rest of this case refutes. The claim here is deliberately narrow, and it is enough: the Synoptics do not sit safely at the bottom of the ladder the objection needs them to occupy.

Gathercole adds a quieter candidate from Luke’s birth narrative, where Zechariah sings of “the Dawn” that “will visit us from on high” (Luke 1:78), which he reads as pointing to a heavenly origin. The meaning of “from on high” is itself disputed — some read it simply as “from God,” with no claim about where the Dawn was before — and nothing in this case rests on it.21

Ehrman’s exaltation reading also leans on a handful of verses that can be read as Jesus becoming Son or Lord at a particular moment: the voice at his baptism, “You are My beloved Son” (Mark 1:11); Paul’s sermon applying Psalm 2, “today I have become Your Father,” to the resurrection (Acts 13:33); Peter’s “God has made this Jesus, whom you crucified, both Lord and Christ” (Acts 2:36); and the Son “declared with power to be the Son of God by His resurrection” (Romans 1:4). Ehrman does not claim that Paul, who on his reading already held Christ to be a pre-existent divine being, or Luke, who makes Jesus Son of God from his conception (Luke 1:35), meant these verses as adoption; his claim is that they preserve older formulas that did, and at Luke 3:22 he argues that Luke himself wrote Psalm 2’s “today I have begotten you.” That is a reconstruction of a layer behind the texts, and it is genuinely debated; the texts as we have them do not require it. Two things count against it. It asks us to believe that Luke and Paul kept formulas whose sense they did not share, and Ehrman himself thinks Paul adjusted the Romans formula to fit his own view, so the words as Paul wrote them carry Paul’s sense, not an adoption. And the earliest material we have does not show exaltation coming first: the hymn behind Philippians 2, which Ehrman also dates before Paul’s letters, already has a pre-existent one “in the form of God,” so the two kinds of language stand side by side from the start. The voice at the baptism echoes Psalm 2 but says only “You are My beloved Son,” without the psalm’s “today I have become Your Father” (though one early family of manuscripts of Luke 3:22 does read the psalm). Luke applies the same psalm to Jesus before Easter, to the rulers gathered “against Your holy servant Jesus, whom You anointed” (Acts 4:25–27), so for him Psalm 2 does not fix the resurrection as the moment Jesus became Messiah; and he has the angel call the newborn Jesus “Christ the Lord” (Luke 2:11). The apostles did read the resurrection as God’s confirmation, but a confirmation needs something to confirm. Paul’s formula has the same shape: the one “declared with power to be the Son of God by His resurrection” is already “His Son, who was a descendant of David according to the flesh” (Romans 1:3–4). The contrast is between the Son in the weakness of the flesh and the Son in power, not between a mere man and an adopted Son.22

What these texts do describe is real, and it should not be flattened. Exalted to God’s right hand, the risen Jesus does what he had not done before: he pours out the Spirit (Acts 2:33) and, as the risen man, receives “all authority in heaven and on earth” (Matthew 28:18). The exaltation gives him new roles, not a new natureWhat changes at the exaltationIs sayingThe eternal Son, who as God has always reigned with the Father, now — incarnate, crucified and risen — takes up as man the reign the Father gives him in his mission: pouring out the Spirit, ruling, and one day judging.Not sayingNot that he became God, or became the Son, or rose in divine rank at Easter; not that he was ever, before it or after, less than the Father in his divine nature; and not that the Father’s giving marks a rank within the Godhead. His deity and sonship are eternal; what is new is the exalted state of his human life and the public exercise of his lordship.. As the companion essay on the Philippians hymn puts it, the exaltation restores and publicly vindicates a status already possessed, and confers a name — a public station — not a divine nature the exalted one had previously lacked.

Ehrman is owed one concession — the same one our own thesis conceded at the outset, and it is narrower than it first appears. The developed creedal vocabulary came laterWhat "developed later" meansIs sayingThe technical creedal vocabulary and systematic formulation (Nicaea's "one essence," Chalcedon's "two natures") came later, as the church's articulation of what was already in the texts.Not sayingNot that the tri-une reality, or the deity of Christ, was a later human invention. The data — Jesus sharing God's throne, name, and worship — is present from the earliest layers.: the New Testament hands us the data — the throne, the name, the worship, the tensions — but not the fourth-century grammar that later systematized it. That much is true, and it is all Ehrman's evidence actually shows. What the development thesis gets wrong is not that the vocabulary developed — of course it did — but its leap to the conclusion that the deity of Christ was a late arrival, when the deity is precisely the thing that developing vocabulary was straining to state. The words for it were refined over centuries; the reality is bedrock, and it is there in the earliest layer we can reach.

The divine identity, revealed at the cross

There is a deeper layer still — the one that gives Richard Bauckham’s study its title, God Crucified. The New Testament includes Jesus in the divine identity not only at the heights — on the throne, in creation, in worship — but at the very bottom, on the cross. Isaiah had seen “the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up” (Isaiah 6:1); and when the same prophet turns to the Servant who is despised and crushed, he says that he too shall be “high and lifted up” (Isaiah 52:13), reaching for the very words of the throne-vision. The God who “inhabits eternity,” Isaiah adds, is the same God who dwells “with him who is of a contrite and lowly spirit” (Isaiah 57:15). And John draws the threads together without apology: Isaiah, he says, “saw his glory and spoke of him” — of Jesus (John 12:41). The exaltation of the crucified Servant is told in the language of YHWH’s own throne.23

This is why the Gospels can place on the lips of the dying Jesus the opening cry of Psalm 22, “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” (Mark 15:34). The one who shares the divine identity enters, on the cross, the depths of human god-forsakennessWho suffers on the crossIs sayingIt is the incarnate Son, in his full humanity, who suffers, dies, and cries out — so that God himself, in the person of the Son, has entered our suffering and death for us.Not sayingNot that the divine nature suffered or changed, and not that the Father was crucified or forsaken (the ancient error of patripassianism). The Father is not the Son; the impassible God does not perish; the cross reveals who God is, it does not make God mortal. Nor was the Son torn from the Father or the Trinity divided: the Father never ceased to love the Son, and the union of the persons was never broken. The forsakenness is real, borne in the Son’s human experience, not a severing within the one God. On the divine-identity reading this is not a lapse into something less than God but the fullest disclosure of who God is: the God of Israel is the God who gives himself, to the uttermost, for his people.24

What this does, and does not, establish

Honesty about scope is itself part of the case. This essay establishes one strand of a cumulative argument: that the New Testament, read across its full breadth, presents Jesus as sharing the identity of the one God of Israel and as the rightful recipient of God's worship — and that this presentation is early, widespread, and woven into the church's earliest practice, not bolted on at the end. The convergence of independent authors on the same astonishing inclusion is hard to explain as coincidence or gradual drift.

It does not, by itself, establish the developed creed. It does not settle the disputed grammar of Romans 9:5 or prove the Trinity from a proof-text. And it does not stand alone: the textual case here interlocks with the historical case for the resurrection, the witness of the earliest post-apostolic church, and the philosophical coherence of trinitarian monotheism — each treated elsewhere in this Library. What it offers is the foundation those other lines build on. When Thomas reached toward the wounds and said "My Lord and my God," he was not inventing a category. He was naming, in four words, what the whole New Testament had been saying all along.

Frequently asked questions

Is Jesus actually called God in the New Testament, or is that a later invention?

Yes — John 20:28 at the least calls him God directly, and more broadly the claim is not that the New Testament states the Nicene Creed word for word; that vocabulary is later. It is that independent authors, writing across half a century in two languages, repeatedly include Jesus within the unique identity of the one God of Israel. He shares God's throne, creation, eternal name, and worship. Thomas's My Lord and my God, John's Word who was God, Hebrews' Your throne O God, and Revelation's first and last all converge on the same astonishing inclusion.

Couldn't Jesus just be God's supreme agent, given the divine name without being God?

No. The agency motif Jehovah's Witnesses and biblical Unitarians appeal to is genuinely present in the Hebrew Bible — an emissary can bear the sender's name and authority — but it cannot bear the weight they put on it: it breaks at one point — cultic worship. A gesture of homage was not the line; Israelites could bow before a king as well as the LORD. What Judaism guarded for God alone was the substance of worship — sacrifice, invocation, prayer. Angels in these same books twice refuse worship and redirect it to God, yet Jesus accepts it, and Marana tha, our Lord come, is prayer addressed directly to him by the first Judean church. A figure you worship and pray to, while confessing God is one, has already outrun the category of supreme agent.

Didn't the belief that Jesus was God evolve slowly, as Bart Ehrman argues?

No. Ehrman's developmental thesis is owed one narrow concession: the developed creedal vocabulary, the technical language of Nicaea and Chalcedon, is unquestionably later. But that is all his evidence shows. The dispute is not really about dates, since Ehrman himself finds a pre-existent Christ in material older than Paul; it is about what kind of being the first Christians took Jesus to be, and the earliest evidence answers it. Marana tha, our Lord come, is prayer addressed directly to him by the first Judean church; the Philippians hymn is widely regarded as pre-Pauline. The high devotion is bedrock, not a late accretion. The development is in the clarity, not in the substance.
