The Minimal Facts Argument: Building the Resurrection Case on Ground Even Sceptics Concede

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

Short on time? Read the quick answer →

An Argument Designed to Survive Disagreement

Most arguments for the resurrection of Jesus run into the same wall on the first move. The case begins by quoting the Gospels, and the sceptic answers that the Gospels are precisely what is in dispute — late, biased, theologically motivated documents written by believers for believers. The conversation stalls before it starts. The achievement of the minimal facts approach, developed over four decades by the philosopher and historian Gary Habermas and pressed into its fullest scholarly form by his student Michael Licona, is to refuse that stalemate. Instead of asking the sceptic to grant the reliability of the New Testament, it asks only for a short list of facts that the sceptic almost certainly already grants — and then argues that the resurrection is the best explanation of those.1

This essay is the overview of the resurrection case as a whole. It does not re-argue each fact in depth — the empty tomb, the early creed, Paul's conversion, the appearances, the transformation of the disciples, and the conversion of James each have their own dedicated treatment in this library. The aim here is to understand the method: why minimal facts, what the two criteria are, how the inference to the best explanation works across the whole set, and — crucially — where serious critics say the method strains. The strength of the argument is also the source of its sharpest objections, and both deserve a careful hearing.

The Two Criteria

A "minimal fact," in Habermas's sense, is not simply a fact he finds persuasive. It is a fact that satisfies two deliberately demanding tests. First, it must be strongly evidenced — typically by multiple independent lines of testimony. Second, and this is the distinctive move, it must be accepted by the great majority of scholars who study the subject, including the sceptical ones — atheist, agnostic, Jewish, and liberal scholars, not only evangelicals.2 The second criterion is doing the rhetorical work. It means that whatever survives the filter is common ground; the believer and the unbeliever can argue about its explanation without first having to settle the inspiration or even the general reliability of Scripture.

Habermas's claim to know what the consensus is rests on an unusual project. Since the mid-1970s he has compiled a running bibliography of academic writing on the resurrection in English, French, and German, tracking how scholars come down on roughly 140 sub-questions. That bibliography now contains on the order of several thousand sources.3 From it he distils the facts that command near-universal assent. The list is usually given as four to six items; in its standard form it runs: Jesus died by crucifixion; his disciples sincerely believed he rose and appeared to them; the persecutor Paul was converted by what he took to be an appearance of the risen Jesus; the sceptic James, Jesus' brother, was likewise converted; and — added with an explicit flag that it is more contested — the tomb was found empty.4

Why crucifixion is the bedrock

The first fact illustrates the method's discipline. That Jesus died by Roman crucifixion is attested by all four Gospels, by Paul, and — outside the Christian sources entirely — by the Jewish historian Josephus and the Roman historian Tacitus, who called the new movement a "pernicious superstition" and had no motive to flatter it.5 This is why even John Dominic Crossan, co-founder of the Jesus Seminar and one of the most sceptical figures in the field, can write that Jesus' execution "is as sure as anything historical can ever be."6 Habermas does not cite Crossan because Crossan is friendly; he cites him because Crossan is hostile, and the concession therefore counts. That is the engine of the whole approach — letting the opposition supply the premises.

The point is worth pressing, because the crucifixion is the load-bearing fact and sceptics probe it hardest. Beyond the Christian writings, a widening circle of non-Christian sources refers to the execution — though they do not all carry the same weight, and the honest presenter keeps the distinction sharp. Josephus and Tacitus state it outright (the Josephus notice carries later Christian embellishment, but its core reference to the crucifixion under Pilate is widely accepted as authentic). The satirist Lucian of Samosata mocks the Christians for worshipping "the man who was crucified in Palestine." A Syriac letter attributed to Mara bar Serapion appears to allude to the Jews putting to death their "wise king," though it never names Jesus and the identification is a scholarly inference rather than a certainty. And the Babylonian Talmud records that "Yeshu" was "hanged" on the eve of Passover — a hostile Jewish memory of an execution, even if "hanged" is not simply Roman crucifixion and the reference's identification is debated. So the accurate summary is not that five sources "narrate" the crucifixion but that two of them — Josephus and Tacitus — state it, while Lucian, Mara, and the Talmud presuppose or allude to it. None was friendly to the movement.7

Did Jesus really die? The swoon theory

The bedrock is not merely that Jesus was crucified but that he died — and a persistent minority theory, the "swoon" or apparent-death hypothesis, disputes exactly that, proposing that Jesus survived the cross, revived in the cool of the tomb, and was mistaken for one risen from the dead. Two considerations tell decisively against it. The first is medical. Roman crucifixion killed by asphyxiation, blood loss, and shock, and it was carried out by professional executioners with every incentive to confirm death. A detailed 1986 study in the Journal of the American Medical Association reviewed the physiology and concluded that interpretations "based on the assumption that Jesus did not die on the cross … appear to be at odds with modern medical knowledge."8 The second is historical, and it was pressed not by an apologist but by the arch-sceptic David Friedrich Strauss. Writing in 1879, Strauss ridiculed the theory: a man who "had stolen half-dead out of the sepulchre," who "crept about weak and ill" in desperate need of bandaging and care, could not possibly "have given to the disciples the impression that he was … the Prince of Life."9 A battered survivor would have drawn pity and a physician, not the worship of a conqueror of death. The swoon theory has to make the disciples' explosive conviction rest on a scene that would have produced its opposite.

The Earliest Report: The Creed Paul Received

If the crucifixion is the bedrock, the earliest report of what the first disciples proclaimed is the true foundation of the positive case — and it is not a Gospel written decades later but a formula older than any of them, embedded in a letter that even the most sceptical scholars date to the early-to-mid 50s. Writing to the Corinthians, Paul hands on what he explicitly says is tradition he had himself received:

"For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received, that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures, and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve." (1 Corinthians 15:3–5)10

The verbs "delivered" and "received" are the technical vocabulary of passing on fixed oral tradition, and the passage's balanced clauses, its non-Pauline phrasing, and its Semitic features have persuaded scholars across the spectrum that Paul is here quoting a formula he did not compose but inherited — one that predates his letter by years. John Kloppenborg's careful analysis is representative in concluding that the core is a pre-Pauline formula, even while he is more cautious than some about confident claims for its precise Aramaic wording, its Jerusalem origin, and its very earliest dating.11

How early? The chain is short and each link is firm. Jesus was crucified around AD 30. Paul, by his own account, was converted within a few years of that — most reconstructions place his conversion one to three years later — and he tells the Galatians that three years after his conversion he went up to Jerusalem and stayed with Cephas (Peter), also seeing James the Lord's brother, the two men the formula names as recipients of appearances.12 Whether Paul first received the creed at his conversion or on that Jerusalem visit, the tradition traces back to within roughly two to five years of the crucifixion — and its content back earlier still, to the community that coined it. That is why critical scholars reach for unusually strong language. Ulrich Wilckens judged that the formula "goes back to the oldest phase of all in the history of primitive Christianity"; Walter Kasper held that the creed was already "in use by AD 30"; and J. Murphy-O'Connor represents the wide agreement that Paul is quoting received tradition rather than composing it — a cluster of judgments gathered in Gary Habermas's survey of the field.13 The window is far too narrow a gap for a legend to fill.

What even the sceptics grant

The most striking feature of the second minimal fact — that the disciples sincerely believed the risen Jesus had appeared to them — is how far across the ideological spectrum it is conceded, and on the historian's own terms rather than the believer's. Gerd Lüdemann, an atheist New Testament scholar, wrote that "it may be taken as historically certain that Peter and the disciples had experiences after Jesus' death in which Jesus appeared to them as the risen Christ."14 E. P. Sanders, among the leading historical-Jesus scholars of his generation, was equally blunt: "That Jesus' followers (and later Paul) had resurrection experiences is, in my judgment, a fact. What the reality was that gave rise to the experiences I do not know."15 The Anglican scholar Reginald Fuller called the disciples' Easter faith "one of the indisputable facts of history" and held that the historian "must … postulate an 'x'" to explain it — remarking, of the appearance to Jesus' sceptical brother, that "if there were no record of an appearance to James the Lord's brother in the New Testament we should have to invent one."16 Even Bart Ehrman, no ally of the apologetic case, grants that "it is a historical fact that some of Jesus' followers came to believe that he had been raised from the dead soon after his execution."17 Habermas sums up the pattern: "perhaps no fact is more widely recognized than that early Christian believers had real experiences that they thought were appearances of the risen Jesus."18

It is essential to be precise about what these concessions do and do not grant, because the point is easily abused. Not one of these scholars is here conceding, as a matter of historical argument, that Jesus rose from the dead. Lüdemann, Sanders, and Ehrman each propose or leave open a naturalistic explanation of the experiences, and to enlist any of them as a witness to the resurrection itself would flatly misrepresent them. What they concede is narrower — and still remarkable: that the earliest followers really did have experiences they took to be encounters with a living Jesus, and that the tradition reporting it is astonishingly early. That conceded ground is the starting line the argument reasons from, never the finish line it claims to have already reached.

It is also why Paul is arguably the single most valuable witness in the entire discussion. His testimony is early; it is written in his own hand rather than reported at second or third remove; and it comes from a former enemy of the movement he now died proclaiming. When he lists the appearances in 1 Corinthians 15 he is naming men he had personally met, and his account of his Jerusalem visits in Galatians 1–2 shows a gospel he received independently of the Jerusalem apostles yet which they publicly endorsed — not a modern-style audit of anyone's memory, but powerful evidence that the message Paul preached and the message of Peter and James were one and the same from the start.19

From Facts to Resurrection: The Inference

The minimal facts argument is not a deduction. It is an inference to the best explanation, the same form of reasoning a historian or a detective uses: among the live hypotheses, which one accounts for the whole body of data with the most explanatory scope and power, the least strain, and the fewest unsupported assumptions? Licona's 2010 monograph, The Resurrection of Jesus: A New Historiographical Approach, makes this explicit, borrowing the criteria for adjudicating hypotheses from the philosophy of history — explanatory scope, explanatory power, plausibility, degree of being ad hoc, and illumination — and applying them as a working historian would to any contested event.20

The argument's force is cumulative. No single fact, taken alone, forces the resurrection. An empty tomb by itself admits of theft or relocation. Visionary experiences by themselves admit of hallucination. What the naturalistic alternatives struggle with is the conjunction. The strongest form of the visionary objection is not the private individual hallucination but the well-documented phenomenon of shared bereavement and religious visions — the kind Bart Ehrman presses, citing collective experiences such as Marian apparitions, where many people sincerely report seeing a figure together. That deserves a direct answer. Two features resist it: first, the appearances include people with no grief and no prior expectation — Paul, a hostile outsider actively persecuting the movement, and James, whose family is remembered as thinking Jesus out of his mind, and who did not believe during the ministry21 — whereas bereavement visions arise among those already devoted to and longing for the one they have lost; and second, no documented apparition tradition on record has produced a community that concluded a particular person had been bodily raised and then proved willing to suffer and die proclaiming it. The stolen-body hypothesis might explain an empty tomb, but it cannot explain why the disciples would suffer, and in documented cases die, for what they would then know to be a fraud, nor why Paul, attacking the movement from the outside, would convert. The legend hypothesis founders on chronology: the resurrection was being preached in Jerusalem within a very few years — on the early Christian account, within weeks — and, as the creed examined above shows, the tradition itself traces to within a few years of the crucifixion, far too early for legend to have done its slow work.

The argument, then, is that each rival explanation can cover one or two facts but breaks on the others, while a single hypothesis — that Jesus actually rose — covers all of them at once. Licona's conclusion is stated with care: "Jesus' resurrection from the dead is the best historical explanation of the relevant historical bedrock."22 Note the wording. He claims the best explanation, reached by ordinary historical method, not a mathematical proof.

The name for this reasoning — and its honest limit

It is worth naming the move precisely, because critics sometimes treat it as a sleight of hand. The philosopher Charles Sanders Peirce called it abduction — a third mode of reasoning alongside deduction and induction, with the form: "The surprising fact, C, is observed. But if A were true, C would be a matter of course. Hence, there is reason to suspect that A is true."23 That is exactly the shape of the resurrection argument: the disciples' transformation, Paul's conversion, the empty tomb and the rest are the surprising facts; a real resurrection would make them a matter of course; hence there is reason to suspect it. And here is the honest difficulty. Stated crudely, that pattern is the textbook fallacy of affirming the consequent — "if A then C; C; therefore A" — because some other cause might produce the same facts. Peirce did not hide the weakness: writing in 1878 he granted that this kind of inference "is a weak kind of argument," one that "often inclines our judgment so slightly toward its conclusion that we cannot say that we believe the latter to be true; we only surmise that it may be so."24

What turns a weak surmise into a reasonable conclusion is not a change in logical form but the weighing of rivals. A bare abduction entertains one hypothesis; a disciplined inference to the best explanation entertains all the live ones and asks which accounts for the whole body of data with the most scope and the least strain — and, crucially, which eliminates the causes that are simply not adequate to the effect. This is ordinary historical method. The historian, reasoning as Peirce did from "numberless documents and monuments" to the real existence of a Napoleon he had never seen, reaches a person or an event as the only supposition that explains the traces it left behind.25 Applied here, the theft, hallucination, and legend hypotheses are not waved away; each is shown to be causally inadequate to some part of the evidence — which is what the section below examines. The comparative move blunts the affirming-the-consequent worry without dissolving it entirely, since one can always ask whether the true explanation is one no one has yet proposed; that is why the argument leans on the facts even critics grant rather than on any single inference. What it delivers, in the end, is the kind of verdict a jury delivers: not deductive certainty, but the best account of the evidence.

The Strongest Objections

The minimal facts approach is popular, and popularity has made it a target. The serious objections do not dispute that Jesus was crucified or that the disciples believed they saw him. They attack the method — and an honest survey has to state them at full strength.

Dale Allison: probability is in the eye of the beholder

The most formidable critic here is not a village atheist but Dale Allison, one of the most respected New Testament scholars alive, whose 2005 Resurrecting Jesus and 2021 The Resurrection of Jesus: Apologetics, Polemics, History treat the evidence with unusual rigour — and who remains, by his own account, genuinely undecided. Allison grants much of the data. His objection cuts deeper than any single fact: he argues that the inference from the facts to the resurrection cannot be made independently of the worldview the investigator brings to it. "Probability," he writes, "is in the eye of the beholder."26 Whether the resurrection is the best explanation depends in part on one's prior estimate of whether such a thing can happen at all — and that prior is not itself settled by the historical data.

This is the strongest objection in the literature, and it should be conceded as far as it goes. The minimal facts do not, by themselves, compel assent from someone whose background convictions assign a vanishingly small prior to any miracle — the point David Hume pressed in 1748 and which Allison restates with modern rigour.27 The honest reply is not to deny this but to reframe what the argument claims. It does not claim to coerce the committed naturalist. It claims that, if one is open to the possibility that God might raise the dead, the historical evidence then makes the resurrection the most reasonable explanation of an otherwise baffling set of facts. The argument removes the historical excuse for unbelief; it cannot remove the metaphysical one, and it should not pretend to. Allison himself, notably, judges the resurrection appearances and the empty tomb to be better evidenced than most sceptics allow — he simply will not take the last step on historical grounds alone. The point to see is that what stays his hand is a metaphysical prior, not a gap in the historical evidence — which is exactly this argument's contention: the history is not what is holding the honest inquirer back.

Robert Cavin, Richard Carrier, and the "consensus" problem

A second objection targets the second criterion directly. When Habermas says a fact is "accepted by the majority of scholars," two distinct challenges follow. The philosopher Robert Greg Cavin questions whether the inference itself — from the agreed facts to the conclusion that a miracle is the best explanation — is sound.28 But inference to the best explanation is simply the standard tool for reconstructing any contested past event, and the resurrection is being judged by the very criteria — explanatory scope, power, and being less ad hoc — that historians apply to any hypothesis whatever; it is not being handed a special exemption. On inspection Cavin's worry, like Allison's, resolves into the prior probability of a miracle — the metaphysical dispute this essay openly concedes history alone cannot settle — rather than a flaw in the inference form itself. And the historian Richard Carrier, among others, presses the count directly: which scholars, counted how? Habermas's bibliography is not a randomised survey; it is a compilation of those who have chosen to publish on the resurrection — a population that may over-represent the religiously committed. A widely circulated statistical critique by members of the Global Center for Religious Research argued that the underlying dataset is opaque and that the consensus claims, especially the figure that some three-quarters of scholars accept the empty tomb, cannot be independently verified from what Habermas has published.29

This is a fair methodological warning and the figures should be handled with the corresponding caution. The empty-tomb percentage in particular — drawn from Habermas's 2005 article in the Journal for the Study of the Historical Jesus — is his own tally of the literature he has read, not a poll, and critics are right that it cannot be audited.30 Two things, however, blunt the force of the objection. The "minimal" facts may not be minimal enough

A subtler worry, pressed from within friendly scholarship as well as from outside, is that some items on the list smuggle in more than the consensus actually concedes. "The disciples believed they saw the risen Jesus" is nearly universally granted; "they saw group appearances," as the creed reports, is granted by fewer; and "the tomb was empty" by fewer still. The honest version of the argument keeps these tiers distinct, building first on the genuinely common ground and only then arguing — separately and openly — for the more contested items. Where presenters blur the tiers, treating every fact as equally bulletproof, they hand the critic an easy and deserved rebuttal. The method's integrity depends on its restraint.

The State of the Scholarship

Where does the live debate actually stand? On the foundational data, there is genuine and broad agreement: across the spectrum, scholars accept that Jesus was crucified and that his followers had experiences they sincerely interpreted as encounters with the risen Christ. That much is not seriously contested. What is contested is everything downstream — the cause of those experiences, the historicity of the empty tomb, and above all whether history can license the supernatural conclusion at all.

The naturalistic alternatives remain on the table and are defended by serious people. Gerd Lüdemann argued for visions rooted in guilt and grief; Bart Ehrman appeals to well-documented bereavement visions; Allison holds the question open. None of these scholars thinks the disciples were lying, and the apologist should not either — the experiences were real to those who had them. The dispute is about their source. The minimal facts argument contends that no naturalistic mechanism yet proposed accounts for the whole set — the crucifixion, the early and group experiences, the conversion of a persecutor and a sceptic, and the empty tomb — without straining badly somewhere. Its critics contend that the resurrection hypothesis strains worse, because it requires a miracle. That, in the end, is the real fault line, and it is a philosophical one as much as a historical one.

What the Argument Does and Doesn't Establish

The minimal facts argument is best understood as a carefully bounded instrument. It establishes that a small set of facts about the death and aftermath of Jesus is firmly grounded and broadly conceded, and that the most natural explanation of those facts together — for anyone not antecedently committed to ruling miracles out — is that Jesus rose from the dead. It does this without asking the sceptic to grant biblical inspiration, biblical inerrancy, or even the general reliability of the Gospels. That is a genuine and unusual achievement, and it is why the approach has reshaped how the resurrection is argued in the last forty years.

What it does not do is force the conclusion on someone whose worldview assigns no room to the act of God. The most candid statement of that limit comes from the other side. The process philosopher Charles Hartshorne—a non-Christian who did not accept the resurrection—reflected on the 1985 debate between Gary Habermas and the atheist Antony Flew and granted that he could neither explain away the evidence Habermas pressed nor agree with Flew’s denial of it, before naming the real obstacle: “My metaphysical bias is against resurrections.”31 It does not prove the resurrection in the way a theorem is proved; it makes it the best explanation, which is the most any historical argument about any event can claim. And it depends, at its contested edges — the empty tomb, the precise scope of the consensus — on premises that the honest advocate must label as more disputed than the rest. Habermas and Licona, at their best, say exactly this. The argument's power is that it begins where even the critic stands; its limit is that the last step, from "best historical explanation" to "and therefore God acted," is one the evidence invites but cannot compel. As the apostle whose own conversion is one of the minimal facts would put it, the case is to be made "with gentleness and respect" (1 Peter 3:15) — confident in how far the evidence reaches, and candid about where argument ends and trust begins.

Frequently asked questions

Did the disciples just hallucinate that they saw Jesus alive?

The strongest version appeals to shared bereavement visions, which deserves an answer. But two features resist it. Some who reported appearances had no grief or expectation, like Paul, a hostile persecutor, and James, a former skeptic. And no apparition tradition has ever produced a community convinced a person was bodily raised, then willing to suffer and die proclaiming it.

Weren't the Gospels written decades later, so isn't the resurrection just a legend?

The legend theory founders on chronology. The resurrection was being preached in Jerusalem within weeks, and the creed Paul says he received and delivered in 1 Corinthians 15:3-7 can be dated to within a few years of the crucifixion, far too early for legend to do its slow work. The case also starts from facts even skeptics like Crossan concede on the evidence.

Do most scholars really accept the empty tomb?

Handle that claim with caution. The empty-tomb figure is Habermas's own tally of the literature he has read, not an audited poll, and critics rightly note it can't be independently verified. This essay flags the empty tomb as the more disputed, softer point in the chain. The case is strongest leaning on facts critics themselves grant, like the crucifixion and the disciples' sincere experiences.

Isn't inference to the best explanation just "affirming the consequent"?

Stated crudely it has that shape — if the resurrection happened we would expect these facts, we have these facts, so the resurrection happened — and some other cause might in principle produce the same facts. But a disciplined inference to the best explanation is not a bare one-hypothesis leap. It weighs every live rival — theft, hallucination, legend — and keeps only the explanation that covers the whole set of facts while eliminating the causes that are inadequate to them. That is ordinary historical and forensic reasoning: it yields not deductive proof but the best account of the evidence, the standard a jury uses. Charles Peirce, who named this mode of reasoning, granted it is a weak kind of argument on its own — which is exactly why the case is built comparatively, on the facts even sceptics grant, rather than on a single inference.
