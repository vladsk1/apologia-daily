The Full Humanity of Christ: Why It Matters That God Took Real Flesh

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

Among the very first errors the church had to fight was not the denial that Jesus was God. It was the denial that he was a man. Within a generation of the apostles, teachers were circulating who found the whole idea of a bleeding, hungering, dying God unbearable — too undignified, too fleshly, too low. Surely, they reasoned, the divine Son had only appeared to be human, the way an actor wears a mask. So the aged apostle John, or a writer close to him, drew a startlingly sharp line: “every spirit that confesses that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is from God,” and every spirit that does not “is not from God.”1 The flesh was not a detail to be waved away. It was the test.

Modern readers usually meet the opposite worry — a Jesus so human he is only human, a Galilean teacher later inflated into a god. Elsewhere in this library we make the sustained case for the other half of the confession: that the New Testament includes Jesus within the very identity of the one God of Israel (see Is Jesus God in the New Testament? and the HANDS framework). This essay defends the half that is easy to lose precisely because we are so busy defending the first: that the same Jesus is, without remainder, a real human being. The historic confession is not either God or man, and not a blend of the two into something that is neither. It is the harder, stranger claim that he is fully God and fully man — and that if he is not truly one of us, the good news collapses.

Stating the claim carefully

The doctrine can be put in one disciplined sentence: the eternal Son of God, without ceasing to be God, took to himself a complete human nature — body and rational soul — and was born, lived, and died as a genuine human being. Each clause is load-bearing. Without ceasing to be God rules out the idea that the deity was traded away for the humanity. God took to himself a human natureWho became man, and howIs sayingIt is the eternal Son — the second person of the Trinity — who assumed a human nature, adding humanity to his deity.Not sayingNot that the Father or the divine nature turned into flesh, nor that God suffered as God. The divine nature did not convert into a human one; the Son took humanity to himself while remaining fully, unchangeably God. Complete human nature — body and rational soul rules out a Christ who is human on the outside only. And born, lived, and died insists this is history, not appearance.

It is worth being precise about the logic, because the humanity is the premise skeptics least expect a Christian to press. The argument is not that Jesus was a mere man; it is that the one who is God also became truly man. So affirming the humanity does nothing to weaken the deity — the two natures are held together in one person, a point the church spent four centuries learning to state without contradiction. Everything below defends the second nature; nothing below retracts the first.

The Pauline spine: real flesh, real birth, real death

The oldest datable Christian documents we possess are the letters of Paul, generally dated to the 50s of the first century — earlier than any of the Gospels. It is often assumed that Paul, so exalted in his Christology, has little interest in the earthly Jesus. That is a mistake. Paul never argues for Christ’s humanity; he everywhere assumes it, which is a stronger kind of evidence, because a thing presupposed offhandedly by a writer making some other point was not up for debate.

Start with a single verse Paul dropped almost in passing: “when the fullness of time had come, God sent forth his Son, born of woman, born under the law.”2 Every word matters. Born of woman — the Son entered the world the way every human does, through a mother, in a body. Born under the law — he entered it as a first-century Jew, bound by Torah, located in a real people at a real moment. This is Galatians, one of the letters no serious scholar disputes Paul wrote; and it plants the incarnation in ordinary human birth without a hint that Paul thought the point controversial.

The most carefully weighted statement comes in Romans, where Paul says God sent “his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh.”3 The word likeness has misled readers into hearing a hint of mere appearance — as if Christ only resembled human flesh. But the phrase is “the likeness of sinful flesh,” not “the likeness of flesh.” On a reading widely defended in the commentary tradition, the qualifier bears on “sinful” rather than on “flesh”: Christ took real, full human flesh, but not the sinfulness that marks fallen humanity. The point does not finally hinge on that grammatical judgment, though — even interpreters who construe the phrase differently agree on the two things the verse holds together: his flesh was genuine, and he was without sin.4

Two further texts press the point still harder, and they come from the letters to Timothy. “For there is one God, and there is one mediator between God and men, the man Christ Jesus“The man Christ Jesus”Is sayingThe one mediator is genuinely, fully human — Paul names Christ’s manhood as essential to his work of standing between God and us.Not sayingNot that Jesus is merely a man. The same letters call him the one whose grace was given “before the ages began” and who “was manifested in the flesh”; his humanity is real without cancelling his deity..”5 And, in what looks like a snatch of an early hymn, the claim that Christ “was manifested in the flesh”6 — language that reads almost as if aimed in advance at the docetists John would confront. A word of candor is owed here: the letters to Timothy and Titus (the “Pastoral Epistles”) are the Pauline letters whose authorship critical scholars most often question, and a fair reader should know that.7 But the case for Christ’s humanity does not lean on them. It stands complete on the letters no one disputes — Galatians, Romans, 1 Corinthians, Philippians — with the Pastorals as corroboration, not foundation.

The last Adam: humanity at the center of the gospel

Where Paul dwells on Christ’s humanity most deliberately is his Adam theology. In 1 Corinthians 15 he sets the two representative men side by side: “For as by a man came death, by a man has come also the resurrection of the dead.” And more sharply still: “The first man Adam became a living being”; “the last Adam became a life-giving spirit” — “the first man was from the earth, a man of dust; the second man is from heaven.”8 The parallel he draws in Romans 5 turns on exactly this: as one man’s trespass brought condemnation, so “that one man Jesus Christ” brings the gift of grace.9 Christ can undo what Adam did only because he stands where Adam stood — as a real human being, the head of a renewed humanity.

A clarification matters here, and we borrow it from the companion essay on the Philippians 2 hymn, where it is worked out at length. Paul genuinely uses Adam–Christ typology, and it is one of his richest ways of asserting Christ’s true humanity. But the typology does not reduce Christ to a merely human second Adam. As N. T. Wright and Gordon Fee argue against James Dunn’s purely Adamic reading, the Philippians hymn describes one who was already “in the form of God” before “taking the form of a servant” and being “born in the likeness of men” — a transition Adam never made.10 So the second-Adam theme does two jobs at once, and we must let it do both: it establishes that Christ is authentically human, and it presupposes that the one who became the last Adam was more than Adam ever was. Full humanity, assumed by one who was already fully God.

The Gospels, Hebrews, and the creed agree

Paul is the spine, but the doctrine is grounded across the whole New Testament. The Gospels show a Jesus who grows (Luke 2:52), tires and sleeps, hungers and thirsts, weeps at a friend’s grave (John 11:35), sweats and bleeds, and dies a real bodily death that a Roman execution squad was expert at confirming. None of this is incidental scenery; it is the texture of a genuine human life.

The letter to the Hebrews makes the theology of it explicit, and it is the New Testament’s fullest statement of why the humanity is not negotiable. “Since therefore the children share in flesh and blood, he himself likewise partook of the same things”; therefore “he had to be made like his brothers in every respect, so that he might become a merciful and faithful high priest.”11 The logic is that of representation: a high priest must belong to the people he represents. And the same letter presses it toward comfort: “we do not have a high priest who is unable to sympathize with our weaknesses, but one who in every respect has been tempted as we are, yet without sin.”12 His temptations were real; his sinlessness was real; the two are held together, not played against each other.13

This is simply what the church has always confessed. The Nicene Creed runs its verbs at a sprint — “came down from heaven … was incarnate … was made man … was crucified … suffered … was buried” — and our essay on the creed notes what it insists upon: he was “born of a real mother, in a real body,” and went “all the way down — real pain, real death, a real grave.” In the fourth century Gregory of Nazianzus put it exactly: “What He was He continued to be; what He was not He took to Himself” — the incarnate Son added humanity and subtracted no deity.14 One plain historical detail seals the ordinariness of it. Paul, who knew the Jerusalem church, refers matter-of-factly to “James the Lord’s brother,” and to “the brothers of the Lord” as known figures in the early movement.15 Whatever one makes of their precise relation, the language assumes a real human family around a real human Jesus.

What the doctrine rules out

The early church did not hammer out the two-natures confession in the abstract; it did so by rejecting two opposite errors, and naming them is the clearest way to see what “fully human” guards.

Docetism (from the Greek dokeĩn, “to seem”) held that Christ only appeared to have a body and only seemed to suffer — the very error 1 John was written against. Ignatius of Antioch, on his way to martyrdom around AD 110, answered it with vehemence: Christ “was truly born” and “truly…nailed [to the cross] for us in His flesh,” and “suffered truly, even as also He truly raised up Himself, not, as certain unbelievers maintain, that He only seemed to suffer.”16 If Christ’s suffering was theater, Ignatius saw, then so was our salvation.

Apollinarianism was subtler and, for that reason, more instructive. Apollinaris of Laodicea granted Christ a human body but taught that the divine Word simply replaced the human mind or soul — a kind of divine intelligence driving a human body; the church condemned the view at the Council of Constantinople in 381. It sounds pious; it protects the deity. But it leaves Christ’s humanity a shell — the “divine robot” problem — and it prompted the most important single sentence in the whole debate. Gregory of Nazianzus replied: “that which He has not assumed He has not healed; but that which is united to His Godhead is also saved.”17 If the Son did not take a human mind, then the human mind — the very seat of our rebellion — is left unredeemed. To save the whole person, the Son had to become a whole person.

The Council of Chalcedon in 451 drew the boundary that has defined orthodox Christianity across Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant lines ever since: one and the same Christ, “truly God and truly man,” “consubstantial with us according to the manhood; in all things like unto us, without sin,” made known “in two natures, inconfusedly, unchangeably, indivisibly, inseparably” — that is, without confusion, change, division, or separation.18 Two full natures, one person — neither absorbed into the other, neither diminished by the other.

Why it matters

This is not a technicality for specialists. Three things hang on it, and the New Testament names each.

First, representation. Salvation in the biblical frame works through a substitute who stands in our place — “that one man Jesus Christ.” A savior who was not truly one of us could not truly stand in for us. Hebrews is blunt: he “had to be made like his brothers in every respect.” Second, a real death. God, Paul says, “for our sake … made him to be sin who knew no sin”;19 but only what is genuinely human can genuinely die. A phantom cannot bleed, and a phantom’s cross saves no one — which is exactly why Ignatius fought docetism as a gospel matter and not a quibble. Third, sympathy. The Christ who “in every respect has been tempted as we are” is able to “sympathize with our weaknesses” not by divine condescension observed from above, but from the inside, as one who has been here. Gregory’s rule cuts to the center of it: what is not assumed is not healed. The incarnation is God taking real flesh, not God in a costume — and only the real flesh saves.

The strongest objection, fairly stated

The most serious challenge to this essay comes not from someone who thinks Jesus was too divine, but from someone who thinks the emphasis cuts the other way. If you press the humanity this hard — born, tempted, ignorant of the hour (Mark 13:32), praying in agony, dying — have you not simply described a man? The skeptic presses the point: does not the full weight of the human evidence support the thesis that Jesus was a human being whom later Christians promoted to divinity? This is, in effect, the case Bart Ehrman makes in How Jesus Became God: that the earliest layers show an exalted human, and that full-blown incarnation is a later development.20 A sharper form runs through the ancient heresy of adoptionism — that God adopted the man Jesus as his Son, whether at his baptism or his resurrection — and through James Dunn’s influential argument that unambiguous pre-existence is clearest only in John’s Gospel.21 The objection deserves its full force: the humanity really is everywhere in the sources, and any honest account has to reckon with it rather than explain it away.

Here is the reply, and the concession comes first. We grant — gladly, since it is the whole point of this essay — that the human evidence is pervasive and real. What we do not grant is the inference that humanity competes with deity, so that the more human Jesus is shown to be, the less divine he can be. That inference is precisely what the two-natures confession denies, and it denies it on the strength of the same sources. The New Testament that shows Jesus hungry also has him receiving the worship and the divine name reserved for YHWH alone (the case laid out in Philippians 2 and the HANDS essay). The developmental story has to explain why our earliest material — the Philippians 2 passage (widely regarded as pre-Pauline), the reshaped Shema of 1 Corinthians 8:6 — already presupposes a divine Christ, rather than arriving at one late. And adoptionism founders on the same texts: Paul’s Son is sent “when the fullness of time had come,” not created or adopted en route; he is the one who was “in the form of God” before he took a servant’s form. The correct conclusion from the abundant humanity is not “therefore only a man,” but “therefore truly a man” — which is the very thing Chalcedon affirms alongside, not instead of, his deity.

A second, narrower objection targets the sources directly: since the Pastoral Epistles are widely thought to be post-Pauline, “the man Christ Jesus” and “was manifested in the flesh” should not count. The answer is the one already given: concede the disputed authorship, and notice that nothing in the argument depends on it. Galatians 4:4, Romans 8:3, 1 Corinthians 15, and the humanity of the Philippians hymn are all in the undisputed letters, and Hebrews and the Gospels stand entirely outside the Pastorals question. Remove 1 and 2 Timothy and Titus from the table and the case is unweakened.

The honest state of the question

Two clarifications keep this essay honest about what it does not settle. Both are places where thoughtful, orthodox Christians disagree, and this essay takes no side on either.

The first is impeccability. That Jesus was tempted and did not sin is not in dispute; Scripture states it plainly. But whether he was able to sin — the old distinction between posse non peccare (“able not to sin”) and non posse peccare (“not able to sin”) — is a genuine intramural debate with serious defenders on each side. What all orthodox parties affirm, and all this essay affirms, is that his temptations were real and his sinlessness was actual.22 The second is the relation of Jesus’ brothers to Mary, which touches a Marian question that has long divided Catholic and Orthodox Christians from Protestants; this essay draws no inference about it, resting only on the plain fact that the earliest sources speak of Jesus’ human family.

On the main claim, the picture is far less contested than the surrounding footnotes might suggest. That Jesus was a real first-century human being is common ground between confessing Christians and the great majority of secular historians; scholars who deny his deity almost all still affirm his humanity. The live scholarly debate is almost entirely about the other nature — how early, and how high, the confession of his deity runs. The humanity was never the church’s problem to prove. It was the church’s first thing to defend.

Conclusion: what it establishes, and what it does not

This essay does not, by itself, prove the deity of Christ — that case is made elsewhere, and it must be, because the two natures stand or fall together. Nor does it resolve every mystery in how one person can be at once infinite and finite, all-knowing and growing in wisdom; the creeds fenced that mystery rather than dissolving it. What it does establish is modest and immovable: the earliest and best sources present Jesus as a genuine human being — born of a woman, living under the law, taking real flesh, tempted, bleeding, dying — and the church has always confessed exactly that, over against every attempt to make him merely seem human. The Incarnation is not God wearing humanity like a garment he might slip off. It is the eternal Son making our nature his own, all the way down to the grave, so that what he assumed he might heal. A Christ who is not one of us cannot represent us, cannot die for us, and cannot sympathize with us. The gospel needs him to be, and joyfully confesses him to be, fully God and fully man.

Frequently asked questions

If Jesus is God, was he really fully human?

Yes. The historic confession is not that Jesus is God dressed up as a man, nor a blend of the two, but that the eternal Son took a complete human nature to himself without ceasing to be God: fully God and fully man, two natures in one person. Paul assumes it everywhere (“born of woman,” Galatians 4:4; “the man Christ Jesus,” 1 Timothy 2:5), and Hebrews states it flatly: he was “made like his brothers in every respect.” The humanity does not compete with the deity; both are affirmed together.

Doesn’t all the human evidence show Jesus was just a man later called God?

The human evidence is real, and Christians affirm it — that is the point of this doctrine. But it does not follow that humanity crowds out deity. The same early sources that show Jesus hungry and weeping also give him the worship and divine name reserved for God alone, and our earliest material (the Philippians 2 passage, the reshaped Shema of 1 Corinthians 8:6) already presupposes his deity rather than arriving at it late. The right conclusion from the abundant humanity is “therefore truly a man,” not “therefore only a man.”

Why does it matter whether Jesus was fully human?

Three things depend on it. Representation: a savior must belong to the people he stands in for (Hebrews 2:17). A real death: only what is genuinely human can genuinely die, so a Christ who merely seemed human saves no one — which is why the early church fought docetism as a gospel issue. And sympathy: the Christ who “in every respect has been tempted as we are” understands us from the inside (Hebrews 4:15). As Gregory of Nazianzus put it, “that which He has not assumed He has not healed.”
