Deep Dive Essay Pro

What the Early Church Actually Suffered

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

Ask most people what happened to Christians in the Roman Empire and you will get a fairly consistent picture: three centuries of relentless hunting, believers hiding in catacombs, lions in the Colosseum, an empire straining every nerve to stamp out a faith it could not kill.

Almost every element of that picture is wrong, or at least badly out of proportion. And the first duty of an honest essay on this subject is to say so—not as a reluctant concession dragged out under cross-examination, but at the front, because the exaggerated version is doing real damage. A believer who has been handed the Hollywood account will, sooner or later, meet a well-read sceptic who dismantles it, and will reasonably wonder what else he has been told that will not survive contact.

The actual record is patchier, stranger, and better documented than the legend. It is also sufficient to establish what this essay claims: that the cost of being a Christian was real, recurring, and backed by law.

What This Essay Is Not About

One boundary first. The deaths of the apostles themselves—Peter, Paul, James son of Zebedee, James the brother of Jesus—belong to a different argument, one about whether the founding witnesses were sincere rather than fraudulent, and they are treated with the necessary care in the companion essay on the origin of the disciples' belief. That essay also states the logical limit that governs this one and must never be blurred: martyrdom establishes sincerity, not truth. People die for false beliefs constantly. What dying rules out is deliberate fraud, and only for those in a position to know.

This essay picks up after the apostolic generation and runs to the early fourth century.

The Deflation, Stated at Full Strength

The most forceful modern challenge to the traditional picture is Candida Moss's The Myth of Persecution (2013), and her case deserves to be put at its strongest rather than waved at.1

Moss argues, first, that the familiar scheme of “ten great persecutions” is a later Christian construction, not a Roman policy—a tidy number imposed retrospectively on a messy record. Second, that for most of the first three centuries Christians were not being pursued at all: on her reckoning, imperial initiatives led to the execution of Christians for fewer than ten years in total across the whole period, and outside those windows Christians owned property, held meetings, and in some places held office. Third, that the martyr acts are largely later compositions, edited, expanded, and in some cases invented outright, so that the genre cannot be read as reportage. Fourth—and this is the claim that gives the book its title—that what happened was mostly prosecution rather than persecution: Christians were tried under general law for refusing a civic act, not hunted for holding a belief, and Roman authorities largely did not understand themselves to be suppressing a religion at all. And fifth, that the myth of relentless persecution has been put to bad use ever since.

That the ten-persecutions scheme is a later construction, and that Christians were not the object of a constant manhunt, are not really contested among specialists. The third is mainstream in principle—that the genre is late, edited and partly invented has been the scholarly position since Delehaye—though Moss presses it further than many who share it. The fourth is a serious conceptual argument, answered below; it is also where the strongest recent pushback lands, Wolfram Kinzig's 2021 study arguing that Christians were in fact serious and intended targets of the violence.2 The fifth is a claim about modern churches rather than ancient ones; it is the most contested part of her book, nothing in this essay turns on it, and it is not adjudicated here. An apologetic that needs the Hollywood version is an apologetic in trouble.

What the Record Actually Shows

Grant all of that, and a solid body of evidence remains—much of it from Roman officials rather than Christian storytellers.

Nero, AD 64 — and a live dispute

The earliest episode is reported not by a believer but by Tacitus, writing around 116 with undisguised contempt for the group in question. After the fire of Rome, Nero fastened the blame on “a class hated for their abominations, called Christians by the populace,” and Tacitus describes their deaths in detail—torn by dogs, crucified, burned as torches—adding that they were convicted less for arson than for odio humani generis, and that the cruelty was such that pity arose even though the victims were guilty and deserved exemplary punishment.3

A caveat belongs here rather than in a footnote, because this is the essay's first exhibit and it is the one a well-read reader will check. Brent Shaw has argued that the Tacitean evidence will not bear the weight placed on it—that Christians were not yet a distinct, identifiable target in 64 and that Tacitus read the hostility of his own day backwards. Christopher Jones replied that knowledge of Christians under that name could easily have reached Rome by the mid-60s; Shaw responded again. The exchange is unresolved.4 This is also where Moss herself is sharpest: Nero is the one item in this section she actively disputes, on much the same grounds. Nothing later in this essay depends on Nero, and it is set out here as the contested item it is. What the passage does support, if Tacitus is credited, is that Christians in Rome could be killed for belonging to the group—not that a legal category of “the name” yet existed, which is Pliny's question half a century later.

Trajan's rescript, c. AD 112: the legal shape of the whole period

The single most important document is the exchange between Pliny the Younger and the emperor Trajan. Pliny, unsure how to handle Christians brought before him, describes his procedure—he asks three times whether the accused are Christians, threatens execution, and executes the obstinate—and asks whether the name itself is the crime.5

Trajan's reply established the policy that held, with interruptions, for a century and a half. Christians are not to be sought out (conquirendi non sunt); anonymous denunciations are to be disregarded, as setting the worst sort of precedent and unworthy of the age; but if they are properly accused and convicted, and refuse to recant, they are to be punished.6

That is a precise and unusual legal position, and it cuts in two directions at once. In the church's favour: barring anonymous accusation meant an accuser had to give his name and expose himself to a charge of malicious prosecution, which is a real deterrent, and several historians read the rescript as substantially moderating. Against: the liability never went away. Christianity was not the object of a manhunt; it was a standing exposure that any neighbour, creditor or rival could turn lethal at a time of his choosing, and that was otherwise ignored. This is why Moss is right that most Christians most of the time were not being hunted, and why it does not follow that the risk was notional. A law enforced only when someone informs on you is not a mild law. It makes every relationship a potential exposure.

It is also the answer to Moss's central claim. Grant that Roman magistrates thought of themselves as enforcing public order rather than suppressing a religion. A defendant executed for refusing to sacrifice, where sacrificing would have ended the matter on the spot, still faces a religiously specific test—and Trajan's rescript makes recantation the whole hinge of the procedure, which is precisely what a charge of ordinary criminality would not do.

Local outbreaks

Within that framework, sporadic local violence recurs. Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, was executed in the mid-second century. The churches of Lyons and Vienne suffered mob violence in 177, described in a letter preserved by Eusebius. The Scillitan martyrs were condemned at Carthage in 180, and the surviving record is the earliest dated Christian document in Latin—in form a bare court transcript rather than a martyr narrative: brief, unadorned, mostly the proconsul's questions and the defendants' answers. Whether it is a lightly edited copy of the official acta or a Christian composition modelled on one is still argued, and a Christian redactor certainly supplied its ending; even on the sceptical reading it is a very different kind of object from the expanded legends of later centuries. Perpetua and Felicity died at Carthage in 203.7

It is worth noting what these four cases are, given the concession made above: all four are among the small group of accounts Moss herself judges the most authentic.8 They are used here only for the bare fact that the executions happened, not for narrative detail.

Decius, AD 250: the documentary turning point

The first genuinely empire-wide measure came under Decius, who in 250 required all inhabitants—not Christians specifically—to sacrifice to the gods and obtain a certificate, a libellus, attesting that they had done so before a commission. Jews appear to have been exempted. On the leading modern reading the decree was an attempt to reorganise the religious life of the empire rather than an anti-Christian measure, which makes its effect on Christians collateral and no less severe.9

What makes this decisive for the historian is that the certificates survive. Between forty and fifty libelli have been recovered, ordinary bureaucratic slips naming the petitioner, stating that he has always sacrificed and has now done so before the officials, and countersigned.10 These are not martyr stories; they are receipts from a filing system. They come entirely from Egypt, most of them from a single Fayum village, so what they establish beyond argument is that the requirement was issued, administered by local commissions, and generating paperwork ordinary people had to obtain. That the order was empire-wide is an inference from the decree's terms together with the literary record—Cyprian at Carthage, Dionysius at Alexandria, the Pionius material at Smyrna—and it is a secure one.11

Valerian and Diocletian

Valerian's edicts of 257 and 258 shifted tactics toward the leadership and the institution—targeting bishops and clergy, forbidding assembly, and confiscating property. The last and largest effort, the Great Persecution, was launched by Diocletian in 303: church buildings demolished, scriptures ordered surrendered and burned, clergy imprisoned, and eventually universal sacrifice required again. Diocletian himself abdicated in 305; the policy was carried on by Galerius and, in the East, by Maximinus Daia. It ran with varying force in different regions—it lapsed early in the West—until Galerius issued an edict of toleration in April 311, effectively conceding that it had failed; Maximinus renewed it in the East until 313.12

The Best Evidence Is the Church's Own Embarrassment

Here is the argument that does the most work, and it is not a story about heroism. It is not aimed at Moss, who grants that Decius and Diocletian happened; it is aimed at the flatter claim a reader is far likelier to meet online—that the persecutions are essentially a Christian invention.

The controversies that split churches institutionally in the third and early fourth centuries were not, in the first instance, disputes over the creed at all. They were about what to do with the Christians who had complied—the lapsi, the lapsed. After Decius, the churches of Carthage and Rome tore themselves apart over whether those who had sacrificed, or bought a certificate without sacrificing, could be readmitted, and on what terms; the rigorist Novatian split the Roman church over it and was set up as a rival bishop against Cornelius. After Diocletian, the North African church divided again over the traditores, the clergy who had handed over the scriptures, producing the Donatist schism that outlasted the empire in that region.13

Two things follow, and they pull in opposite directions, which is precisely why the evidence is good.

First, the persecutions were real. A church does not spend fifty years developing a discipline for apostates under pressure unless there was pressure and there were apostates. This is evidence of the kind hardest to fake: an institution's own records of a problem it would rather not have had.

Second, the persecutions were survivable, and many did not endure them. Large numbers of Christians sacrificed, or bribed their way to a certificate, or fled. The lapsed were a mass phenomenon, not a scattering of cowards. Any account that presents the early church as uniformly heroic is contradicted by the church's own crisis literature.

That second point is worth holding onto, because it is more useful pastorally than the legend it replaces. The early church was not made of a stronger grade of person. It was made of people who mostly wanted to live, some of whom did not comply, and it then had to work out how to forgive the ones who had. Where believers did stand firm, the tradition itself did not credit their own reserves: Paul had told the Philippians that it had been granted to them not only to believe but to suffer, and the confessors of these centuries are honoured as recipients rather than heroes.

What the Outsiders Said

Roman observers consistently found Christian willingness to die baffling and irritating rather than admirable, which is what makes their testimony useful.

Lucian of Samosata, writing satire, mocks the credulity of Christians who despise death and hold their possessions in common, so that any competent charlatan could grow rich among them—while confirming, in the mockery, both the readiness and the common purse.14 Galen, the physician, noted that Christians included individuals whose self-discipline and self-control reached a level not inferior to that of genuine philosophers, though he thought their reasoning worthless.15 Marcus Aurelius may belong here too: a passage in the Meditations contrasts the philosopher's reasoned readiness for death with mere obstinacy, and names the Christians—but that clause is widely judged to be a later reader's marginal note rather than Marcus's own, and if that judgement is right he never mentions Christians anywhere. It is offered as a possible witness, not a load-bearing one.16

Neither Lucian nor Galen is endorsing the faith. Galen alone concedes anything like admiration, and only for the conduct. What they share is that they confirm the behaviour while despising the reasons for it, which is exactly what makes them worth citing.

A Quotation to Get Right

Tertullian, writing his Apology around 197, produced the line every book on this subject quotes: usually rendered “the blood of the martyrs is the seed of the church.”

He did not write that. Addressing the persecutors directly, he wrote: Plures efficimur, quotiens metimur a vobis; semen est sanguis Christianorum—in the standard English of the Ante-Nicene Fathers, “The oftener we are mown down by you, the more in number we grow; the blood of Christians is seed.”17 The familiar version adds “of the church,” changes “Christians” to “martyrs,” and turns a taunt into a slogan.

The correction matters for two reasons. It is what the text says, and getting it right costs nothing. And Tertullian's actual claim—that persecution was causing growth—is a rhetorical boast by a partisan advocate in a polemical work, not a demographic finding. Persecution sometimes suppressed the church badly: the mass compliance under Decius that the lapsi controversy documents is the clearest case. Quote the line as evidence of what a Christian apologist said in 197. Do not quote it as evidence of what persecution actually did.

The Objection This Essay Cannot Answer

One challenge deserves stating because the record itself invites it, and because this essay does not defeat it.

Rome suppressed the Bacchanalia in 186 BC, banned the druidic religion, expelled Jews from the city under Tiberius and again under Claudius, and issued a rescript against the Manichaeans in 302.18 Christians were not uniquely targeted, and “a group was periodically illegal in Rome” is not by itself evidence of anything about that group. The honest reply is a concession: what the record in this essay establishes is cost, not distinctiveness. Why this particular movement grew under that cost when others did not is a different question; the mechanism of that growth is taken up in the companion essay, though it too declines to claim the church's charity was unique in kind.

A second point cuts the same way. Moss and others have pressed that a significant share of recorded martyrs sought or provoked death rather than merely accepting it—and the ancient testimony marshalled above bears on this directly, since Lucian's charlatan-among-the-credulous — and, if the naming clause is genuine, the obstinacy Marcus Aurelius records — are exactly what such behaviour would look like from outside. That is a real qualification on any argument from the martyrs' bearing, and it belongs on the table.

The Honest Verdict

Three statements, each bounded.

The popular Christian picture is false, and should be abandoned rather than defended. Persecution was intermittent, mostly local, and for most of the period legally constrained by a policy that forbade seeking Christians out—until the empire-wide measures from 250 abandoned that restraint. The martyr literature is largely a devotional genre and cannot in general be read as court reporting, though a small number of texts, the Scillitan record above all, stand closer to transcripts, and the difference matters.

The strong deflationary picture is also false. It is worth being precise about whose position that is: not Moss's, but the flatter version circulating downstream of her book, that the persecutions are essentially a Christian myth. What she grants is enough to refute it. She grants Trajan's rescript and its terms, the Decian order and the surviving certificates, and the Great Persecution under Diocletian. She disputes Nero, and she disputes intent throughout. Set Nero aside entirely, grant the intent argument in full, and there remains a recurring, state-backed capital liability—attested by Roman administrative correspondence, Egyptian papyri, hostile satirists and physicians, and the church's own records of mass failure under it.

And none of it proves Christianity true. That limit was set at the top of this essay and it holds at the bottom. What the record establishes is that for roughly two and a half centuries, in a society where the cost was real and periodically lethal, a movement with no legal protection kept recruiting.

The period closes with a change whose meaning Christians have never agreed on. Galerius conceded toleration in 311; the settlement at Milan followed in 313; and within a generation the church that had been prosecuted was meeting at Nicaea under imperial patronage. Whether that was a rescue or a domestication is a question this essay does not settle—the traditions divide on it, and it is not ours to adjudicate. What can be said is that the bishops who gathered at Nicaea were not a new establishment class: as the companion essay on the Trinity in the early church puts it, many of them bore the scars and missing eyes of the Great Persecution, and were hardly a docile assembly inclined to vote on imperial command.

Frequently asked questions

Wasn't the persecution of the early church mostly a myth?

No — though it is genuinely exaggerated in popular Christian retellings, and this essay concedes most of Candida Moss's case: the “ten persecutions” scheme is a later construction, most Christians most of the time were not being hunted, and the martyr stories are largely a devotional genre. What she grants is still enough to refute the flatter claim. She grants Trajan's rescript authorising execution for the name, the Decian order of 250 whose certificates survive on papyrus, and the Great Persecution launched by Diocletian in 303. She disputes Nero, and she disputes whether Rome intended to suppress a religion at all. Grant her both and a recurring, state-backed capital liability remains.

If Christians were being persecuted for three centuries, why were they able to own property and meet openly?

Because persecution was not a manhunt. Trajan's rescript of about AD 112 set the policy for the next century and a half: Christians were not to be sought out and anonymous accusations were to be ignored, but those properly accused and unrepentant were to be executed. That made Christianity a standing legal exposure rather than an object of pursuit — ignored most of the time, lethal the moment a neighbour or rival chose to denounce you. From 250 the empire-wide measures under Decius, Valerian and Diocletian abandoned that restraint.

Doesn't the willingness of the martyrs to die prove Christianity is true?

No, and the essay says so at both the top and the bottom. Martyrdom establishes sincerity, not truth — people die for false beliefs constantly. What dying rules out is deliberate fraud, and only for those in a position to know whether they were lying. That narrower argument concerns the apostles specifically and is made in the companion essay on the origin of the disciples' belief. The essay also concedes a real qualification that Moss and others have pressed: voluntary martyrdom — believers who sought or provoked death rather than merely accepting it — was more common than the later tradition admitted.
