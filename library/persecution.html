What the Early Church Actually Suffered

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

Ask most people what happened to Christians in the Roman Empire and you will get a fairly consistent picture: three centuries of relentless hunting, believers hiding in catacombs, lions in the Colosseum, an empire straining every nerve to stamp out a faith it could not kill.

Almost every element of that picture is wrong, or at least badly out of proportion. And the first duty of an honest essay on this subject is to say so—not as a reluctant concession dragged out under cross-examination, but at the front, because the exaggerated version is doing real damage. A believer who has been handed the Hollywood account will, sooner or later, meet a well-read sceptic who dismantles it, and will reasonably wonder what else he has been told that will not survive contact.

The actual record is patchier, stranger, and better documented than the legend. It is also, in the ways that matter for an argument, entirely sufficient.

What This Essay Is Not About

One boundary first. The deaths of the apostles themselves—Peter, Paul, James son of Zebedee, James the brother of Jesus—belong to a different argument, one about whether the founding witnesses were sincere rather than fraudulent, and they are treated with the necessary care in the companion essay on the origin of the disciples' belief. That essay also states the logical limit that governs this one and must never be blurred: martyrdom establishes sincerity, not truth. People die for false beliefs constantly. What dying rules out is deliberate fraud, and only for those in a position to know.

This essay picks up after the apostolic generation and runs to the early fourth century—the period in which being a Christian was, on and off, a capital offence, and in which the church nevertheless became too large to suppress.

The Deflation, Stated at Full Strength

The most forceful modern challenge to the traditional picture is Candida Moss's The Myth of Persecution (2013), and her case deserves to be put at its strongest rather than waved at.1

Moss argues, first, that the familiar scheme of “ten great persecutions” is a later Christian construction, not a Roman policy—a tidy number imposed retrospectively on a messy record. Second, that for most of the first three centuries Christians were not being pursued at all; the periods of active, state-driven persecution amount to a small fraction of the era, and outside them Christians owned property, held meetings, and in some places held office. Third, that the martyr acts—the accounts of individual deaths on which the popular picture rests—are largely later compositions, edited, expanded, and in some cases invented outright, so that the genre cannot be read as reportage. And fourth, that the myth of relentless persecution has been put to bad use ever since, licensing a persecution complex in churches that are in fact comfortable and powerful.

Every one of those four points has substance, and the first three are broadly accepted by specialists who disagree with her framing. An apologetic that needs the Hollywood version is an apologetic in trouble.

What the Record Actually Shows

Grant all of that, and a solid body of evidence remains—much of it from Roman officials rather than Christian storytellers.

Nero, AD 64

The earliest episode is reported not by a believer but by Tacitus, writing around 116 with undisguised contempt for the group in question. After the fire of Rome, Nero fastened the blame on “a class hated for their abominations, called Christians by the populace,” and Tacitus describes their deaths in detail—torn by dogs, crucified, burned as torches—adding that they were convicted less for arson than for “hatred of the human race,” and that the cruelty was such that it produced sympathy even among people who thought them guilty.2 This was local to Rome and short-lived. It is also a hostile source confirming both that Christians existed in numbers in the capital three decades after the crucifixion, and that they could be killed for the name.

Trajan's rescript, c. AD 112: the legal shape of the whole period

The single most important document is the exchange between Pliny the Younger and the emperor Trajan. Pliny, unsure how to handle Christians brought before him, describes his procedure—he asks three times whether the accused are Christians, threatens execution, and executes the obstinate—and asks whether the name itself is the crime.3

Trajan's reply established the policy that held, with interruptions, for a century and a half. Christians are not to be sought out (conquirendi non sunt); anonymous accusations are to be disregarded as setting “a very bad example, unworthy of our age”; but if they are properly accused and convicted, and refuse to recant, they are to be punished.4

That is a precise and unusual legal position, and it is worth pausing on because it explains the whole shape of the era. Christianity was not the object of a manhunt. It was a standing liability—a status that could destroy you at any moment if a neighbour, a creditor, or a rival chose to denounce you, and that was otherwise ignored. This is why Moss is right that most Christians most of the time were not being hunted, and why it does not follow that the risk was notional. A law that is enforced only when someone informs on you is not a mild law. It makes every relationship a potential exposure.

Local outbreaks

Within that framework, sporadic local violence recurs. Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, was executed in the mid-second century. The churches of Lyons and Vienne suffered a mob-driven pogrom in 177, described in a letter preserved by Eusebius. The Scillitan martyrs were condemned at Carthage in 180—the surviving court record is the earliest dated Christian document in Latin, and it is a transcript, not a legend. Perpetua and Felicity died at Carthage in 203.5

Decius, AD 250: the documentary turning point

The first genuinely empire-wide measure came under Decius, who in 250 required all inhabitants—not Christians specifically—to sacrifice to the gods and obtain a certificate, a libellus, attesting that they had done so before a commission.

What makes this decisive for the historian is that the certificates survive. Roughly forty-odd libelli have been recovered from the sands of Egypt: ordinary bureaucratic slips, naming the petitioner, stating that he has always sacrificed and has now done so in the presence of the officials, and countersigned.6 These are not martyr stories. They are papyrus receipts from a filing system, and they establish beyond argument that an empire-wide loyalty test was imposed and enforced, and that people had to produce proof of compliance.

Valerian and Diocletian

Valerian's edicts of 257 and 258 shifted tactics toward the leadership and the institution—targeting bishops and clergy, forbidding assembly, and confiscating property. The last and largest effort, the so-called Great Persecution, began under Diocletian in 303: church buildings demolished, scriptures ordered surrendered and burned, clergy imprisoned, and eventually universal sacrifice required again. It ran, with varying force in different regions, until Galerius issued an edict of toleration in 311, effectively conceding that the policy had failed.7

The Best Evidence Is the Church's Own Embarrassment

Here is the argument that does the most work, and it is not a story about heroism.

The bitterest internal controversies of the third and early fourth centuries were not about doctrine. They were about what to do with the Christians who had complied—the lapsi, the lapsed. After Decius, the churches of Carthage and Rome tore themselves apart over whether those who had sacrificed, or bought a certificate without sacrificing, could be readmitted, and on what terms; the rigorist Novatian broke with Rome over it. After Diocletian, the North African church split again over the traditores, the clergy who had handed over the scriptures, producing the Donatist schism that outlasted the empire in that region.8

Two things follow, and they pull in opposite directions, which is precisely why the evidence is good.

First, the persecutions were real. A church does not spend fifty years developing a penitential apparatus for apostates under pressure unless there was pressure and there were apostates. This is evidence of exactly the kind that is hardest to fake: an institution's internal administrative wreckage, preserved in its own records, about a problem it would rather not have had.

Second, the persecutions were survivable, and many did not endure them. Large numbers of Christians sacrificed, or bribed their way to a certificate, or fled. The lapsed were a mass phenomenon, not a scattering of cowards. Any account that presents the early church as uniformly heroic is contradicted by the church's own crisis literature.

That second point is worth holding onto, because it is more useful pastorally than the legend it replaces. The early church was not made of a stronger grade of person. It was made of people who mostly wanted to live, some of whom did not comply, and which then had to work out how to forgive the ones who had.

What the Outsiders Said

Roman observers consistently found Christian willingness to die baffling and irritating rather than impressive, which is what makes their testimony useful. Marcus Aurelius, in his private notebook, contrasts the philosopher's reasoned readiness for death with what he takes to be the Christians' mere obstinacy—theatrical, unreflective stubbornness.9 Lucian of Samosata, writing satire, mocks the credulity of Christians who despise death and hold their possessions in common, so that any competent charlatan could grow rich among them—while confirming, in the mockery, both the readiness and the common purse.10 Galen, the physician, noted with a shrug that Christians behaved with a discipline usually reserved for genuine philosophers, though he thought their reasoning worthless.11

None of these men is paying a compliment. All three confirm the behaviour.

A Quotation to Get Right

Tertullian, writing his Apology around 197, produced the line every book on this subject quotes: usually rendered “the blood of the martyrs is the seed of the church.”

He did not write that. What he wrote, addressing the persecutors directly, is closer to: we multiply every time you mow us down; the blood of Christians is seed.12 The familiar version adds “of the church,” changes “Christians” to “martyrs,” and turns a taunt into a slogan.

The correction matters for two reasons. It is simply what the text says, and getting it right costs nothing. And Tertullian's actual claim—that persecution was causing growth—is a rhetorical boast by a partisan advocate in a polemical work, not a demographic finding. Persecution sometimes suppressed the church badly, particularly in North Africa under Diocletian. Quote the line as evidence of what a Christian apologist said in 197. Do not quote it as evidence of what persecution actually did.

The Honest Verdict

So where does this land? Three statements, each bounded.

The popular Christian picture is false, and should be abandoned rather than defended. Persecution was intermittent, mostly local, legally constrained by a policy that forbade seeking Christians out, and interspersed with long stretches of ordinary life. The martyr literature is a devotional genre and cannot be read as court reporting.

The strong deflationary picture is also false, and it is the overcorrection a reader is more likely to meet today. Moss's own case does not require it: she does not deny that Nero killed Christians in Rome, that Trajan authorised their execution for the name, that Decius imposed an empire-wide sacrifice test whose paperwork we still possess, or that Diocletian tried for eight years to destroy the church's books, buildings, and clergy. What she denies is continuity, scale, and the reliability of the hagiography. Grant her all three, and a genuine, recurring, state-backed capital liability remains—attested by Roman administrative documents, Egyptian papyri, hostile historians, and the church's own records of mass failure under it.

And none of it proves Christianity true. That limit was set at the top of this essay and it holds at the bottom. What the record establishes is that for roughly two and a half centuries, in a society where the cost was real and periodically lethal, a movement with nothing to offer in the way of worldly advantage kept recruiting—and that the reasons it grew are the subject of a separate argument, not of this one.

The period closes with an irony worth naming rather than celebrating. Galerius conceded toleration in 311; the settlement at Milan followed in 313; and within a generation the church that had been prosecuted was being convened by an emperor at Nicaea to settle its own disputes—a change whose doctrinal side is treated in the essay on the Trinity in the early church. What Rome could not accomplish by killing Christians, it would shortly begin to accomplish, in a quite different sense, by promoting them. That is not a happy ending. It is the beginning of a harder problem, and Christians have been arguing about it ever since.

Frequently asked questions

Wasn't the persecution of the early church mostly a myth?

It is genuinely exaggerated in popular Christian retellings, and this essay concedes Candida Moss's main points: the “ten persecutions” scheme is a later construction, most Christians most of the time were not being hunted, and the martyr stories are a devotional genre rather than reportage. But the deflation is often overstated in turn. Moss does not deny that Nero killed Christians, that Trajan authorised execution for the name, that Decius imposed an empire-wide sacrifice test whose certificates survive on papyrus, or that Diocletian spent eight years trying to destroy the church's books and clergy.

If Christians were being persecuted for three centuries, why were they able to own property and meet openly?

Because persecution was not a manhunt. Trajan's rescript of about AD 112 set the policy for the next century and a half: Christians were not to be sought out and anonymous accusations were to be ignored, but those properly accused and unrepentant were to be executed. That made Christianity a standing legal liability rather than an object of pursuit—ignored most of the time, lethal the moment a neighbour or rival chose to denounce you.

Doesn't the willingness of the martyrs to die prove Christianity is true?

No, and the essay says so at both the top and the bottom. Martyrdom establishes sincerity, not truth—people die for false beliefs constantly. What dying rules out is deliberate fraud, and only for those in a position to know whether they were lying. That narrower argument concerns the apostles specifically and is made in the companion essay on the origin of the disciples' belief.
