What the Early Church Suffered — and Why It Held

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

Sometime around the year 112, the Roman governor of Bithynia sat in judgment over people accused of a strange new religion. He was a conscientious man, a friend of the emperor, and he had a problem: he did not know what the crime was. So he did what a careful administrator does. He asked them, on pain of death, whether they were Christians. If they said yes, he asked again. And a third time. Those who kept saying yes, he sent to execution — not, he admitted, because he had proved them guilty of anything, but because “whatever it was they were confessing, their stubbornness and inflexible obstinacy surely deserved to be punished.”1

That governor was Pliny the Younger, and his letter to the emperor Trajan is the earliest surviving Roman government document on how to handle Christians. It matters here for one reason above all others: the people in front of him could have walked free at any moment. A pinch of incense before the emperor’s image, a prayer to the Roman gods, a curse on the name of Christ — Pliny offered all of it, and released everyone who took the offer.1 The ones who died, died because they would not. This is the fact the whole story turns on. The early Christians were not victims of a manhunt they could not escape. They were people who were handed an exit and refused to take it.

The name itself was the crime

Trajan’s reply to Pliny became the working policy of the empire for over a century, and its logic is chilling in its simplicity. Christians, the emperor ruled, were not to be hunted down (conquirendi non sunt); anonymous accusations were to be ignored as beneath “the spirit of our age.” But anyone properly accused and convicted, who would not recant, was to be punished.2 Read it slowly. There is no charge of arson, sedition, or any specific act. The offence is the name — being a Christian, and admitting it out loud. Pliny had wondered whether it was “the name itself” that should be punished, or only crimes attached to the name; Trajan’s answer, in effect, was: the name.1

This is what gives the early Christian witness its peculiar edge. To be delivered from danger, a believer did not have to renounce Christ in his heart or betray anyone else. He had only to perform a small public gesture — and refuse to say three words, “Christ is Lord,” in reverse. The steady stream of people who would not perform that gesture is the phenomenon the rest of this essay is about.

The certificates in the sand

We are not depending on pious legend for any of this. In the year 250 the emperor Decius issued an empire-wide order: every inhabitant was to sacrifice to the gods and obtain a signed, witnessed certificate — a libellus — proving he had done so.3 It was the first genuinely systematic imperial test of loyalty, and it fell on Christians like a hammer, because sacrificing was precisely the thing they could not do. Roughly forty-six of these certificates have been recovered from the dry sands of Egypt, on papyrus, and published.3 They are mundane, bureaucratic, and utterly damning. One reads like a tax receipt: so-and-so has “always sacrificed to the gods,” and “now, in your presence, in accordance with the edict, I have sacrificed and poured a libation and tasted the offerings,” with a request that the official countersign.3

The certificates prove that the pressure was real and mortal — real enough, indeed, that complying was a genuine temptation, and some did comply. But that is exactly what throws the others into relief. The edict was universal: every inhabitant, Christian or not, had to sacrifice and hold the receipt. It fell on Christians alone as an impossible demand — and behind every surviving libellus that a Christian signed stands the unwritten record of those who would not sign, and faced the magistrate instead.

The named witnesses

And we can name them. Not all of them — most died anonymous — but enough, in accounts sober and specific enough that even the historians most sceptical of martyr literature single them out as the earliest and best-attested we possess.

The oldest is Polycarp of Smyrna, an old bishop who as a young man had known people who had known the apostles. Around 155 or 156 he was arrested and pressed by the proconsul to swear by the emperor’s fortune and curse Christ. His reply is one of the oldest recorded sentences of Christian courage: “Eighty and six years have I served Him, and He never did me any injury: how then can I blaspheme my King and my Saviour?”4 He was burned in the stadium. The letter his church wrote to describe it — on the traditional dating, within a year of his death — is the earliest surviving account of a Christian martyrdom.4

The Scillitan martyrs — seven men and five women from a town in North Africa — were tried at Carthage on 17 July 180. The court record survives, and it is the earliest dated document of the Latin-speaking church.5 Offered thirty days to reconsider, their spokesman Speratus answered simply that in a matter so plain there was nothing to think over. Asked what he kept in his satchel, he said: the letters of Paul. They were beheaded.5

Then there is Perpetua. In 203, at Carthage, a young, well-born nursing mother named Vibia Perpetua was imprisoned with a group of catechumens, among them her pregnant slave, Felicity. Part of the account that survives is Perpetua’s own prison diary — the earliest known writing by a Christian woman — and it records her father begging her, again and again, to think of her baby and simply deny the name.6 She would not. Master and slave, the noblewoman and the servant girl, went into the arena together. The record of a slave and a matron dying side by side for the same Lord is itself a quiet revolution.

A generation earlier, in 177, a savage local outbreak at Lyons in Gaul produced a letter so vivid that the historian Eusebius copied it out whole.7 Its hero is not a bishop or a scholar but Blandina, a slave girl her fellow Christians feared would be too frail to hold out. She outlasted them all — tortured until her executioners gave up exhausted, then hung on a stake, then wrapped in a net and thrown to a bull — and the letter says the others watching “saw in the form of their sister him who was crucified for them.”7 Rome kept assuming its cruelty would find the weakest link. Again and again the weakest link held.

What Rome could not understand

Here is the strongest part of the evidence, and it comes not from Christians but from their cultured despisers. Pagan observers watched all this and found it genuinely baffling — and they said so in writing.

The satirist Lucian of Samosata had no love for the Christians; he thought them gullible. But even mocking them he recorded what he saw: “The poor wretches have convinced themselves, first and foremost, that they are going to be immortal and live for all time, in consequence of which they despise death and even willingly give themselves into custody.”8 The physician Galen — the most famous doctor of the ancient world — noted the same thing more admiringly. In a passage that survives only in Arabic translation, he remarks that the followers of Christ include people whose “contempt of death is patent to us every day,” along with a self-discipline in food and conduct “not inferior to that of genuine philosophers.”9 The emperor Marcus Aurelius, in his private notebook, seems to gesture at the same stubbornness, wishing that a soul ready to die would meet it after reasoned reflection and not, he says, out of “sheer opposition, like the Christians” — though scholars widely and rightly regard the words “like the Christians” as a later reader’s note that crept into the text, so this witness must be handled with care.10

Notice what the hostile sources concede. They do not deny the courage; they cannot explain it. To a Roman, a willingness to die for a conviction, quietly and without a sword in your hand, was not heroism but a kind of madness — and that incomprehension is worth more than a hundred friendly testimonials. Even the earliest and most brutal episode, Nero’s scapegoating of the Christians after the great fire of 64, is known to us mainly through the pagan historian Tacitus, who despised the sect yet recorded that “an immense multitude” was condemned, convicted less of arson than of “hatred of the human race,” and put to death with a cruelty that stirred even Roman pity.11 (Some historians, following Brent Shaw, now doubt whether that particular episode targeted Christians as Christians so early; others, like Christopher Jones, defend the traditional reading. The debate is real and worth knowing — but the firmer ground is Pliny onward, and there the pattern is not in dispute.12)

“The blood of Christians is seed”

It was in that atmosphere, around the year 197, that the North African lawyer Tertullian flung his famous line at Rome. It is almost always misquoted. He did not write “the blood of the martyrs is the seed of the church.” What he wrote is sharper: “The oftener we are mown down by you, the more in number we grow; the blood of Christians is seed.”13 It was a taunt and a boast, addressed to the very officials doing the mowing.

We should be honest about what that sentence is and is not. It is a partisan’s rhetoric, not a demographic law; persecution did not mechanically multiply believers, and the church grew for many reasons in seasons of peace as well as pressure. But as a report of something Tertullian could see with his own eyes — a movement that executions were failing to extinguish, that kept producing more Speratuses and Blandinas than the courts could silence — it was simply true. A faith that its enemies could not kill out of existence, and that its own adherents would not buy their way out of, is a historical fact that wants explaining.

The honest objection — and why the core still stands

That fact has to be stated carefully, because popular Christian memory has often overstated it, and a serious scholar has called the bluff. The historian Candida Moss, in The Myth of Persecution, argues that the cherished picture of a church under relentless, three-century assault — the tidy scheme of “ten great persecutions,” an unbroken “Age of Martyrs” — is a later construction. Specialists broadly agree with her on this, and it is worth conceding plainly: for most of these centuries most Christians in most places were not being hunted. Persecution was usually episodic and often local — a hostile governor here, a mob there, and only under Decius (250), Valerian (257–258), and Diocletian (from 303) anything like an empire-wide, top-down campaign.14 Some later martyr legends are embroidered, and a few are fiction.

But look closely at what this concession does and does not touch. It trims the frequency and the legend; it leaves the core exactly where it was. Moss herself singles out a small set of accounts — among them Polycarp, the Scillitan martyrs, the letter of Lyons, and Perpetua and Felicity — as the earliest and closest to authentic records.15 She disputes how much of their narrative detail is later shaping, and on that she has a real point. But even she does not deny that these people really died rather than renounce Christ — and that bare fact, not the embellished detail, is all the core needs. The point of the sceptical correction was never that no one died — it was that the deaths were rarer, and the danger more intermittent, than the legends implied. And a danger that is intermittent is still a danger. That the sword fell occasionally rather than daily did not make it less sharp for Polycarp, or give Perpetua’s father a weaker case. As the historian Wolfram Kinzig has recently urged in reply to the deflationary mood, the sources will not let us shrink the persecutions to nothing; the martyrs were real people who really died, and the courage was not a myth.16 The steelman, honestly weighed, leaves the documented core standing — and the core is enough.

What this does, and does not, prove

Two honest limits, and then the point they lead to. First: dying for a belief proves sincerity, not truth. People have laid down their lives for causes that were mistaken, and courage is not a truth-detector. What martyrdom does rule out is cynical pretence. These were not people play-acting a faith for gain; you do not go to the beasts to keep up an act. Their deaths prove that these Christians sincerely believed what they had been taught — that Jesus is Lord and risen — and held it with their eyes open. (Whether the first witnesses could have died for something they knew to be false is a separate and even sharper question, and it belongs with the evidence for the resurrection, argued elsewhere in this Library.)

Second: the case that Christianity is true does not rest here. It rests, above all, on the resurrection of Jesus — and that case is made, at length and with force, elsewhere in this Library. This page is not the argument for the faith; it is a window onto what that conviction cost the people who held it first. Watch what they were willing to pay, and then go and weigh why they were so sure.

Because in the end the martyrs did not think of their courage as their own achievement. Writing from his own imprisonment, the apostle Paul told the Philippians that it had been “granted” to them — as a gift — not only to believe in Christ but also to suffer for his sake.17 That is how the early church spoke of its confessors: not as supermen who summoned heroism out of themselves, but as ordinary people — slaves, mothers, aging bishops, teenagers — who were given the grace to stand when standing cost everything, and who found, as Blandina’s friends found watching her, that they were not standing alone. The blood was seed because the field was not theirs. And the same Lord they refused to deny is the one still asking, gently, of everyone who reads their story: will you follow me?

Frequently asked questions

Were Christians really persecuted for 300 years straight?

No — and honest history says so. For most of the first three centuries, most Christians in most places were not being actively hunted; persecution was usually episodic and local, and only three emperors (Decius in 250, Valerian in 257–258, and Diocletian from 303) mounted anything like an empire-wide campaign. The popular scheme of “ten great persecutions” is a later simplification. But the correction cuts the frequency, not the fact: to be a known Christian was legally to risk death for the whole period, and men, women and slaves paid that price rather than deny Christ.

Doesn’t the fact that people died for it prove Christianity is true?

Not by itself. People die for beliefs that turn out to be false, so martyrdom proves sincerity, not truth. What it does rule out is cynical pretence — you do not face the beasts to keep up an act. So the martyrs show, at minimum, that these Christians genuinely believed Jesus was risen and Lord, and were no cynics. Whether the very first witnesses could have died for a story they knew to be false — a still sharper question — belongs with the evidence for the resurrection, argued elsewhere in this Library.

How do we know these martyr stories aren’t just legends?

Some later ones are embroidered, and a few are fiction — we should say so. But a core of accounts is singled out even by sceptical specialists as the earliest and best evidence we have: the Martyrdom of Polycarp, the Acts of the Scillitan Martyrs, the letter describing the martyrs of Lyons, and the prison diary of Perpetua, among others. Critics like Candida Moss dispute how much of the narrative detail is later shaping — but not that these people really died refusing to deny Christ. And the general picture is corroborated by hostile Roman writers: Pliny and Tacitus record the executions, Lucian and Galen the Christians’ startling readiness to face them.
