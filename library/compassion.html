Who Taught the World to Care for the Weak?

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

Ask almost anyone today how to judge a society, and a particular answer will surface without much thought: look at how it treats its weakest members—the sick, the destitute, the disabled, the prisoner, the unwanted child. We reach for that test as if it were written into the human mind, the way “two and two make four” is written there. It feels less like a moral position than a moral instinct.

It is not an instinct. It is an inheritance—and a strange, hard-won, historically rare one. For most of recorded history most people did not believe it, and the sophisticated civilisations that gave us geometry, tragedy, law and philosophy emphatically did not. In the ancient Mediterranean world, care flowed toward the strong and the useful, and worth was something you earned by your strength, your status, or your usefulness to the city. The idea that a human being has a claim on your compassion precisely when he can give you nothing back would have struck a thoughtful Roman not as obvious but as perverse. This essay is about where that idea came from, why it once seemed mad, and what it built.

A World That Cared for the Useful

Start where the pressure falls hardest: on the newborn who is not wanted. In the ancient Mediterranean, a father was not obliged to raise every child born in his house. An unwanted infant—deformed, sickly, female, or simply one mouth too many—could be “exposed,” left out to die or be taken by slavers. And this was not the furtive vice of bad men; it was recommended by the best of them. In Plato’s ideal republic, the offspring of inferior parents, and any others “born defective,” are to be “properly disposed of in secret, so that no one will know what has become of them.”1 Aristotle is more clinical still: “As to the exposure and rearing of children, let there be a law that no deformed child shall live.”2

The medical literature simply assumes it. Soranus of Ephesus, the leading gynaecologist of the second century, opens his discussion of the care of the newborn not with how to nurture the child but with how to recognise which children are “worth rearing” in the first place—the vigorous, sound, well-formed infant—adding that “by conditions contrary to those mentioned, the infant not worth rearing is recognised.”3 The phrase is worth pausing on. A newborn had to qualify. Its worth was a judgement to be rendered, not a status it simply possessed.

This same logic ran upward through the society. There were places in the Roman world where the sick were tended—the valetudinaria, the infirmaries attached to army camps and to some large slave estates. But notice whom they served: soldiers, whose bodies were the empire’s instruments, and slaves, who were valuable property. Care was an investment in the useful, not a debt owed to the weak. Even the state’s charity ran this way: the imperial alimenta fed freeborn Italian children as the empire’s future citizens and soldiers, not the destitute as destitute. None of this made the ancients monsters; they loved their children, honoured their benefactors, and could be moved to pity. The point is narrower and stranger: nowhere in that Greco-Roman world was there a settled conviction that the weak, as the weak, had first claim on the strong—nor any movement, law, or institution built on that claim.

The Honest Objection: Compassion Is Anti-Natural

Before asking what changed this, we should let the sharpest critic of the change speak, because he understood it better than most of its friends do. The objection is not that caring for the weak is wrong in some petty sense. It is that caring for the weak is, in a hard biological sense, anti-natural: it preserves exactly what an unsentimental nature would let perish, and it spends the strong on the survival of the unfit.

Friedrich Nietzsche saw this with total clarity, and he did not flinch. In The Anti-Christ he wrote that “pity thwarts the whole law of evolution, which is the law of natural selection. It preserves whatever is ripe for destruction… by maintaining life in so many of the botched of all kinds, it gives life itself a gloomy and dubious aspect.”4 And he named the culprit exactly: “Christianity has taken the part of all the weak, the low, the botched; it has made an ideal out of antagonism to all the self-preservative instincts of sound life.”5

Here is the uncomfortable thing a Christian should say plainly: on the descriptive facts, Nietzsche is right. Christianity is the great historical champion of the weak, and that championing does cut against the grain of a nature “red in tooth and claw.” He has correctly identified what the movement did. Where the argument goes wrong is at a single, decisive joint—and it is a joint the naturalist cannot repair from his own materials.

Nietzsche calls selection a “law,” and lets the word do two jobs at once. In one sense “the law of selection” is a description: it reports what tends to happen when the strong are unconstrained. In the other sense he wants it to be a prescription: a command to stop propping up the weak and let nature take its course. But a description of what happens carries no instruction about what we ought to do—the gap David Hume named long ago, that no “ought” follows from an “is” without some further premise smuggled in.6 That nature culls the weak is a fact. That we should is not a fact of the same kind, and it cannot be squeezed out of the first one. To get from “the strong outlast the weak” to “therefore help them do so”—which is the genocidal turn, the one Heinrich Himmler would later make explicit when he told his officers that “there is nothing particular about man; he is but a part of this world”7—you need a value that stands above nature and passes judgement on it. And that is precisely what pure naturalism has, by its own account, ruled out. It can tell you what survives. It cannot tell you that survival is good, or that the weak may be discarded, or that they may not. To forbid the atrocity you must appeal to a moral order that nature does not contain. This is a point about logic, not biography: Nietzsche himself, who held anti-Semitism and nationalist politics in contempt, did not take that step. The claim is only that once “man is merely a part of nature” is treated as a command rather than a description, nothing within nature forbids where it can be taken.

So the objection, pressed to the end, does not defeat Christian compassion. It exposes its foundation. Compassion for the weak really is a rebellion against nature—and the only thing that can justify a rebellion against nature is a standard higher than nature. The question the naturalist cannot dodge is where that standard could possibly come from.

What Changed the Reflex

Two convictions, working together, changed it, and the first did not begin with Christianity. That every human being is made “in the image of God” (Genesis 1:26–27) is a Jewish doctrine, older than the church and standing on its own feet: worth is conferred by the Creator, not earned by usefulness, so it cannot be forfeited by weakness. On this account the deformed newborn Aristotle would not rear and the slave the valetudinarium would not have bothered with carry the same stamp as the emperor. The measure of a person is no longer what he can do. It is whose image he bears. And Israel’s law had already begun turning that conferred worth into concrete obligation—gleaning left in the field for the poor and the stranger (Leviticus 19:9–10), a tithe set aside for the widow, the orphan and the sojourner (Deuteronomy 14:28–29), a God who “executes justice for the fatherless and the widow, and loves the sojourner” (Deuteronomy 10:18)—the practice Christianity would carry out of its Jewish inheritance and into the Gentile world.

The second conviction is what Christianity welded to the first and carried out into the Gentile world: that God himself had stooped to the bottom of the human heap. The Gospels reach for a startling word to describe how Jesus met the wretched. Where we might say he “felt sorry” for someone, they say he was moved with splanchnizomai—a verb from splanchna, the guts, the visceral churn of compassion. He is “moved with compassion” for two blind beggars (Matthew 20:34); he stretches out his hand to a leper, the untouchable of untouchables, because he is “moved with compassion” (Mark 1:41); he stops a funeral for a widow’s only son because “he had compassion on her” (Luke 7:13); and in the parable it is the despised Samaritan, not the priest, who is “moved with compassion” for the man in the ditch (Luke 10:33).8 The direction of the feeling is the whole point: it runs down and out, toward the ones with nothing to offer.

And Jesus fused this compassion to his own identity in a way with no ancient parallel. Whatever you did “for one of the least of these,” he said, “you did for me” (Matthew 25:40).9 The hungry, the sick, the imprisoned stranger were not merely worthy of charity; they were where the King himself could be found. A civilisation that came to believe that could no longer say, with a clear conscience, that the humiliated are contemptible.

What the Conviction Built

Convictions can be sentimental and change nothing. This one built institutions, and the institutions are the evidence. Early Christian communities took in the infants their neighbours exposed and organised the care of the sick and the poor—including, as the emperor Julian would later complain when he tried to revive paganism against them, the poor who were not their own.10 They cared for the sick as the sick, not as assets.

The clearest institutional fruit is the hospital, and here the claim has to be made carefully, because it is easy to overstate and skeptics rightly pounce on the overstatement. Christians did not invent medicine; the Greeks had done that. Nor did they invent the healing shrine or the military infirmary. What has no clear precedent in the ancient world is the hospital in the fuller sense: a permanent, charitably endowed foundation open to the sick poor as such, and staffed to care for them. The standard early example is the complex Basil of Caesarea established outside that city around 372. Historians such as Timothy Miller and Andrew Crislip argue for genuine institutional innovation here; others, notably Peregrine Horden and Vivian Nutton, urge caution about how sharp and how early the break really was.11 The honest verdict is the bounded one: not that Christians invented all care, but that institutional priority in the charitable care of the sick poor is what survives that debate.

The same downward pull reached the arena. To the Roman public, watching men and beasts kill one another was ordinary entertainment; to Christians it was the celebration of exactly the un-personing they rejected. They refused to attend. The apologist Minucius Felix has his Christian spokesman explain that believers “abstain from… your pomps and exhibitions,” whose bloody enticements they know and condemn.12 The boycott was, for centuries, a minority protest. But the tradition remembered a moment when it broke through: around the year 404 a monk named Telemachus is said to have leapt into the arena to part the gladiators and been stoned to death by the enraged crowd—after which, the historian Theodoret reports, the emperor Honorius numbered him among the martyrs and abolished the games.13 The account is late enough that its details should be held loosely; but the gladiatorial spectacles did end under Christian emperors, and the direction of the pressure is not in doubt.

Behind the individual reforms lay a whole ethic of care that struck contemporaries as bizarre. The historian of early Christianity Larry Hurtado argued that the Christian “love ethic”—the organised, cross-class care of the poor, the sick, widows, orphans and prisoners, extended even to strangers—was distinctive enough in its Roman setting that it helped set the movement apart and drew converts precisely by its oddity.14 And the law slowly followed the change of heart: in 374 the emperor Valentinian I made the killing of an infant a crime, a legal catching-up to a conviction the churches had held for three centuries.15

There is a way of putting the Christian claim here that reaches all the way to its centre. Nature spends the weak to preserve the strong; the gospel says the strong one spent himself to save the weak. The one his followers confessed as Lord went to a slave’s cross—and was raised, so that the people he died for are not merely pitied but forgiven, adopted as God’s own children, and filled with his Spirit. That is not the survival of the fittest. It is very nearly its exact inversion—and it is the pattern the whole ethic imitates.

What This Shows—and What It Doesn’t

Two limits, stated in the open. This history shows where our compassion came from; it does not show that Christianity is true. A set of beliefs can transform the world and still be false, and a religion’s social record is not a proof of its doctrines. The case that the faith is true rests elsewhere—above all on the resurrection of Jesus, argued at length in this Library—and nothing here is offered as a substitute for it. Where the values came from and whether the beliefs are true are two different questions, and this essay answers only the first.

And the record is not clean. Christians have failed this ethic spectacularly and often—have owned slaves, blessed conquests, turned the weak away from their own doors. But notice what does the judging in that sentence: it is the ethic itself, the standard the tradition handed down, by which we condemn the tradition’s betrayals of it. The failures are real, and the honest history in the companion essays sets them out in full. They are failures against a measure the failing generations had themselves received—and again and again it was that measure, read afresh, that raised up the reformers who called the church back to it. Nor is the claim that the church built the modern welfare state: much of the machinery of organised care—public health, secular human-rights law, the state’s provision for the poor—was extended by later reformers who no longer named the source, and some of it against the churches’ resistance. The narrower point is about origins: the conviction those reformers built on, that the weak carry an inviolable claim simply by being human, is the inheritance—carried forward, often, on capital an earlier age had banked.

What remains, then, is not a knock-down proof of anything, but a fact that is genuinely surprising once you see it: the reflex we mistake for instinct—that a society is judged by how it treats its weakest—is not the natural setting of the human heart. It was learned. It was learned late, against the grain of nature and against the wisdom of the ancient philosophers, from a people who worshipped a God they believed had come down to the very bottom and made his home there. “Whatever you did for the least of these,” he said, “you did for me.” The world that took him seriously became, slowly and imperfectly, a world that went looking for him among the weak—and could not, ever again, quite unlearn the habit of stooping.

Frequently asked questions

Weren’t plenty of ancient people compassionate too?

Yes, and the essay does not deny it. Individual pagans loved their children, honoured their benefactors, and could be moved to genuine pity; Stoic writers reasoned movingly about a shared humanity. The claim is narrower and it survives all of that: no ancient civilisation of the Mediterranean world outside Israel rested a law, movement, or institution on the conviction that the weak, as the weak, have first claim on the strong simply by being human. Care flowed toward the useful—soldiers, valuable slaves, benefactors, the city. Israel is the exception, and the root: the Hebrew law already commanded care for the widow, the orphan and the stranger, and that is the inheritance Christianity carried to the Gentile world.

Isn’t caring for the weak just evolved human empathy?

Part of it plausibly is: kin loyalty and reciprocal help are things nature can produce, because they pay. But the conviction at issue goes further than anything reciprocity explains—that the useless stranger, the enemy, the child who can never repay you, has equal and inviolable worth. Nietzsche saw clearly that this runs against selection, not with it. And even if empathy were wholly evolved, that would only describe a feeling we happen to have; it would not tell us the weak ought to be protected. The move from “we evolved to feel this” to “this is what we owe them” needs a standard evolution cannot supply.

Doesn’t all this prove Christianity is true?

No, and the essay refuses that inference outright. A belief system can remake the world for the better and still be false; a religion’s social record is not evidence of its doctrines. What this history shows is only where a particular moral conviction came from—that it was learned, late and against the grain, rather than being the natural default it now feels like. Whether Christianity is true is a separate question, argued elsewhere in this Library and resting above all on the resurrection of Jesus.
