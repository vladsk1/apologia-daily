"In the Beginning Was the Word": John 1:1 and the Eternal Pre-existence of Christ

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

Three Clauses That Refuse to Be Small

Most of the New Testament begins where you would expect a story about Jesus to begin: a manger, a baptism, a genealogy. The Gospel of John begins before all of that — before there was a beginning at all. "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God."1 In a single breath, the Evangelist reaches back past creation, past time, and locates Jesus of Nazareth there. The claim is staggering, and the church has spent twenty centuries unpacking it. The Council of Nicaea (325) leaned on it; Arius and Athanasius fought over it; the translators of the New World Translation rendered it differently than nearly everyone else, and the dispute over a single missing Greek article still draws blood at doorsteps and in seminar rooms.

Why does it matter? Because if John 1:1 means what the historic church has taken it to mean, then the man who wept at Lazarus's tomb is not a creature, however exalted, but the eternal God in person. Everything downstream — atonement, worship, the Trinity — turns on whether the Word "was God." This essay defends the classical reading, explains the Greek for the non-specialist, and answers the strongest objections fairly, by name.

The Prologue and Its Architecture

John 1:1–18 is a self-contained overture, often called the Prologue. It moves in concentric arcs: from the Word's relation to God and creation (vv. 1–5), to John the Baptist's witness (vv. 6–8), to the Word's coming into the world and its rejection and reception (vv. 9–13), and finally to the climactic announcement, "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" (v. 14).2 The structure matters because pre-existence is not asserted once and dropped; it is the load-bearing beam of the whole passage. The Word who "was" in the beginning (v. 1) is the one through whom "all things were made" (v. 3), and the same Word who "became flesh" (v. 14). Eternity, agency in creation, and incarnation are welded to a single subject.

Clause by clause

The opening verse has three clauses, and each carries a distinct freight. First: "In the beginning was the Word." The verb is the imperfect ēn ("was"), not the aorist egeneto ("came to be") that John uses of created things in verse 3 and of John the Baptist in verse 6. The Word does not come into being at the beginning; the Word already is when the beginning happens. This is the grammar of eternal pre-existence.

Second: "and the Word was with God" (pros ton theon). The preposition pros with the accusative suggests relationship and orientation — the Word is face-to-face with God, distinct from him. Whatever John means in the third clause, he does not mean that the Word simply is the Father. There are two who can be "with" one another.

Third, and most contested: "and the Word was God" — kai theos ēn ho logos. Here the Evangelist holds two truths in tension. The Word is distinct from God (clause two) and yet the Word is God (clause three). The orthodox reading does not collapse that tension; it confesses it.

The Greek of the Third Clause, for the Layperson

The whole modern debate lives in the word order and the article. In Greek, "the" (the definite article, ho) does a great deal of work. In clause three, theos ("God") has no article and stands before the verb: kai theos ēn ho logos. Word for word the order runs "and—God—was—the—Word," but because ho logos ("the Word") carries the article it is the subject, and the anarthrous theos is the predicate — so the sense is "and the Word was God."

In 1933, the grammarian Ernest Cadman Colwell observed a pattern: definite predicate nouns that precede the verb usually lack the article.3 "Colwell's rule" was soon misapplied in reverse — as if an anarthrous predicate before the verb must therefore be definite, proving theos means "the God." That overreaches; Colwell's rule does not run backward, and many scholars who love the orthodox conclusion have leaned on it too hard.

The more careful work was done by Philip B. Harner in a 1973 article in the Journal of Biblical Literature.4 Harner studied anarthrous predicate nouns preceding the verb and argued that their primary force is usually qualitative — they describe the nature of the subject. His paraphrase of John 1:1c is precise: "the logos… had the nature of theos."5 Harner judged that the qualitative force here is "so prominent that the noun cannot be regarded as definite." His own preferred sense is that the Word "had the same nature as God" — neither the bare "a god" (which would make the Word merely one divine being among others) nor a flat "the Word was God" read as identity with the Father, but a statement that the Word fully shares the divine nature while remaining personally distinct.6 Daniel B. Wallace, in his standard reference grammar, reaches the same verdict: in John 1:1c theos is "neither definite nor indefinite" but qualitative — the Word possesses the full nature of deity that the Father possesses, while remaining personally distinct from him.7

This is the elegant payoff. Had John written ho theos ēn ho logos (with the article on theos), he would have said the Word is identical to the Father — the heresy later called modalism. Had he written theos ēn ho logos intending the indefinite "a god," he would have made the Word a lesser divine being — a kind of polytheism. What John actually wrote threads the needle: the Word shares the very nature of God without being the person of the Father.

Where the "Word" Came From

John did not coin Logos in a vacuum. Two streams fed it. The Greek stream runs through Heraclitus and the Stoics, for whom the logos was the rational principle ordering the cosmos. The Jewish stream is deeper and, most scholars now judge, primary. In the Hebrew Scriptures, God's word creates ("by the word of the LORD the heavens were made," Psalm 33:6) and his Wisdom is personified as present at creation (Proverbs 8:22–31). Intertestamental Judaism developed this further: in the Wisdom of Solomon, Wisdom is "a breath of the power of God" (7:25), and the philosopher Philo of Alexandria spoke of the Logos as God's agent in creation and revelation — though for Philo the Logos remained a mediating figure, not God himself.8

John takes this rich vocabulary and does something no Jewish writer had done: he identifies the Word not merely as God's instrument but as God, and then says the Word "became flesh" — a particular man, in history. That is the hinge. The categories were Jewish; the claim was new.

Pre-existence Across the Gospel

The Prologue is not an isolated flourish. Pre-existence echoes through John. In 1:3, "all things were made through him, and without him was not any thing made that was made" — the Word is on the Creator's side of the line between Creator and creation. In 8:58, Jesus says, "Truly, truly, I say to you, before Abraham was, I am" (prin Abraam genesthai egō eimi), and his hearers pick up stones — a reaction most naturally explained by a claim that provoked a charge of blasphemy, and, on a common reading, an echo of the divine "I am" (Exodus 3:14, and more precisely the egō eimi of Isaiah 40–55).9 In 17:5, Jesus prays to be glorified "with the glory that I had with you before the world existed." The Prologue's grammar is confirmed by Jesus's own words in the body of the Gospel.

The Strongest Objections, Stated Fairly

1. The Jehovah's Witnesses and "the Word was a god"

The New World Translation renders John 1:1c, "and the Word was a god."10 It rests on a real feature of the Greek, it is held by sincere people, and it deserves a careful answer rather than a dismissal. Their argument: theos here lacks the article, whereas ton theon in clause two has it; Greek can use the article to mark definiteness; therefore the anarthrous theos should be read indefinitely — "a god" — preserving the distinction between the Father (ho theos) and the Word. They can point to John 1:18's "only-begotten" language and to a theology in which the Son is God's first and greatest creation. A well-read Witness will put it more carefully than that: the Watchtower's own appendix to its Reference Bible does not rest on the claim that an article-less noun is simply indefinite, but argues from anarthrous predicate nouns that precede the verb — and cites the same Harner study cited above. But Harner will not carry the conclusion: his own preferred sense is that the Word "had the same nature as God" — not "a god," which would make the Word merely one divine being among others.6 The popular form of the argument places more weight on the grammar than the grammar can bear; the careful form takes a qualitative reading and then translates it indefinitely — a further step, and one Harner’s own paraphrase does not take. The great majority of Greek scholars reject "a god" as the best reading.

The mainstream grammatical reply is that the absence of the article does not by itself make a noun indefinite, and that the construction here points to quality, not indefiniteness. Harner's study showed that anarthrous predicate nouns before the verb are predominantly qualitative; on his analysis "a god" misreads the grammar.6 Bruce Metzger judged the rendering "a frightful mistranslation," noting that the absence of the article in this position does not make the predicate indefinite when the context does not require it.11 Wallace concurs that the qualitative force is most likely.7 One should concede a fair point to the Witnesses: the article-less theos does safeguard a distinction — the Word is not the Father. But the distinction John draws is between two persons sharing one divine nature, not between God and a demigod.

One further point is worth making gently rather than as a gotcha — and its force should be stated precisely, because it is narrower than polemics often claim. Within John’s own prologue theos stands without the article again at 1:6, 1:12 and 1:13, and the NWT renders every one of them "God," not "a god." Be precise with that list, because the popular version of this point is wrong and a trained Witness will say so: those are anarthrous genitives, not preverbal predicate nominatives, so they are not the identical construction — and his rule was never about article-lessness as such. The sharper case is 1:18, and it is worth raising before a Witness does. There the Greek text the NWT translates prints monogenēs theos — theos again without the article, though again not in the construction of 1:1c — and the NWT renders it "the only-begotten god," lower case, in the same verse in which it has just printed "God" of the Father, where the Greek is equally without the article.12 The article is constant across the two; the referent is not. Witnesses rightly reply that context — not a mechanical rule — should govern each verse, and that principle is correct; we grant it fully. So this point does not by itself decide the meaning of any single verse. Its only force is the limited but real one of showing that the "a god" rendering is theology-driven rather than rule-driven.

As for "only-begotten," it is silence rather than support: monogenēs, even on the most generous reading, is silent on whether the begetting is eternal — and a begetting with no beginning is not a making. That is the distinction the Nicene fathers drew in so many words: begotten, not made. Julius Mantey, co-author of the grammar the Watchtower had cited in its support, wrote to the Society in 1974 objecting that he had been quoted out of context, and that nothing in that grammar was ever meant to imply "a god" is a permissible rendering of John 1:1.13 The weight of the case is carried by Harner’s construction and by the monotheistic, creation-saturated context of John 1, which is what rules "a god" out. That reading is a mistake, and one who shares the very nature of God is not a creature.

2. The Arian / subordinationist reading

The fourth-century presbyter Arius held that the Son, though pre-existent and exalted above all creatures, was himself created — "there was when he was not." Modern subordinationists echo this: John 1:1 makes the Word divine in some derived sense, not the eternal God. The strongest form of the argument leans on John 14:28 ("the Father is greater than I") and on the genuinely subordinate language of the Son's mission throughout John.

The reply, classically, is that the Son's subordination in John is functionalFunctional, not ontological, subordinationIs sayingIn his incarnate mission the Son willingly submits to the Father's will — an economic/functional ordering of roles.Not sayingNot that the Son is lesser in being, made, or later than the Father (Arianism). He is homoousios — co-equal and co-eternal, sharing one divine nature. (in his role and incarnation), not ontological (in his nature). The verb ēn in 1:1 — eternal "was," not created "came to be" — rules out a beginning to the Word's existence, which is precisely what Arianism requires. Athanasius pressed this against Arius: a creature cannot be the agent through whom "all things were made" (1:3) without being made through himself.14 The Nicene fathers concluded that the Son is homoousios — of the same nature as the Father — and John 1:1, read with its imperfect verb and qualitative predicate, is one of the texts that drove them there.

A Different Question: How Early?

One more challenge is raised here constantly, and it belongs in a section of its own because it is not an objection to what John 1:1 means. It is an objection to what John 1:1 is worth. In How Jesus Became God (2014), Bart D. Ehrman argues that belief in Jesus’s divinity evolved: the earliest Christians held an “exaltation” Christology — Jesus was a human being raised to divine status at the resurrection — which was progressively pushed back, to his baptism and then to his birth. Alongside it, and on his own account already present in material older than Paul’s letters, ran a second model: an incarnation Christology in which Christ is a pre-existent divine being who took human form. John, the latest Gospel, gives that second model its most developed form.15 Applied to this verse: John is late, so John 1:1 reports what one community confessed near the end of the first century rather than what the first Christians believed.

Two things should be said, and the first is a concession: the explicitness of John 1:1 has no exact parallel in Mark. The second is that Ehrman reads what the Prologue asserts much as we do — a Word who was with God in the beginning, through whom the universe was made, and who became flesh; on his own account John’s is a very high Christology indeed. He is a witness to what John wrote, not to whether John was right, and it is the reading, not the doctrine, that he grants. So the objection leaves the exegesis of this verse standing and moves the argument onto historical ground.

On that ground it does not hold. The disagreement is not really about dates — Ehrman himself finds a pre-existent Christ in material older than Paul’s letters — but about what kind of being the earliest Christians took him to be: on his account a pre-existent divine being of a lower order, an angel or something like one.15 The agency literature he appeals to is real, and some Second Temple texts do exalt intermediary figures to dizzying heights. Two features of the earliest Christian material are hard to place among them.

The first is where Jesus is put. In 1 Corinthians 8:6 Paul takes Israel’s own confession that the LORD is one and divides its two divine titles, so that the “one God” is the Father and the “one Lord” is Jesus Christ, “through whom are all things” — and he does it in the middle of an argument against idolatry. That reading of the verse is influential, arguably dominant, and genuinely contested: James McGrath argues that “one Lord” is set alongside the creed rather than folded into it.16 Taken as the majority take it, it is not one more step up a ladder. Wisdom is praised; Enoch is exalted; but no Jewish writer took the creed of divine uniqueness and wrote a second figure into it. Paul did. That is a difference in kind and not in degree — a creed is either rewritten or it is not — and that cuts both ways: a plank of this shape cannot be discounted a little, so on McGrath’s reading it yields nothing at all. It is worth exactly what the majority reading of the verse is worth. Ehrman will not be moved by the objection that a “one Lord” who is not the one God leaves Paul confessing a second divine power; he takes that horn, holding that ancient Judaism had room for such a being.

The second feature is worship, and the point needs care, because a gesture of homage was not itself the line: Israelites could bow before a king as well as before the LORD — 1 Chronicles 29:20 uses a single verb of prostration for both. What Jewish monotheism guarded jealously for God was not the posture of homage but the substance of worship — sacrifice, invocation, prayer. Larry Hurtado presses a different point from Bauckham’s, and a narrower one — he grants the chief-agent category and locates the break elsewhere: the intermediary figures of Second Temple Judaism characteristically refuse worship and redirect it to God, while the earliest Christians were praying to Jesus and singing to him within the first two decades.17 Marana tha (1 Corinthians 16:22) is not the homage due a vice-regent — it is an invocation, calling on the risen Lord to come; that is, prayer, offered by the first Aramaic-speaking church. Ehrman does not grant what this shows, and the disagreement is worth naming exactly: both sides agree the worship is early; they disagree over how to interpret it and how far it reaches toward later dogma.

So the weight has to fall on the placement and the worship, and Hurtado’s point is not that the earliest Christians honoured Jesus more highly than other intermediaries but that they did to him what those texts characteristically have their intermediaries refuse. Development there certainly was, in vocabulary and in precision. But on the two markers Judaism itself used, the earliest Christian evidence we have already has the figure on God’s side of them — and that is precisely where a developmental account of this kind needs him not to be. The case is set out in full — including McGrath’s reading and the state of the scholarly argument — in The Shema and the Trinity.

The State of the Scholarship

On the grammar, there is a broad consensus that theos in 1:1c is qualitative and affirms the full deity of the Word; this is the position of Harner, Wallace, Metzger, and the standard reference grammars. The minority who defend "a god" are concentrated outside the academic mainstream, though their grammatical premise — that an anarthrous noun can be indefinite — is true as far as it goes, but does not determine this construction, which is qualitative; and in its careful form, as set out above, their argument does not rest on that premise at all but on the qualitative reading itself, which does not yield "a god" either. On the history, the field is more divided: the "early high Christology" school (Hurtado, Bauckham, Hengel) is strong but not unanimous, and critics such as Ehrman — and, on the historical question only, James D. G. Dunn, a trinitarian who in Christology in the Making argued that full incarnational pre-existence is clearly present only in John — represent a real and respectable dissent.18 A reader should know that the dating and development of Christology is genuinely contested terrain. The grammar of this verse is not contested in the same way, and neither is the shape of the claim the Prologue makes: a Word who was before creation, who was with God, and who became flesh. What is disputed is how early the church arrived at that confession, and how far John's "was God" finally reaches. Those are real disagreements, and the distinction worth keeping is between a verdict and a consensus: this essay has given its reasons for judging that the developmental account does not carry, and it does not pretend that the field has settled the question. But they are disagreements downstream of this sentence rather than about it. On the sentence, the ground is firm: the Word was before the beginning, face-to-face with God, and God in nature — and that Word became flesh.

What This Does and Doesn't Establish

John 1:1, carefully read, establishes that this author, writing this Gospel, confesses the Word as eternally pre-existent, personally distinct from the Father, and fully sharing the divine nature — and that he sees the man Jesus as that Word enfleshed. That is a great deal. It is enough to anchor the Nicene confession in the text and to answer both the "a god" and the Arian readings on grammatical and contextual grounds.

It does not, by itself, settle the historical question of how quickly the whole church arrived at this confession; that argument requires Paul's letters, the early hymns, and the worship evidence that Hurtado and Bauckham marshal. Nor is John 1:1 the entire New Testament case for Christ's deity — it is one strand, woven together with Colossians 1, Hebrews 1, Philippians 2, and Thomas's cry, "My Lord and my God" (John 20:28). What John 1:1 offers is the strand stated most plainly: in the beginning was the Word, and the Word was God, and the Word became flesh. The whole of Christian faith has found it worth a lifetime's study — offered, as Peter urged, with gentleness and respect to all who weigh it.

Frequently asked questions

Doesn't John 1:1 say the Word was 'a god' since the Greek has no 'the' before God?

Jehovah's Witnesses correctly observe that theos lacks the article here, unlike ton theon in clause two, and infer 'a god.' But absence of the article does not by itself make a noun indefinite. Philip Harner's 1973 study showed such preverbal predicate nouns are usually qualitative, describing nature: the Word had the very nature of God, fully divine, while remaining personally distinct from the Father.

If the Word was 'with God,' how can the Word also be God without contradiction?

John holds two truths in tension rather than collapsing them. Clause two, 'the Word was with God' (pros ton theon), marks the Word as face-to-face with God and personally distinct. Clause three affirms the Word shares the divine nature. Had John written the article on theos he'd have taught modalism; had he meant 'a god,' polytheism. Instead the Word fully shares God's nature without being the person of the Father.

Isn't Jesus being called God just a late invention by the last Gospel writer?

No. Bart Ehrman argues that Christian belief about Jesus developed, with John the most developed form, and he is right that John 1:1's explicitness has no exact parallel in Mark. But the dispute is not really about dates: Ehrman himself finds a pre-existent Christ in material older than Paul, and he reads what John's sentence asserts much as we do - though it is the reading he grants, not the doctrine. It is about what kind of being the earliest Christians took him to be, and on his account that is a pre-existent divine being of a lower order, an angel or something like one. The exalted intermediary figures he appeals to are real. Two features of the earliest Christian material are hard to place among them. First, where Jesus is put: in 1 Corinthians 8:6 Paul divides the two divine titles of Israel's own confession that the LORD is one, so that the 'one God' is the Father and the 'one Lord' is Jesus Christ, 'through whom are all things.' Neither of those is a gradation, and on the earliest Christian evidence we have the figure already stood where a developmental account of this kind needs him not to be.
