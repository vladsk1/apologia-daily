How Did the Church Take Over the Roman Empire?

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

In the year 30 or thereabouts, the followers of a crucified Galilean numbered perhaps a few hundred, in one corner of one province, with no wealth, no political standing, and no army.1 Two and a half centuries later, with the faith still illegal and two imperial attempts to suppress it already spent, they were numerous enough that the state gave up and legalised them. A generation after that, an emperor who wanted to reverse the whole thing concluded that the way to beat them was to copy them.

That is a strange thing to have happened, and it is worth asking how it happened—carefully, because this is a question on which both believers and critics routinely say more than the evidence permits. The popular Christian version treats the growth of the church as a self-evident miracle. The popular sceptical version answers in one word: Constantine. This essay argues that both are wrong, that the truth is more interesting than either, and—this matters—that what the truth establishes is narrower than apologists usually claim.

Stating the Question Carefully

Three clarifications before any evidence, because the argument is easy to inflate.

First, this essay is not about the first generation. Why the movement survived the execution of its founder at all—when comparable Jewish messianic movements of the period did not—is a separate argument that reaches further than this one, and it is made at length in the companion essay on the argument from post-resurrection behaviour. Here the question begins where that one ends.

Second, notice what the question is not. This essay does not explain how Christianity became the majority faith of the Mediterranean. That is a fourth-century story and the state is all over it. It explains something smaller and better evidenced: how Christianity became impossible to suppress before the state had any part in it.

Third, and most importantly: growth is not truth. A religion's success tells you that something about it worked—that it met needs, held loyalty, and reproduced itself. It does not tell you that its central claims are true. Islam expanded politically far faster than Christianity ever did, though the conversion of the populations it conquered took centuries rather than decades.2 The Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints has grown, through much of the twentieth century, at a rate comparable to the one usually credited to the early church. Any argument that runs “Christianity spread, therefore Christianity is true” proves far too much, and a thoughtful sceptic will say so within about ten seconds. That inference is not made here and should not be made anywhere.

What the evidence does support is narrower and genuinely useful: the widespread story that Christianity owes its success to imperial power is false as an account of the period in which the church became too large to suppress, and the behaviour its own enemies credited with its growth is attested from outside.

How Many Christians Were There? An Honest Answer

Nobody counted. There is no census of Christians in the Roman Empire, and every figure in every book on this subject is an estimate built from fragments.

The most influential attempt is the sociologist Rodney Stark's, in The Rise of Christianity (1996). His method is deliberately simple: assume the church grew at a steady compound rate, anchor it at an endpoint historians roughly agree on and a deliberately conservative starting assumption, and see what falls out. Starting from about 1,000 Christians in the year 40 and growing at 40 per cent per decade, you reach roughly 7,500 by the year 100, about 218,000 by 200, and about 6.3 million by 300—something over ten per cent of an empire of perhaps sixty million.3

The honest thing to say about that table is that it is a model, not a measurement, and its author said so. The rate is not derived from ancient data. Stark fixes it from his two endpoints and then observes that it is close to the rate the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints has averaged over the past century—a plausibility check on a rate already chosen, not an input to it.4 That comparison is doing real work, and it cuts both ways: it makes the figures credible, and it demonstrates that an exponential curve of this shape is not unique to Christianity or evidence of anything supernatural.

Two cautions follow, and the second is the one usually left out. The first is that the rate cannot be extended: run at 40 per cent per decade to the year 400 and it produces more Christians than the empire had inhabitants, which is a fair measure of what a constant-rate model is and is not.5 The second is that the agreement among historians is thinner than it looks. Keith Hopkins, often cited as independent confirmation, is not one: he takes Stark's arithmetic as his starting point, restates the rate as 3.4 per cent a year, and writes that his debt to Stark's thinking pervades the article. What he adds is a harder question—what such totals would have to mean on the ground, in communities small enough that most of the sociology cannot be tested on them.6 Bart Ehrman, working the same problem more recently, puts the year-300 figure at roughly two and a half to three million, less than half Stark's.7

So the responsible summary is a range, not a point. The published estimates for the year 300 run from about 2.5 million to about 6.3 million—somewhere between roughly four and ten and a half per cent of the empire, concentrated in cities and very unevenly spread. The spread between those figures is the honest measure of what we know. What is not in dispute is the shape: a standing start, and steep sustained growth, while the faith was illegal. That is enough for the argument.

What Growth Looked Like from Outside

The most valuable evidence here is not Christian, because Christian sources have an obvious interest in reporting success. It is the testimony of officials and critics who found the movement irritating, contemptible, or dangerous, and who nonetheless recorded that it was spreading and described what it did.

Around AD 112, Pliny the Younger, governing Bithynia-Pontus, wrote to the emperor Trajan for guidance on prosecuting Christians. His letter is the earliest surviving administrative description of the church, and its alarm is administrative rather than theological: the “contagion of this superstition,” he reports, has spread not only through the cities but through the villages and countryside, affecting people of every age and rank and both sexes, and the temples had been nearly deserted before he began his crackdown.8 One caution belongs with it: a governor writing to his emperor has an interest in magnifying the problem he is solving and then advertising the recovery, and Pliny duly reports the temples refilling. That he also reports many recanting is what tells against reading the letter as simple boosterism.

Half a century later the satirist Lucian of Samosata, who thought Christians credulous fools, described them mobbing the prison of a detained leader, bribing the guards to sit with him overnight, sending delegates from the cities of Asia at collective expense, and sparing no trouble and no money—which is why, he observes, any competent charlatan could grow rich among them.9 The contempt is the point: Lucian is not praising the behaviour, he is explaining how exploitable it made them, and in doing so he confirms it from outside, a century and a half before Constantine.

From inside the illegal period there is also a hard administrative number. Writing about 251, Cornelius, bishop of Rome, listed his church's establishment for a colleague: forty-six presbyters, seven deacons, and more than fifteen hundred widows and persons in distress supported by the congregation.10 That is a relief roll, not a sermon, and it comes from a community that was at that moment illegal.

The Mechanisms

What, concretely, made the difference? Four things recur across the sources and the scholarship—and one caveat belongs in front of them.

The caveat is that the most serious dissenting reconstruction does not credit any of them. Ramsay MacMullen argued that conversion in these centuries turned less on charity than on public demonstrations of power—healings and above all exorcisms, which a pagan could read as a straightforward contest between gods.11 His account covers the same period as this section and is not answered here. It is worth saying plainly that it is a rival mechanism, not a rival to this essay's thesis: whatever else exorcism was, it was not imperial power.

A second caveat: none of the four is unique in kind. Roman collegia provided mutual aid and burial; Jewish communities had a developed and much older tradition of poor relief; and diffusion along household and trade networks is how the cults of Isis and Mithras spread, and later Manichaeism. What follows is a claim about a combination, and about one feature of it that the sources single out.

Care of the sick, at cost

Twice the empire was struck by major epidemics—the Antonine plague from about 165, and the plague named for Cyprian from about 249. Ancient cities had no capacity for mass nursing. Bishop Dionysius of Alexandria, writing of the second outbreak in a letter preserved by the historian Eusebius, describes Christians staying with the sick, taking on their care, and dying in numbers as a result; he contrasts this with pagan neighbours who abandoned theirs.12 The contrast is drawn by an interested party and should be weighed as such, though flight from plague was a commonplace of ancient life that hostile and neutral sources also record.13

Stark's much-quoted argument is that this was not merely admirable but demographically decisive: elementary nursing can substantially reduce mortality even without medicine, so Christian communities would have lost fewer of their members and gained the loyalty of survivors they had nursed.14 Two honest qualifications. The differential-survival arithmetic rests on modern mortality estimates applied to diseases we cannot confidently identify. And the letter itself cuts against the model at one point: nursing raises exposure, and Dionysius says the carers died. Whether the net effect on a community was positive is precisely what the evidence cannot settle.

A different ethic of the body

The exposure of unwanted infants—leaving a newborn outdoors to die or be taken—was legal and unremarkable in the Greco-Roman world. Christian texts prohibit it from the earliest layer: the Didache forbids abortion and infanticide in the same breath as murder, and the Epistle to Diognetus singles out Christians as people who “marry, as do all… but they do not destroy their offspring.”15 A community that refused exposure and cared for foundlings will, other things being equal, retain more people over generations. Other things were not equal: the same communities came to prize celibacy, which pulls the other way, and how common exposure actually was is itself disputed. Treat the ethic as well attested and the demographic effect as an argument rather than a result.

Networks first

Stark's most durable contribution is sociological rather than statistical, and it was not his alone: new religious movements grow overwhelmingly along existing relational lines—family, household, trade, friendship—rather than through public proclamation to strangers.16 Acts shows this repeatedly, in the conversion of whole households (Cornelius, Lydia, the Philippian jailer, Crispus), alongside the public preaching it also records. It is an unglamorous mechanism, and it explains steady compounding better than any account resting on mass conversions.

An unusually open door

The movement admitted, without a bar of status, categories that the surrounding cults ranked: slaves, the poor, women, and non-Greeks. A caution is owed here, because this point is regularly overstated in Christian writing. Stark argued that Christian women enjoyed a dramatically higher status than their pagan contemporaries; that contrast has been blunted from the other side by later specialists, and Lynn Cohick's study of women in the world of the earliest Christians documents considerably more social, economic and religious scope among Greco-Roman women than the contrast allows.17 That settles nothing about how the early churches were ordered, which is a question this essay does not enter. The defensible version is narrower: Christian communities were unusually socially mixed, and membership was not rationed by status.

The Objection: Constantine

The standard sceptical reply is that all of this is decoration, and the real cause is a single political event. In 312 Constantine won at the Milvian Bridge and attributed the victory to the Christian God; in 313 he and Licinius agreed the settlement conventionally called the Edict of Milan, ending persecution; imperial patronage followed; and in 380 Theodosius made Nicene Christianity the official religion of the empire.18 On this telling, Christianity did not win the Roman world—it was handed it.

This objection deserves a real concession and then a real answer.

The concession is substantial. Everything after 312 is entangled with state power, and the church's growth after 380 with legal coercion of non-Nicene worship. The steepest part of the curve in absolute numbers is the fourth century, and no honest account attributes that to persuasion alone. Anyone who cites the fourth-century majority as evidence of Christianity's persuasive power is trading on a period when the thumb was on the scale.

The answer is about timing, and it does not rest on the growth model. Three things stand outside the window the Constantine explanation covers, and none of them is a projection. Pliny's letter and Lucian's satire describe a spreading, organised movement in the second century. Cornelius's relief roll is an administrative document from an illegal church in 251. And the state's own two attempts to bring Christians to heel had already failed with the machinery of empire engaged: Decius's universal sacrifice order of 250, which required all inhabitants to sacrifice rather than targeting Christians by name, and—after Valerian's edicts of 257 and 258 against the clergy and the church's property—Diocletian's directly targeted persecution from 303, which Galerius formally abandoned in the edict of toleration of April 311, a year before the Milvian Bridge.19 That last document is the point: the state conceded first. Whatever Constantine did in 312, he did it to a church two emperors had already failed to remove, and one that by then reached into the palace and the army. The legal situation across the whole period is the subject of the companion essay on persecution.

What This Establishes, and What It Does Not

Let the verdict be stated plainly, in both directions, because a bounded conclusion honestly held is worth more than a large one that collapses on contact.

It does establish something worth having. The claim that Christianity owes its position to Constantine's decree, or to the coercive apparatus of an empire, is false as an account of the period in which the church became too large to suppress. For two and a half centuries it grew with no legal protection and no political advantage on offer—not hunted continuously, as the popular picture has it, but permanently exposed, in a status any neighbour could turn lethal by informing. And the mechanism its own enemies singled out was the unglamorous business of caring for people, including people who were not its members.

The sharpest witness to that comes from the far side of the period, and its date has to be stated rather than blurred. In 362, fifty years into a Christian empire, the emperor Julian—raised Christian, and the last emperor who was not one—set out to restore the old gods and found he could not. Writing to a pagan priest in Galatia, he diagnosed why. It was not that Christianity had better arguments or stronger backing; it was that the Christians out-behaved the pagans. “It is disgraceful,” he wrote, “that, when no Jew ever has to beg, and the impious Galilaeans support not only their own poor but ours as well, all men see that our people lack aid from us.” His remedy was to fund a pagan imitation and order his own priesthood to take up the practice.20

Two things must be said about that letter, and neither destroys it. It survives only through the church historian Sozomen, and Peter Van Nuffelen has argued that it is a later fabrication—a case Jean Bouffartigue has answered, and which remains open.21 So it is the most vivid witness rather than a load-bearing one. But the complaint is not isolated: Julian makes it again in the undisputed Fragment of a Letter to a Priest, where he writes that the pagan priesthood's neglect of the poor was observed by “the impious Galilaeans,” who devoted themselves to philanthropy and gained ascendancy through the credit it won them.22 And note what Julian's own contrast is: not with Judaism, whose care for its poor he holds up as the standard, but with pagan neglect. The practice he could not match was one the church had carried out of its Jewish inheritance into the Gentile world.

Julian is therefore not evidence about what caused the growth of the second and third centuries. He is evidence of something else worth having: that an emperor holding the entire apparatus of the state, and using it—he barred Christians from teaching the classics and restored temple funding—judged that the thing he had to defeat was their charity, and that imitation was his best hope of it.23

It does not establish that Christianity is true.What this one argument does not doIs sayingThe growth of the church, by itself, is not evidence that the Christian claims are true — a movement can spread for many reasons and still be mistaken.Not sayingNot that Christianity is untrue, or that its truth cannot be shown. The case for that rests on other evidence — the resurrection above all — argued across the rest of this library. This essay simply declines to rest any weight on the numbers. Nothing in this essay is offered as evidence that Jesus rose from the dead; that case rests on different data—the first generation's behaviour, not the third century's numbers—and is made in the companion essays. Movements grow for many reasons, and a religion can be both admirable in its effects and false in its claims. Anyone who offers the rise of the church as proof of the faith has changed the subject, and a reader who accepts it on that basis has been given a confidence that will not survive the first competent objection.

For a Christian reader the honest takeaway is not triumph but recognition. The behaviours that made the difference—staying with the sick, taking in the abandoned, feeding somebody else's poor—are not clever apologetic strategies. They are what Jesus told his followers to do, done at cost, by people who mostly left no names, and done—on their own testimony—because they believed he had been raised and would ask them what they had done with it. That is a modest thing to be able to say, and it has the advantage of being true.

Frequently asked questions

Didn't Christianity only take over because Constantine made it the state religion?

No — the objection explains the wrong stretch of the curve. It concedes a great deal first: everything after 312 is entangled with imperial power and the church's growth after 380 with legal coercion, so the fourth-century majority is not evidence of persuasive power. But the evidence for the earlier period does not depend on any of that. Pliny describes a spreading movement around AD 112, Lucian describes its organised charity around 165, a Roman bishop's relief roll from 251 lists over 1,500 dependants, and the state's own attempts under Decius and Diocletian had already failed — Galerius conceded toleration in April 311, a year before the Milvian Bridge.

Doesn't the fact that Christianity grew so fast show it must be true?

No, and the essay says so plainly. Growth shows that a movement met needs and held loyalty; it says nothing about whether its claims are true. Islam expanded politically far faster, though conversion of the conquered populations took centuries; and Rodney Stark checked his own growth rate for early Christianity against modern Latter-day Saint growth. Any argument of the form ‘it spread, therefore it is true’ proves far too much.

How many Christians were there actually, before Constantine?

Nobody counted, and every figure is an estimate. The published range for the year 300 runs from roughly 2.5 million (Bart Ehrman) to about 6.3 million (Rodney Stark's model) — somewhere between about four and ten and a half per cent of the empire. Keith Hopkins is often cited as independent confirmation of the higher figure but is not: he adopts Stark's arithmetic as his starting point. The spread between the estimates is the honest measure of what is known.
