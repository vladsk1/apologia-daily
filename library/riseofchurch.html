How Did the Church Take Over the Roman Empire?

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

In the year 30 or thereabouts, the followers of a crucified Galilean numbered perhaps a few hundred, in one province, with no wealth, no political standing, and no army. Three centuries later, before any emperor had made their faith compulsory, they were numerous enough that a Roman emperor who wanted to suppress them concluded it could not be done by force, and tried instead to copy them. By the end of the fourth century they were the majority religion of the Mediterranean world.

That is a strange thing to have happened, and it is worth asking how it happened—carefully, because this is a question on which both believers and critics routinely say more than the evidence permits. The popular Christian version treats the growth of the church as a self-evident miracle. The popular sceptical version answers in one word: Constantine. This essay argues that both are wrong, that the truth is more interesting than either, and—this matters—that what the truth establishes is narrower than apologists usually claim.

Stating the Question Carefully

Two clarifications before any evidence, because the argument is easy to inflate.

First, this essay is not about the first generation. Why the movement survived the execution of its founder at all—when comparable Jewish messianic movements of the period did not—is a separate and stronger argument, and it is made at length in the companion essay on the argument from post-resurrection behaviour. Here the question begins where that one ends: given that the movement existed, how did it get from a few hundred people to tens of millions without holding any of the levers by which religions normally spread?

Second, and more importantly: growth is not truth. A religion's success tells you that something about it worked—that it met needs, held loyalty, and reproduced itself. It does not tell you that its central claims are true. Islam grew faster than Christianity in its first century. Mormonism has grown, in the modern era, at a rate comparable to the one usually credited to the early church. Any argument that runs “Christianity spread, therefore Christianity is true” proves far too much, and a thoughtful sceptic will say so within about ten seconds. That inference is not made here and should not be made anywhere.

What the evidence does support is narrower and genuinely useful: the widespread story that Christianity owes its success to imperial power is false as an account of the first three centuries, and the behaviour that actually drove its growth is attested by its enemies.

How Many Christians Were There? An Honest Answer

Nobody counted. There is no census of Christians in the Roman Empire, and every figure in every book on this subject is an estimate built from fragments—a bishop's letter here, a martyr list there, the size of a known congregation, the number of churches in a city.

The most influential attempt to put numbers on it is the sociologist Rodney Stark's, in The Rise of Christianity (1996). Stark's method is deliberately simple: assume the church grew at a steady compound rate, anchor it at two points historians roughly agree on, and see what falls out. Starting from about 1,000 Christians in the year 40 and growing at 40 per cent per decade, you reach roughly 7,500 by the year 100, about 218,000 by 200, and about 6.3 million by 300—something over ten per cent of an empire of perhaps sixty million.1

The honest thing to say about that table is that it is a model, not a measurement, and its author said so. The rate is not derived from ancient data; it is a rate that produces plausible endpoints, and Stark calibrated it partly against the documented growth of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints in the twentieth century.2 That comparison is doing real work, and it cuts both ways: it makes the figures credible, and it demonstrates that an exponential curve of this shape is not unique to Christianity or evidence of anything supernatural.

Historians have pushed back on the confidence while largely accepting the order of magnitude. Keith Hopkins, working the problem independently from the demographic side, arrived at totals in the same broad range while being considerably more sceptical about how much sociology the numbers could bear.3 So the responsible summary is this: by the eve of Constantine's conversion, Christians were probably a large minority—plausibly somewhere around a tenth of the empire, concentrated in cities and unevenly spread—and the growth from a standing start had been steep and sustained. That is enough for the argument. Precision beyond it is false precision.

What Growth Looked Like from Outside

The most valuable evidence here is not Christian, because Christian sources have an obvious interest in reporting success. It is the testimony of officials and critics who found the movement irritating, contemptible, or dangerous, and who nonetheless recorded that it was spreading.

Around AD 112, Pliny the Younger, governing Bithynia-Pontus, wrote to the emperor Trajan for guidance on prosecuting Christians. His letter is the earliest surviving administrative description of the church, and its alarm is administrative rather than theological: the “contagion of this superstition,” he reports, has spread not only through the cities but through the villages and countryside, affecting people of every age and rank and both sexes, and the temples had been nearly deserted before he began his crackdown.4 Pliny is a hostile witness describing a movement large enough to dent the local economy of sacrifice, in a province far from Judaea, eight decades after the crucifixion.

Two and a half centuries later comes the most striking concession of all. The emperor Julian—raised Christian, and the last serious attempt to restore the old gods—wrote to a pagan priest in Galatia diagnosing why his restoration was failing. His answer was not that Christianity had better arguments or stronger backing. It was that the Christians out-behaved the pagans. They cared for their own poor, and, he complained, for everyone else's too; the “impious Galileans support not only their own poor but ours as well,” while pagans neglected theirs. His remedy was to order the pagan priesthood to start doing the same thing.5

That is an emperor with every motive to say otherwise, identifying the mechanism of his opponents' success and instructing his own side to imitate it. It is the single best piece of evidence in this essay, and it is not a Christian source.

The Mechanisms

What, concretely, made the difference? Four things recur across the sources and the scholarship.

Care of the sick, at cost

Twice the empire was devastated by epidemic—the Antonine plague from about 165, and the plague named for Cyprian from about 249. Ancient cities had no capacity for mass nursing, and the ordinary response to a household of the infected was flight. Bishop Dionysius of Alexandria, writing of the second outbreak in a letter preserved by the historian Eusebius, describes Christians doing the opposite: staying with the sick, taking on their care, and dying in numbers as a result.6

Stark's much-quoted argument is that this was not merely admirable but demographically decisive: elementary nursing—food, water, keeping a fevered patient warm—can substantially reduce mortality even without medicine, so Christian communities would have lost fewer of their members and gained the loyalty of survivors they had nursed.7 The mechanism is plausible and the differential-survival arithmetic is genuinely contested; it rests on modern mortality estimates applied to diseases we cannot confidently identify. Treat the behaviour as well attested and the demographic multiplier as a reasonable conjecture, not a finding.

A different ethic of the body

The exposure of unwanted infants—leaving a newborn outdoors to die or be taken—was legal and unremarkable in the Greco-Roman world. Christian texts prohibit it from the earliest layer: the Didache, a manual plausibly from the late first or early second century, forbids abortion and infanticide in the same breath as murder, and the second-century Epistle to Diognetus singles out Christians as people who “marry, as do all… but they do not destroy their offspring.”8 A community that refused exposure and cared for foundlings will, over generations, simply retain more people than one that did not.

Networks, not preaching

Stark's most durable contribution is sociological rather than statistical: new religious movements grow overwhelmingly along existing relational lines—family, household, trade, friendship—rather than through public proclamation to strangers.9 The book of Acts, read carefully, shows exactly this pattern of households and synagogue networks. It is an unglamorous mechanism, and it explains the steady compounding better than any account resting on mass conversions.

An unusually open door

The movement admitted, on equal terms, categories that the surrounding cults ranked: slaves, the poor, women, and non-Greeks. Here a caution is owed, because this point is regularly overstated in Christian writing. Stark's claim that Christian women enjoyed a dramatically higher status than their pagan contemporaries has been substantially qualified by later specialists—Lynn Cohick's work on women in the earliest Christian communities shows that Greco-Roman women held more social and economic scope than the contrast requires, and that the early churches were not uniformly egalitarian in practice.10 The defensible version is narrower: Christian communities were unusually socially mixed, and membership was not rationed by status. That is enough to matter and does not need embellishing.

The Objection That Actually Bites: Constantine

The standard sceptical reply is that all of this is decoration, and the real cause is a single political event. In 312 Constantine won at the Milvian Bridge and attributed the victory to the Christian God; in 313 he and Licinius agreed the settlement conventionally called the Edict of Milan, ending persecution; imperial patronage followed; and in 380 Theodosius made Nicene Christianity the official religion of the empire.11 On this telling, Christianity did not win the Roman world—it was handed it.

This objection deserves a real concession and then a real answer.

The concession is substantial. Everything after 312 is entangled with state power, and everything after 380 is entangled with coercion. The steepest part of the growth curve, in absolute numbers, is the fourth century, and no honest account can attribute that to persuasion alone. Ramsay MacMullen—no friend of pious reconstructions—argued that late-antique Christianisation owed far more to top-down pressure, to the prestige of aligning with power, and to spectacular claims of miracle and exorcism than to the gentle mechanisms above.12 Anyone who cites the fourth-century majority as evidence of Christianity's persuasive power is trading on a period when the thumb was on the scale.

The answer is about timing. The objection explains the wrong stretch of the curve. By 312 the church was already large enough that Constantine's alignment with it is better read as a recognition of an existing constituency than as the creation of one—and the two most recent imperial attempts to destroy it, under Decius from 250 and Diocletian from 303, had both failed with the machinery of the state fully engaged. Pliny's letter, Julian's complaint, and the plague testimony all sit outside the window the Constantine explanation covers. Whatever produced the first three centuries of growth, it was not imperial favour, because there was none: for most of that period, association with the church was a legal liability, which is the subject of the companion essay on persecution.

What This Establishes, and What It Does Not

Let the verdict be stated plainly, in both directions, because a bounded conclusion honestly held is worth more than a large one that collapses on contact.

It does not establish that Christianity is true. No fact in this essay bears on whether Jesus rose from the dead. Movements grow for many reasons, and a religion can be both admirable in its effects and false in its claims. Anyone who offers the rise of the church as proof of the faith has changed the subject, and a reader who accepts it on that basis has been given a confidence that will not survive the first competent objection.

It does establish something worth having. The claim that Christianity spread by the sword, or by Constantine's decree, or by the coercive apparatus of an empire, is simply false as an account of the period in which the church became too large to suppress. For three centuries it grew while illegal, at real personal cost, with no political advantage on offer—and the mechanism its own enemies identified was the unglamorous business of caring for people, including people who were not its members. Julian's letter is the proof, and Julian was trying to defeat them.

For a Christian reader the honest takeaway is not triumph but recognition. The behaviours that made the difference—staying with the sick, taking in the abandoned, feeding somebody else's poor—are not clever apologetic strategies. They are what the movement's founder told it to do, done at cost, by people who mostly left no names. That is a modest thing to be able to say, and it has the advantage of being true.

Frequently asked questions

Didn't Christianity only take over because Constantine made it the state religion?

This is the objection the essay takes most seriously, and it concedes a great deal: everything after 312 is entangled with imperial power and everything after 380 with coercion, so the fourth-century majority is not evidence of persuasive power. But it explains the wrong stretch of the curve. By 312 the church was already large enough that Constantine's alignment reads better as recognising an existing constituency than creating one, and two full imperial attempts to destroy it—under Decius and Diocletian—had already failed.

Doesn't the fact that Christianity grew so fast show it must be true?

No, and the essay says so plainly. Growth shows that a movement met needs and held loyalty; it says nothing about whether its claims are true. Islam grew faster in its first century, and Rodney Stark calibrated his own growth model for early Christianity against modern Mormon growth rates. Any argument of the form ‘it spread, therefore it is true’ proves far too much.

How many Christians were there actually, before Constantine?

Nobody counted, and every figure is an estimate. Stark's widely cited model yields roughly 6.3 million by the year 300—something over ten per cent of the empire—but that is a projection from an assumed constant growth rate, not a measurement. Keith Hopkins reached comparable orders of magnitude working independently. The responsible claim is that Christians were probably a large minority, concentrated in cities and unevenly spread.
