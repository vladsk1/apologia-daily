The Titles of Jesus: What the New Testament's Names for Christ Reveal About Who He Claimed to Be

Apologia Daily · deep dive · fully sourced

On a spring night in Jerusalem, a prisoner stood before the high priest. The case against him was collapsing; the witnesses contradicted one another. Then the high priest put a question that cut to the heart of the matter: "Are you the Christ, the Son of the Blessed?" The prisoner answered, "I am, and you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power, and coming with the clouds of heaven" (Mark 14:61–62). The high priest tore his robes and cried blasphemy.1 Whatever else that scene tells us, it tells us this: in the first century, the names a man claimed for himself could be a matter of life and death. The titles of Jesus are not decorative honorifics added by later piety. They are load-bearing. To understand who the New Testament says Jesus is, we have to understand what these titles meant to the people who first heard them.

The Question, Stated Carefully

The claim under examination is not that any single title, by itself, proves the deity of Christ. That would be a clumsy argument, and the honest scholar abandons it quickly. Several of Jesus' most important titles are, in their native setting, thoroughly human — royal, but human. The real claim is cumulative: that when the major christological titles are read in their first-century Jewish context, and read together, they place Jesus in roles, and finally under a name, that reach into the unique identity of the God of Israel. We will examine each title on its own terms, give the strongest objections their full weight, and then ask what the evidence does — and does not — establish.

Son of Man: The Misleading Title

"Son of Man" is the strangest of the titles, and Jesus' favorite self-designation — it appears roughly eighty times in the Gospels, almost always on Jesus' own lips, and almost never as a confession by others. To modern ears it sounds like a statement of humility: "son of man" simply means "a human being," as in Ezekiel, where God repeatedly addresses the prophet as "son of man" (Hebrew ben adam). And that ordinary sense is real. But the crucial background lies elsewhere — in a vision in Daniel 7.

There the prophet sees "one like a son of man" come "with the clouds of heaven." This figure approaches the Ancient of Days and receives something staggering: "to him was given dominion and glory and a kingdom, that all peoples, nations, and languages should serve him; his dominion is an everlasting dominion, which shall not pass away" (Daniel 7:13–14).2 This is no ordinary court scene. The figure comes on the clouds — imagery the Hebrew Bible associates with God's own appearing (Psalm 104:3; Isaiah 19:1). He receives universal, everlasting dominion, and the service of all nations — though, as we will see, it is the manner of his coming rather than the extent of his rule that turns out to carry the weight. In the Aramaic of Daniel, the verb translated "serve" (pelach) is throughout a verb of religious service — but not of service to the true God alone. The same book uses it of serving Nebuchadnezzar's gods, and at 7:27 its object is, on one natural reading, the holy people rather than God himself. The word establishes cultic homage; it does not by itself establish who receives it.3

This is the background Jesus invokes at his trial. Notice first what Daniel does not say: the figure approaches and receives, but he is never seated. Psalm 110:1, on its own, seats a Davidic lord at God's right hand. By fusing the two, Jesus claims both at once — Daniel's clouds and the psalm's seat — and claims them for himself, standing in the dock. Exactly why this counted as blasphemy is itself debated — Darrell Bock argues it was the perceived arrogation of a divine prerogative, taking a seat enthroned in God's presence, rather than the bare messianic claim — but blasphemy was the charge that stuck.4 "Son of Man," far from being the humble title, may be the most exalted of all.

The Objection from Daniel Itself

Before the linguistic challenge comes a prior one: that Daniel 7 never describes a divine figure at all. Two critical readings block the inference at step one, and both are seriously held. The first is corporate. Daniel's vision interprets itself, and the kingdom given to the "one like a son of man" is glossed as given to "the saints of the Most High" (Daniel 7:18, 22) and to "the people of the saints of the Most High" (7:27) — so, on this reading, the figure symbolises faithful Israel, much as the four beasts symbolise empires. No text makes that identification, and the inference is what fails: Hebrews 1:5–13 sets the Son categorically above every angel — "To which of the angels did God ever say… 'Sit at my right hand'?" — and angels refuse the worship the risen Jesus receives., who receives the kingdom on behalf of the heavenly host and of Israel.5 That variation in Daniel's own wording is read in opposite directions — some take "the holy ones" to be faithful Israel; Collins takes them to be angels whose people is Israel. Others read the figure as an individual without reading him as heavenly at all — a messianic or even a historical figure of the Maccabean crisis. And a third reading takes him as an individual heavenly being of more than angelic rank — closer to a divine figure than to a patron angel. It is not a Christian house position: Daniel Boyarin, writing as a Jewish scholar with no Christian stake in the question, argues for it directly, though he remains a minority voice.6

These are serious readings, and neither is fringe. Note first that they are rivals, not allies: on the corporate reading the figure has no existence apart from what it stands for; on Collins's he is a real heavenly individual — Michael, receiving the kingdom on behalf of the angelic host, and of Israel through it. Some readings combine the two. A critic may of course rest on the disjunction — on either reading, he will say, we do not have a divine individual — so each has to be answered on its own terms. Taken separately, each has a real cost.

The corporate reading has to account for an asymmetry in the vision's own imagery. The beasts come up out of the sea (7:3) and the saints are on earth, warred against by the horn (7:21); the "one like a son of man" arrives "with the clouds of heaven" and is brought before the Ancient of Days rather than judged by him (7:13). Whether that arrival is a descent from heaven or an approach to the throne is itself debated — the verse says only that he came and was presented — so nothing should be built on the direction of travel. What can be built on is the vehicle: the beast-kingdoms are given the chaos-sea; this figure is given the clouds. That is the asymmetry the corporate reading has to absorb, and it is part of why a major line of critical scholarship goes angelic rather than corporate.

The angelic reading has its own ceiling, and it is not where apologists usually look for it. The exclusive element was never the dominion: Daniel 7:27 appears to extend an everlasting kingdom to the holy people as well — though the referent of "his kingdom" there is debated — and the Psalms promise an everlasting throne to a Davidic king and pray the homage of the nations for him (Psalm 89:36–37; 72:8–11). An argument resting its weight on everlasting rule is resting it on the wrong plank. What no other figure is given is the cloud-rider's place. Riding the clouds is YHWH's alone — he "makes the clouds his chariot" (Psalm 104:3), he "rides on a swift cloud" (Isaiah 19:1; and of riding the heavens more generally, Deuteronomy 33:26; Psalm 68:33). Outside this vision, the Hebrew Scriptures give that way of coming to no one but God. Collins has an answer to this and it should be stated: he derives the scene from the Ugaritic Baal cycle, where "rider of the clouds" is Baal's standing epithet and a younger god is granted everlasting kingship before an aged high god — so on his account the image was never YHWH's exclusive property to begin with, and Daniel is reusing an inherited pattern rather than transferring a divine prerogative. The derivation itself, though, is read in both directions: on the more common account the Hebrew Scriptures take the cloud-rider epithet from Baal and give it to YHWH polemically, which is why, inside Israel's own Scriptures, it marks the one God off from every rival rather than being common property. What it does not dissolve is the difficulty inside Daniel's own frame: in the Scriptures Daniel's readers had, that mode of coming belongs to God, and a reading that seats a creature in it relocates the anomaly rather than removing it — it owes an account of the substitution, not merely a source for the imagery.

The Similitudes of Enoch are sometimes brought in on one side or the other, and they genuinely cut both ways. In the body of that work the Son of Man is a heavenly individual (1 Enoch 46:1–3) — named before the stars were made and hidden with God before the world (48:2–6) — seated on the throne of glory to judge, and given the homage of the kings of the earth (62:5, 9). But in a closing section that many scholars judge a later appendix, though the point is disputed, the seer himself is addressed as the Son of Man (71:14). The date of the Similitudes is disputed and they are not attested at Qumran, so nothing here should be built on them. What they do show is that the Danielic figure was read in Jewish tradition as an individual, and read very high.8

Second Temple Judaism did exalt intermediary figures very high, so the honest reply is not that no one was ever exalted. It is what Jesus does with the text. And the figure at the trial is not merely a heavenly one. Jesus fuses Daniel 7:13 with Psalm 110:1 — and, as we saw, the seat alone is not the point, since Psalm 110 seats a Davidic lord. What is startling is who takes it, and with what: a prisoner in the dock, claiming that seat in God's own presence and Daniel's clouds with it. That combination is not a higher rung on the ladder of creatures but the Creator's own side of the line. An archangel receiving a stewardship on Israel's behalf is not that claim.

This does not settle Daniel's own referent, and the argument does not need it to. What neither reading explains away is a first-century man applying that scene to himself, in the first person, and being condemned for it. That hearing is itself disputed — no follower is placed inside the chamber, and critics argue Mark shaped the scene. The Aramaic form of these sayings is taken up next; the historicity of the hearing itself is a larger question this essay does not try to settle. Set the trial aside entirely and the pattern does not go with it. The same collision is already there in a Jesus who forgives sins on his own authority and draws the identical verdict of blasphemy from the scribes in a crowded house — "Who can forgive sins but God alone?" (Mark 2:5–7) — and in the mutual knowledge he claims with the Father (Matthew 11:27), and in the "but I say to you" set against what was said to Israel of old (Matthew 5:21–22, 27–28, 33–34). No one scene carries the case; the collision recurs in Mark and in the sayings tradition alike, and a critic who wants it gone has to account for each scene in turn rather than for the trial alone. Critics dispute these too — Mark 2:5–10 is often read as Mark's own composition — but the objection then has to be run separately against each, which is a different and harder task from setting one hearing aside.

The Casey Objection

Here the strongest challenge must be heard. The late Maurice Casey of Nottingham, one of the finest Aramaists of his generation, argued that the whole "Son of Man" edifice rests on a mistranslation. In Aramaic, he contended, the phrase bar (e)nash(a) was simply a generic idiom — a roundabout way of saying "a man," or "someone," sometimes used by a speaker to refer obliquely to himself. On this reading, when Jesus said "the Son of Man has nowhere to lay his head," he was using ordinary Aramaic for "a man like me has nowhere to lay his head." The titular, apocalyptic "Son of Man" is then a creation of the Greek-speaking church, which read a generic Aramaic idiom as a fixed title and tied it to Daniel.9 Casey's view, building on Geza Vermes, is a serious linguistic proposal that has to be met on linguistic ground.

Yet it has met serious resistance precisely on the linguistic ground Casey claimed. Paul Owen and David Shepherd, surveying the Aramaic evidence, argued that the emphatic form bar enasha does not behave as Casey's general-idiom thesis requires, and that Casey's appeal to a "stable" Aramaic ranged uncritically over texts spanning a thousand years.10 Casey replied vigorously in print, defending his readings.11 The dispute is genuine and not fully settled. But two points tell against the deflationary reading even if Casey's idiom existed. First, the Gospels overwhelmingly use the doubly definite Greek form, ho huios tou anthrōpou — "the Son of the Man" — an oddly specific construction for a generic "someone." Second, one saying in particular tells against the fully deflationary reading: no ordinary man "comes on the clouds" with great power and glory (Mark 13:26). Casey assigns this Danielic clouds-saying to a secondary layer rather than to Jesus, so it is not by itself decisive against him — but it does not need to be. The doubly-definite Greek above is the more durable point — it is untouched by any saying-layer judgment, though Casey answers it by making the definiteness an artefact of translation into Greek, and that exchange with Owen and Shepherd is where the dispute actually lives. And for those who judge such sayings early, the most economical explanation is that Jesus deliberately took up Daniel's enigmatic figure as a veiled self-designation — veiled enough to invite reflection, pointed enough to convict him at his trial.

Messiah, Son of God, Son of David: The Royal Titles

Here honesty requires a concession that some apologists resist. "Messiah" (Hebrew mashiach, Greek Christos, "anointed one") did not, by itself, mean "divine." It meant God's anointed king — the heir of David who would restore Israel. The background lies in God's promise to David: "I will be to him a father, and he shall be to me a son" (2 Samuel 7:14), and in the royal coronation Psalm: "You are my Son; today I have begotten you" (Psalm 2:7). In this idiom, "Son of God" was a royal title. The Israelite king was God's adopted son; the nation itself is called God's son (Exodus 4:22). A Dead Sea Scrolls text (4Q246), though its referent is debated, suggests that "Son of God" language could attach to an expected messianic figure without, in itself, asserting deity.12

"Son of David" carries the same freight: it names Jesus as the legitimate royal heir, the king of the line of David. None of these titles, taken in isolation, settles the question of divinity. A thoughtful first-century Jew could call a man "Messiah," "Son of David," even "Son of God" in the royal sense, while affirming the strictest monotheism. And yet the New Testament does not leave them where it found them. The Gospel writers press the royal sonship toward a unique, ontological sonship — Jesus is not merely a son but the Son, the "only begotten" (John 3:16), who knows the Father as no creature does (Matthew 11:27). The royal title becomes a vessel filled with more than royalty. The argument here is not that "Son of God" proves deity, but that the New Testament authors expand the title beyond its original banks.

Lord (Kyrios): The Most Loaded Word

If one title bears the heaviest weight, it is Kyrios, "Lord." On the surface it is ordinary — a term of respect, like "sir" or "master." What it had become in Greek-speaking Judaism is more disputed than apologetic summaries usually admit. That Kyrios had become the standard way Greek-speaking Jews rendered and referred to the divine name is the usual account, and both halves of it are genuinely disputed — what the earliest copies wrote, and what was said aloud over them: several pre-Christian copies preserve the Tetragrammaton in Hebrew or palaeo-Hebrew letters, and one renders it as ΙΑΩ.13 But the New Testament writers settle the question for their own usage. When they quote a passage where the Hebrew has YHWH, they write Kyrios — and they then apply those very passages to Jesus. Whatever a first-century synagogue said aloud, that is what the apostles did in Greek, on the page.

This is what makes the New Testament's use of Kyrios for Jesus so startling. Paul writes to the Romans: "if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord… you will be saved… For everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved" (Romans 10:9–13). That last line quotes Joel 2:32, where "the Lord" upon whose name one calls is YHWH himself. Paul takes a text about calling on YHWH and applies it, without apology, to calling on Jesus.14 Even more remarkable is 1 Corinthians 8:6, where Paul appears to take the Shema — Israel's bedrock confession, "the Lord our God, the Lord is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4) — and split it around two figures: "for us there is one God, the Father… and one Lord, Jesus Christ“One Lord” does not demote JesusIs sayingPaul reworks Israel’s own creed so that Jesus is the “one Lord” — Kyrios, the Septuagint’s word for the divine name YHWH — placing him inside the unique identity of the one God.Not sayingNot that the Father alone is God while Jesus is a lesser “Lord.” Because “Lord” here carries YHWH’s own name, the verse confesses Christ’s full deity, not his subordination., through whom are all things." Richard Bauckham has argued influentially — though the reading is itself contested, not settled — that Paul is not adding Jesus alongside the one God as a second deity, but including Jesus within the unique identity of the one God of Israel.15

The Bousset Objection

The classic challenge to this evidence is now over a century old, but it remains the most formidable. In Kyrios Christos (1913), the German scholar Wilhelm Bousset argued that worship of Jesus as "Lord" did not arise among the first Aramaic-speaking disciples in Palestine. It developed later, he held, in the Hellenistic Gentile churches — at places like Antioch — where Greek-speaking converts, surrounded by the "lords" and divinized rulers of pagan cult, gradually elevated Jesus to a cultic "Lord."16 On this thesis, the high Christology of Kyrios is a secondary Greek import, not the faith of Jesus' first followers.

The most damaging reply came from Larry Hurtado, whose Lord Jesus Christ (2003) set out to replace Bousset's book as the standard treatment.17 Hurtado's counter-evidence is a single Aramaic word. In 1 Corinthians 16:22, writing in Greek to a Greek city around AD 53–55, Paul preserves an untranslated Aramaic prayer: Marana tha — "Our Lord, come!" The word will not behave as Bousset's theory needs. An Aramaic invocation of Jesus as "Lord" (Mar), addressed to him in prayer, is very hard to explain as a coinage of the Greek-speaking mission. It points back to the earliest Aramaic-speaking believers in Judea — the very community Bousset claimed had no such devotion.18 Devotion to Jesus as Lord is not late and not Greek. It is primitive and Jewish.

Two replies are on the record and should be stated. Bousset located the Kyrios-cult in the bilingual Syrian centres — Damascus and Antioch — where Aramaic was spoken alongside Greek, so an Aramaic formula does not by itself reach behind that mission; the weight of Marana tha rests on the further judgment that Paul is quoting something already fixed when he reached it. And James D. G. Dunn, who accepts the early date, denies the inference: for him the kyrios title distinguishes Jesus from God rather than identifying him with God — an exalted Lord exercising God's lordship. Dunn's Did the First Christians Worship Jesus? (2010) is the standing book-length dissent, and the case above has to be read as answering him, not as having closed him.19

Logos, Last Adam, and the Divine "I AM"

Three further titles round out the picture. John opens his Gospel with the Logos, the "Word" who "was with God, and the Word was God," and who "became flesh and dwelt among us" (John 1:1, 14) — language drawing on both the creative word of Genesis and Jewish reflection on God's Wisdom. Paul calls Jesus the "last Adam" (1 Corinthians 15:45), the head of a renewed humanity, undoing in obedience what the first Adam undid in disobedience. And in John, Jesus speaks the absolute "I am" (egō eimi): "before Abraham was, I am" (John 8:58) — an echo of God's self-naming to Moses (Exodus 3:14) so provocative that his hearers picked up stones. These confirm the trajectory the other titles trace.

The Honest State of the Scholarship

Where does this leave us? Several debates are real and ongoing. The exact force of "Son of Man" in Aramaic remains contested among specialists; Casey's view, though a minority position, is not refuted to everyone's satisfaction. Who Daniel's own figure refers to remains genuinely unsettled too — corporate, angelic and individual-heavenly readings all have serious defenders, and this essay does not claim to settle it; its argument runs through what Jesus did with the scene, not through Daniel's referent. And the cumulative form of the argument deserves a word of its own, because stacking individually inconclusive inferences does not by itself produce a strong one. What has to be shown is not that the data are many but that they fail independently — that one explanation cannot dissolve them together. The honest statement of that here is bounded. Three of the strongest data — an Aramaic prayer addressed to Jesus (1 Corinthians 16:22), a YHWH-text from Joel applied to him (Romans 10:13), and the Shema reworked around him (1 Corinthians 8:6) — are all Paul's, and two are in one letter, so they are not independent sources. What can be claimed is narrower and still substantial: they are three different kinds of material — a fixed liturgical formula, a scriptural citation, a creedal reformulation — and at least two are often judged material Paul inherited rather than coined, which is what pushes them behind him. A critic who wants all three gone has to explain a Greek-speaking mission preserving, untranslated, an Aramaic prayer it did not invent. And the Danielic collision traced above runs through the Synoptic tradition, where "Son of Man" is absent from Paul altogether. The dating and interpretation of every title can be argued. It is also true — and must be stated plainly — that titles develop: communities reflect, and meanings deepen over decades. No responsible scholar denies that the church's understanding grew. The question is whether that growth was the invention of something foreign to Jesus and his first followers, or the unfolding of something already present in seed. On the central matter — whether high devotion to Jesus was early or late — the scholarly tide has largely turned against Bousset's Hellenistic derivation and toward Hurtado, Bauckham, and Martin Hengel, who concluded that more happened christologically in the first twenty years after the crucifixion than in the next seven centuries of doctrinal history. That is a judgment about the date of the devotion, not a settled verdict on its content: Dunn grants the early date and still denies that kyrios identifies Jesus with YHWH, and James F. McGrath disputes Bauckham's divine-identity category outright (see note 19). Hurtado and Bauckham themselves differ over what carries the weight — devotional practice or divine identity — so "the tide has turned" should not be heard as one agreed position.20

Conclusion: What the Titles Do and Do Not Establish

The titles of Jesus do not give us a single knockdown proof of his deity, and we should not pretend otherwise. "Messiah" and "Son of David" are royal and human; "Son of God," in its first sense, is the language of Israel's kings. An argument that leaned on any one title in isolation would deserve to fail. What the titles establish is something subtler and, in the end, weightier: a cumulative pattern. Read together and in context, they show the first Christians — Jewish monotheists who would die before bowing to an idol — taking the heavenly figure of Daniel, the worship-language of Joel, the very confession of the Shema, and the unspeakable Name itself, and applying all of it to a crucified Galilean. They did this early, in Aramaic, in prayer. The titles do not merely decorate Jesus. They locate him inside what Israel reserved for her God. That is not the whole case for who Jesus is. But it is a remarkable place for the evidence to begin.

Frequently asked questions

Doesn't 'Son of Man' just mean Jesus was calling himself an ordinary human being?

That ordinary sense is real; in Ezekiel God repeatedly addresses the prophet as 'son of man,' simply meaning a human. But the essay argues the crucial background is Daniel 7, where 'one like a son of man' comes with the clouds of heaven — elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible, God's own conveyance. Whom Daniel's figure stands for is disputed, and the essay states the corporate, angelic and individual readings, with Collins's own reply. What neither explains away is that at his trial Jesus fused Daniel 7 with Psalm 110, taking that scene and that seat for himself, and the charge that stuck was blasphemy.

Isn't 'Son of Man' really a mistranslation of a common Aramaic phrase for 'a man'?

The essay honestly presents this challenge from Maurice Casey, building on Geza Vermes, that the Aramaic bar enasha was a generic idiom for 'someone,' turned into a fixed title by the Greek church. It is learned and not fully settled. But Owen and Shepherd pushed back on linguistic grounds, Casey answered them in print, and the Gospels use an oddly specific doubly definite Greek form — 'the Son of the Man,' a strange way to say 'someone.' Casey assigns the clouds-sayings to a later layer, so those cannot settle it against him. The Greek form is the more durable point, though Casey answers it by making the definiteness an artefact of translation — and that exchange with Owen and Shepherd is where the dispute actually lives.

If 'Messiah' and 'Son of God' were just royal titles, how do they point to Jesus being divine?

The essay concedes plainly that these titles are royal and human first: 'Messiah' meant God's anointed king, and 'Son of God' was applied to Israel's kings, and (in a debated Dead Sea Scrolls text) could attach to a messianic figure without asserting deity. No single title in isolation settles divinity. The argument is cumulative: read together and in context, especially with 'Lord' quoting YHWH texts, the titles locate Jesus inside what Israel reserved for her God.
