Did Christianity Change the World?

The deep dive tells you what the argument is. This track makes you able to use it — reconstruct it from memory, answer its hardest objections, and hold the line in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

You probably believe every human being is of equal worth, and that the weak have a claim on the strong simply by being human. You probably think that is obvious. For most of recorded history almost no society was built on it, and the civilisations that gave us Western philosophy did not.

This track defends a genealogical claim, and no more: the conviction that every person has equal, inviolable worth has a traceable history in which Judaism is the root — the image of God (Genesis 1:27) is a Jewish doctrine that stands on its own feet — and Christianity is a major part of the route into Western law and sentiment, carrying that conviction to the Gentile world and welding it to an unlikely centre: God the Son, executed as a criminal. The claim is bounded on purpose. Christianity was a necessary ingredient in a particular combination, not a sufficient cause, which is why the decisive changes came late, unevenly, and against fierce Christian resistance.

Two firewalls hold throughout, and the track never lets either down. First, none of this is evidence that Christianity is true — a belief system can be enormously beneficial and entirely false, and “look what it did for the world” is a bad argument for the faith. Second, the ledger against is real and not incidental — the Crusades, the inquisitions, the slave-owners’ Bible, colonial mission, centuries of anti-Judaism, apartheid, the abuse crisis — and the genealogy is never used to soften any of it. Being handed a standard aggravates the failure to keep it; it does not excuse it.

Understand it

Not more prose — the load-bearing structure. See the skeleton, then learn to defend each bone.

The argument, formally

Three lines. The whole weight rests on the two premises.

P1

The conviction that every human being has equal, inviolable worth is neither self-evident nor the default of the ancient world — it was won late and unevenly, and has a traceable history.

P2

That history runs through a Jewish doctrine — the image of God — carried by Christianity into the Gentile world and welded to God the Son, crucified; Christianity was a necessary ingredient in a particular combination, not a sufficient cause.

∴ C

Therefore the modern assumption that basic moral equality is simply what a decent person naturally thinks is false — it is a specific inheritance, largely Jewish and Christian in route. This shows neither that Christians have been good (the ledger answers that) nor that Christianity is true.

This is a historical-genealogical claim, and its conclusion is fenced on both sides: it establishes where a moral standard came from, and it stops there. It is not an argument that Christianity is true — social benefit never shows that — and it is stated in the same breath as the full record of the church’s failures to keep the standard.

Then the honest step — what “good for the world” does and does not show

That a faith did good is never, by itself, a reason to think it true. Keep the two questions apart, and keep the ledger open.

Defending the premises

Why each premise is more plausible than its denial

Support for P1 — equality is not self-evident, and was not the ancient default

The ancient world did not hold it

Roman power rested on chattel slavery of an ordinariness with few parallels; Aristotle defended slavery as natural; crucifixion was engineered to strip a human being of dignity in public. The conviction that the weak have a claim on the strong simply by being human is not what those civilisations thought, and Tom Holland’s route to the thesis was noticing exactly this — the more closely he studied Greece and Rome, the less he shared their moral universe.

It was won late and against the grain

Abolition, universal dignity, and toleration arrived late and unevenly — which is itself evidence for the bounded claim, not against it. A standard held and disobeyed for centuries is evidence about human beings, not about where the standard came from.

Support for P2 — the route ran through the image of God to a crucified God

The Jewish root, credited as Jewish

That every human bears the image of God (Genesis 1:27) is a Jewish doctrine, developed in Jewish scripture long before and independently of the church. Judaism’s account of universal worth stands on its own feet, and this case does not claim it — what Christianity did was carry it into the Gentile world at scale.

Welded to an unlikely centre

Christianity fused that conviction to a claim Judaism does not make — that God himself, in the person of the Son, had been executed“God was executed” — in what sense Christians say itIs sayingThe one who suffered and died is a divine person, God the Son. A civilisation that came to worship a crucified man as God had, in principle, lost the ability to say the humiliated are contemptible — though in practice it went on saying so for centuries, which is the ledger.

The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

This is what separates someone who knows what Christianity did to the moral imagination of the world from someone who can defend it. Each gets its best form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: if universal dignity was in the deposit from the first century, why did abolition and toleration wait until the eighteenth, and arrive then only in north-western Europe, when Byzantine, Russian, Ethiopian and Iberian-American Christendoms produced none of it? This track meets it in our own voice: the answer is the bounded claim itself — Christianity was a necessary ingredient, not a sufficient cause. Late and uneven arrival shows Christianity by itself did not produce the changes, not that they would have come without it.

“All the good Christianity did proves it’s true” Common

Strongest form

Look at the hospitals, the universities, the abolition of slavery, the idea of human rights — a tree is known by its fruit, and fruit this good must come from a true root.

Reply

No, and this track refuses that inference explicitly: social benefit is not a truth-claim about God. If the faith were false, its benefits would be a reason to be grateful for a mistake, not a reason to believe it. “Known by its fruit” is about recognising false prophets, not a proof of doctrine. The case for the faith’s truth rests on the historical and philosophical arguments made elsewhere in this Library — this argument establishes something narrower and keeps the firewall up.

“What about the Crusades, the inquisitions, slavery, and the abuse crisis?” Common

Strongest form

The church’s record is soaked in blood and hypocrisy — holy wars, torture, witch-burnings, the theological defence of slavery and apartheid, the abuse of children and its cover-up. You cannot claim the credit for the good and disown all this.

Reply

They are set out in full here, not managed — and with the harder point added: the most sophisticated defences of slavery were themselves Christian, and read on their own the pro-slavery proof-texts had the easier of it. The genealogical argument is never used as mitigation. That the church supplied a moral standard does not shorten what that standard measures; being handed it aggravates the failure to keep it. The honest thing a Christian can say is not that the church has been better than others, but that it was handed something better than itself.

“It took eighteen centuries — so Christianity clearly didn’t cause it” Trickier

Strongest form

If equality was really in the gospel from the start, why did it take 1,800 years to abolish slavery, and why only in one corner of Christendom while the rest produced nothing of the kind? The lateness shows the real causes were modern, not Christian.

Reply

Grant the lateness — it is exactly what the bounded claim predicts. Christianity was a necessary ingredient in a particular combination, not a sufficient cause. A standard held and disobeyed for centuries is evidence about human beings, not about where the standard came from; and that the change came late and in one place shows Christianity by itself did not produce it, not that it would have come without it. The claim was never “Christians were good,” which the ledger disproves.

“It was really the Enlightenment or the Stoics that gave us human rights” Hard

Strongest form

Human dignity comes from Enlightenment reason and its fight against clerical power — and before that from the Stoics and Roman jurists who reasoned about a common humanity. And Samuel Moyn argues human rights are a twentieth-century invention; the long Christian genealogies are retrojection.

Reply

All three are engaged, not dodged. The Stoics reasoned about a common humanity, but theirs was a universalism of reason, which grades — and it built no institution and freed no one. The churches were often on the wrong side of the Enlightenment’s fights — granted — but its moral premises were not self-generated. And Moyn presses the sharpest counter; it is a serious position, it is left standing rather than pretend-refuted, and a reader weighing this case should weigh it too. The claim survives as the careful one: a major part of the route, contested in its strong forms, defensible in its bounded one.

Where this comes from — primary sources, not our summary

Tom Holland2019 · Dominion
The most-discussed recent statement of the case — that the West received its instinct for universal dignity from a particular history rather than deducing it from reason, with a crucified God at its root. A popular historian, not a specialist in every period; the strong causal claim is genuinely debated even where the facts are not.
The image of GodGenesis 1:27
The Jewish root of the whole genealogy, credited as Jewish: that every human being bears the image of God, developed in Jewish scripture long before and independently of the church. Paired with Paul on “a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles” (1 Corinthians 1:23) for the crucified-God turn.
Mark Noll2006 · The Civil War as a Theological Crisis
The honest half of the abolition story: read on their own, the isolated pro-slavery proof-texts had the easier of the argument, and the conflict became a crisis of biblical interpretation itself — abolition was a Christian achievement won against Christians.
Samuel Moyn2010 · The Last Utopia
The sharpest counter, included so the reader can weigh it: human rights are a twentieth-century phenomenon and the long genealogies are retrojection. Not refuted here — a serious position that a careful reader should hold against the thesis.

    One strong way to answer — in a 1 Peter 3:15 tone

    “I’m not going to pretend that record away — the Crusades, the inquisitions, the theological defence of slavery and apartheid, the abuse crisis: all real, all a stain, and I won’t use anything I’m about to say to soften them. In fact the sophisticated defences of slavery were themselves Christian. But here’s the claim I’m actually making, and it’s narrower than you might expect. That conviction has a history, and a lot of that history is Jewish and then Christian: the image of God carried into a world that came to worship a man executed as a criminal. And two honest limits: it took eighteen centuries and a lot of fighting against Christians to cash out — so Christianity was a necessary ingredient, not the whole cause — and none of this shows the faith is true. If it were false, all this good would just be a reason to be grateful for a mistake. The church’s honest boast isn’t that it’s been good. It’s that it was handed something better than itself.”
