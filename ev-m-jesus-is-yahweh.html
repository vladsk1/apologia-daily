Is Jesus Yahweh? The Divine-Name Argument

The deep dive explains the case. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild from memory how the New Testament gives Jesus the divine Name and Yahweh’s own texts, meet the “it is just an ordinary title” objection, and hold the answer together in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

There was a name the people of Israel would not say aloud — YHWH, the personal name of God. As Israel's Scriptures circulated in Greek, the divine name came to be rendered, again and again, with Kyrios, "Lord" — though how early is debated. It belonged to God alone: "I am the LORD; that is my name… my glory I give to no other" (Isaiah 42:8). Yet the earliest Christians — Jewish monotheists who guarded that name as jealously as anyone — took the texts that spoke of YHWH, and the name itself, and applied both to a crucified Galilean.

The load-bearing move is not a word-trick. (The popular echad "compound unity" argument is a discredited overreach, and this case sets it aside.) It is what the New Testament writers actually do with the divine name: where the Hebrew has YHWH they write Kyrios, and then they apply those very texts to Jesus. Joel's "everyone who calls on the name of the LORD" becomes Romans 10:13; Isaiah 45's exclusive oath "to me every knee shall bow" becomes Philippians 2:10–11; Isaiah 8's "honor the LORD of hosts as holy" becomes 1 Peter 3:15; Psalm 102, addressed to the Creator, is spoken by the Father to the Son in Hebrews 1:10–12. Paul even reaches inside the Shema, distributing its two words for the one God — "one God, the Father… and one Lord, Jesus Christ" (1 Corinthians 8:6) — placing Jesus within the one God, not beside him. And in John, Jesus speaks the LORD's own "I am he" of Isaiah: "before Abraham was, I am" (John 8:58).

Honest scope: this is inclusion within the unique divine identity of the one God (Bauckham) — Jesus identified with YHWH, never a second Yahweh, never the Father collapsed into the Son. It does not prove the doctrine of the Trinity from a single verse, and several of its readings are genuinely contested (the split of the Shema above all — James McGrath reads "one Lord" as set alongside the creed). Nor does it settle the metaphysics of how the one God is Father, Son, and Spirit. What it establishes is remarkable enough: monotheists gave the crucified Jesus the one name no Israelite would give a creature — early, some of it in Aramaic, and on purpose.

Before you start · key terms

Eight words this argument leans on

Learn these first — the objections below turn on them, and most weak versions of the argument come from blurring one of them.

YHWH — the NameWritten with four Hebrew consonants, it was the personal name of the God who had spoken to Moses from the fire, and reverence for it ran so deep that a reader said Adonai, “Lord,” instead. That name belonged to God alone: “I am the LORD; that is my name … my glory I give to no other” (Isaiah 42:8).

The argument, formally

The New Testament gives Jesus the divine Name — and Yahweh's own texts — by design.

P1

Where Israel's Scriptures have the divine name YHWH, the New Testament writers write Kyrios ("Lord") — and they take those very texts and apply them to Jesus: Joel's "call on the name of the LORD" (Romans 10:13); Isaiah 45's exclusive oath "to me every knee shall bow" (Philippians 2:10–11); Isaiah 8's "honor the LORD of hosts as holy" (1 Peter 3:15); Psalm 102, addressed to the Creator, spoken by the Father to the Son (Hebrews 1:10–12). He is given "the name that is above every name" (Philippians 2:9), and in John he speaks the LORD's own "I am he" (John 8:58 with Isaiah 43:10). (Not the discredited echad word-play, which the case disowns.)

P2

This is inclusion within the one God, not a second deity: Paul reworks the Shema itself, distributing its two divine words — "one God, the Father… and one Lord, Jesus Christ" (1 Corinthians 8:6) — placing Jesus inside Israel's creed (Bauckham's "divine identity"; Wright). And it is early and Jewish: the untranslated Aramaic Marana tha ("Our Lord, come!", 1 Corinthians 16:22) points back behind Paul's Greek toward the earliest church. The one escape — that Jesus is a supreme agent (the shaliach) — cannot bear it: no agent was ever given YHWH's exclusive oath, written into the Shema, or prayed to (the worship an angel refuses, Revelation 19:10).

∴ C

Therefore the New Testament identifies Jesus with YHWH — giving him the divine Name and the Scriptures that belong to its bearer alone, early and by design. That is a claim about the identity of God himself, not the reverence owed a creature. And we hold that the confession is true. Jesus is the LORD — not a second god beside YHWH, and not the Father under another name, but the Son who shares the one divine Name with the Father. He is also truly the crucified man: fully God and fully man, two natures in one person.

This does not prove the doctrine of the Trinity from a single verse, and it does not rest on the discredited claim that echad means a plural unity. Several readings are genuinely contested — the split of the Shema above all — and honesty names them. Its narrower, firmer point: monotheists gave the crucified Jesus the divine Name and Yahweh's own texts, early and by design. How the one God is Father, Son, and Spirit is the further work of the doctrine of the Trinity.

Why "'Lord' is just a title of respect" is the popular skeptic's move.

The strongest form is the agency reading (Jehovah's Witnesses, biblical Unitarians): Kyrios can mean an ordinary master, and Judaism knew exalted intermediaries — angels, personified Wisdom — who bear God's authority, even his name, without being God. The reply grants that not every "Lord" carries the freight of the Name — the argument rests on the loaded uses, the YHWH-texts turned on Jesus — and answers that no such agent was ever prayed to or given YHWH's exclusive oath.

Defending Premise 1

Where Israel’s Scriptures have the divine name YHWH, the New Testament writers write Kyrios (“Lord”) — and they take those very texts and apply them to Jesus: Joel’s “call on the name of the LORD” (Romans 10:13); Isaiah 45’s exclusive oath “to me every knee shall bow” (Philippians 2:10–11); Isaiah 8’s “honor the LORD of hosts as holy” (1 Peter 3:15); Psalm 102, addressed to the Creator, spoken by the Father to the Son (Hebrews 1:10–12). He is given “the name that is above every name” (Philippians 2:9), and in John he speaks the LORD’s own “I am he” (John 8:58 with Isaiah 43:10). (Not the discredited echad word-play, which the case disowns.)

Precision check: this is a bounded historical claim — not that any one verse settles the doctrine of the Trinity, but that a consistent, deliberate pattern runs across distinct kinds of early material. It does not rest on the Hebrew word echad: the plurality in “one flesh” comes from the noun, not from echad, which is simply the ordinary Hebrew numeral “one” and routinely describes things that are emphatically singular. The case rests on something much harder to wave away: what the New Testament writers actually do with the divine name.

The name that renders YHWH Disputed background · settled usage

Exactly what the earliest Greek copies of the Jewish Scriptures wrote where the Hebrew had YHWH, and what was said aloud over them, is genuinely debated: several pre-Christian copies preserve the Tetragrammaton in Hebrew letters rather than the Greek Kyrios. But the New Testament writers settle the question for their own usage. When they quote a passage where the Hebrew has YHWH, they write Kyrios — and they then take those very passages and apply them to Jesus. Whatever a first-century synagogue said aloud, that is what the apostles did in Greek, on the page. It is that move, repeated and deliberate, that carries the argument.

Yahweh’s own texts, spoken of Jesus The core pattern

Paul writes to the Romans: “If you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord… you will be saved,” and then clinches it: “For ‘everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved’” (Romans 10:9–13). That last line quotes Joel 2:32, where the Lord upon whose name one calls is YHWH himself. Paul takes a text about calling on the covenant name of God and, without a word of apology, makes it a text about calling on Jesus.

The hymn Paul quotes in Philippians reaches, for its language, into Isaiah 45 — one of the most fiercely monotheistic chapters in all of Scripture, where the LORD swears, “To me every knee shall bow… every tongue shall swear allegiance” (Isaiah 45:23), in the very passage that insists “I am the LORD, and there is no other.” The hymn bends that exclusive oath to Jesus: “at the name of Jesus every knee should bow… and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord” (Philippians 2:10–11). Peter does the same with the reverence Isaiah reserves for God: “in your hearts honor Christ the Lord as holy” (1 Peter 3:15) reworks Isaiah 8:13, where it is “the LORD of hosts” whom Israel must “honor as holy” — now redirected to Christ. And the writer to the Hebrews takes Psalm 102, a hymn addressed to the eternal Creator — “you, Lord, laid the foundation of the earth,” as Hebrews quotes it from the Greek — and puts it on the Father’s lips as words spoken to the Son (Hebrews 1:10–12).

A monotheist does not do this by accident, and he does not do it with a creature. To take the chapter of Isaiah that most insists there is no god beside the LORD, and to apply its exclusive oath to Jesus, is not to add a second, lesser lord to the picture. It is to say that the one who bears this name is the LORD.

Honest limit: that the Philippians hymn applies YHWH language specifically (rather than “Lord” more loosely) is itself a contested point among interpreters, though Isaiah 45 is among the most emphatically monotheistic chapters in Scripture. The hymn is widely regarded as pre-Pauline material Paul is quoting, though this is debated (Gordon Fee argues Paul composed it).

The name above every name Suggestive · debated

God “has highly exalted him and bestowed on him the name that is above every name” (Philippians 2:9). In a Jewish frame there is only one name above every name; it is the Name itself. The gift Jesus receives at his exaltation is not a promotion to some high rank among creatures but the disclosure that the name he bears — Kyrios, the word the New Testament writers use where the Hebrew has YHWH — is the divine one. On that reading, to confess “Jesus Christ is Lord” is to confess him with the creed’s own word for God.

“Before Abraham was, I am” Sharpest edge · one link weaker

Several times Jesus speaks an “I am” (eg&omacron; eimi) with no predicate to complete it — most startlingly, “before Abraham was, I am” (John 8:58), at which his hearers pick up stones. The closest and most widely recognized background is the LORD’s self-declaration in Isaiah 40–55 — “I am he” (Hebrew ’anî hû’), which the Greek Old Testament renders with the very same absolute eg&omacron; eimi: “understand that I am he. Before me no god was formed, nor shall there be any after me” (Isaiah 43:10).

Honest limit: a tie to God’s self-naming at the burning bush, “I AM WHO I AM” in Exodus 3:14, is often heard as well, but it is the weaker and more contested link, since the Greek there renders the divine name not by eg&omacron; eimi but by ho &omacron;n, “the One who is.” A determined critic can read the present tense as a mere “I have been,” and the saying still claims existence before Abraham; but the natural reading names an existence that never began, and the hearers plainly heard a claim only God could make — which is why they reached for stones.

Objection — “Kyrios just means ‘lord’”

The word is ordinary on the surface; it can mean “sir” or “master.”

Reply

This must be met on its merits, and part of it is granted: not every “Lord” on Jesus’s lips carries the freight of the Name. But the argument here does not rest on the ordinary uses; it rests on the loaded ones — the places where a YHWH-text is quoted and turned on Jesus.

Objection — “You have built a mountain on one creative theologian”

Several of the strongest divine-name texts are Paul’s, two of them in a single letter — so they are not, strictly, independent sources.

Reply

The material is not all of one kind or all from Paul: it runs across a fixed liturgical prayer (Marana tha), scriptural citations (Joel, Isaiah), a creedal reformulation (the Shema), the Petrine reworking of Isaiah 8, Hebrews’ use of Psalm 102, and the Johannine “I am” — and at least two of these are widely judged to be tradition Paul inherited rather than coined. A critic who wants the pattern gone must explain a Greek-speaking mission preserving, untranslated, an Aramaic prayer to Jesus it did not invent.

The room: the systematic character of Paul’s application of YHWH-texts to the Lord Jesus is catalogued in detail by David B. Capes; Bauckham and Wright read 1 Corinthians 8:6 as Paul reworking the Shema around Jesus — influential, arguably the dominant reading, and genuinely contested.
Defending Premise 2

This is inclusion within the one God, not a second deity: Paul reworks the Shema itself, distributing its two divine words — “one God, the Father… and one Lord, Jesus Christ” (1 Corinthians 8:6) — placing Jesus inside Israel’s creed (Bauckham’s “divine identity”; Wright). And it is early and Jewish: the untranslated Aramaic Marana tha (“Our Lord, come!”, 1 Corinthians 16:22) points back behind Paul’s Greek toward the earliest church. The one escape — that Jesus is a supreme agent (the shaliach) — cannot bear it: no agent was ever given YHWH’s exclusive oath, written into the Shema, or prayed to (the worship an angel refuses, Revelation 19:10).

Precision check: the claim is that Paul has not added a second deity alongside the creed — he has reached inside it and placed Jesus within it. It does not prove the doctrine of the Trinity from a single verse, and several of the readings it draws on are genuinely contested — the split of the Shema above all — so honesty names the scholars who read them the other way.

Inside the Shema Influential · contested

Writing to Corinth around AD 53, in a chapter explicitly about the danger of idolatry, Paul sets the gospel against the pagan world’s “many ‘gods’ and many ‘lords’” and then confesses: “yet for us there is one God, the Father… and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things” (1 Corinthians 8:6). To hear its force you have to hear the Shema underneath it. In the Greek of Paul’s Bible, Deuteronomy 6:4 runs Kyrios ho theos… Kyrios heis — the creed carries two words for the one God of Israel, theos (“God”) and Kyrios (“Lord,” standing for the Name). Paul takes those two words and distributes them: the “one God” is the Father, and the “one Lord” is Jesus Christ. He has not added a second deity alongside the creed; he has reached inside it and placed Jesus within it. Richard Bauckham calls this including Jesus in the “unique divine identity” of the God of Israel; N. T. Wright, that Paul has rewritten the Shema around Jesus.

Honest limit: the reading is influential, arguably dominant — and genuinely contested. What is not in doubt is that Paul does it in the middle of an argument against idolatry, where smuggling in a second divine power would be the very sin he is condemning.

The dilemma inside 1 Corinthians 8:6 Argued · answers McGrath

Because “one Lord” carries the creed’s own divine title, the “alongside” reading, in an anti-idolatry argument, yields not strict monotheism but a second divine power through whom all things were made — which is exactly what it set out to avoid. The reading that keeps monotheism is the one that confesses the one divine identity under both of the creed’s titles.

Honest limit: the single verse in 1 Corinthians 8:6 is contested: James McGrath argues that Paul sets “one Lord” alongside the one God rather than folding him inside the creed.

Early and Jewish: Marana tha Strong

Writing in Greek to Corinth in the early 50s, Paul preserves untranslated an Aramaic prayer his readers already knew: Marana tha, “Our Lord, come!” (1 Corinthians 16:22). Paul was in contact with the Jerusalem leaders within a few years of his conversion (Galatians 1:18–19), and he records his clashes openly — over circumcision with the “false brothers” (Galatians 2:3–5) and over table fellowship with Cephas himself (2:11–14); nowhere does he record any dispute over praying to Jesus as Lord. A formula his Greek readers knew untranslated, addressed to Jesus in prayer and never, so far as Paul records, disputed by Jerusalem, points back behind Paul’s Greek toward the earliest church. The devotion is primitive and Jewish, not late and Greek.

Honest limit: the consonants also permit maran atha, “Our Lord has come”; the imperative “Our Lord, come!” is the standard reading, supported by Didache 10:6 and Revelation 22:20.

Objection — the agency reading: a supreme shaliach Hardest

Second Temple Judaism knew of exalted intermediary figures — great angels, personified Wisdom, exalted patriarchs — who bear God’s authority, even his name, without being God. On this reading (pressed by Jehovah’s Witnesses and by biblical Unitarians) Jesus is God’s supreme agent, given the Name as an ambassador bears his king’s, not as the LORD himself.

Reply

This must be met on its merits. But an agent has a ceiling. The intermediary figures of Second Temple Judaism characteristically refuse worship and redirect it to God (“Worship God,” cries the angel in Revelation 19:10), whereas the first Christians prayed to Jesus, called on his name, and sang to him. No shaliach was ever given the exclusive oath of Isaiah 45 or written into the Shema. Agency conferred authority; it never conferred the Name and the worship together. So the agency reading does not hold.

Objection — Bousset: “Calling Jesus ‘Lord’ was a late, Greek development”

A century ago Wilhelm Bousset argued in Kyrios Christos that worship of Jesus as “Lord” arose not among the first Aramaic-speaking disciples but later, in the Hellenistic Gentile churches surrounded by the “lords” of pagan cult. Bousset’s defenders reply that he located the Kyrios-cult in bilingual Syrian centers — Damascus and Antioch — where Aramaic was spoken alongside Greek, so an Aramaic formula does not by itself reach behind that mission.

Reply

The most damaging reply is a single Aramaic word, Marana tha (see the support above): Paul was in contact with the Jerusalem leaders within a few years of his conversion, and nowhere does he record any dispute over praying to Jesus as Lord. The devotion is primitive and Jewish, not late and Greek. (James Dunn, who grants the early date, still resists the conclusion: for him “Lord” distinguishes Jesus from God rather than identifying him with God — an exalted Lord exercising God’s rule. But the distinction Dunn finds is one the Christian confession itself insists on: Jesus is distinguished from God the Father. The title that distinguishes him is the Name itself. Paul is not demoting Jesus by calling him “Lord” but including him in the Shema; far from a lesser title, “one Lord” — on the reading of Bauckham and Wright set out above, which McGrath contests and the dilemma above defends — places Jesus inside Israel’s confession of the one God. So Dunn’s dissent does not hold.)

The room: Bauckham and Wright read 1 Corinthians 8:6 as Paul reworking the Shema around Jesus — influential, arguably the dominant reading; James McGrath reads “one Lord” as set alongside the one God rather than inside the creed; James Dunn grants the early date but holds that Kyrios distinguishes Jesus from God rather than identifying him with God — his is the standing book-length dissent; Hurtado’s Lord Jesus Christ is the fullest reply to Bousset.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: the agency reading (Jehovah's Witnesses, biblical Unitarians) — that Jesus bears the divine Name the way an ambassador bears his king's, a supreme agent without being God. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the reply below.

"Jesus bears the Name as a supreme agent, not as God himself" Hard

Strongest form

The Jewish principle of agency (the shaliach) holds that "a man's agent is as the man himself." Second Temple Judaism knew exalted intermediaries — great angels, personified Wisdom, exalted patriarchs — who bear God's authority, even his name, without being God. On this reading Jesus is God's supreme agent, given the Name as an ambassador bears his king's; much New Testament language of his being "sent," "given," and "appointed" fits it naturally.

Reply

Grant the sending-language; the reading still has a ceiling. No shaliach in Jewish thought was ever given YHWH's exclusive oath ("to me every knee shall bow," Isaiah 45, applied to Jesus in Philippians 2), written into the Shema (1 Corinthians 8:6), or prayed to — the cultic devotion an angel in the same books refuses ("Worship God!", Revelation 19:10). Agency conferred authority; it never conferred the Name and the worship together. An ambassador speaks for the king; he does not receive the king's own exclusive oath and prayers.

"'Kyrios' just means 'lord' — an ordinary title of respect" Common

Strongest form

Kyrios is an everyday Greek word — "sir," "master." It is used of slaveowners and officials all through the New Testament. Calling Jesus "Lord" no more makes him God than calling a landowner "lord" does.

Reply

Granted, and the argument concedes it: not every "Lord" on Jesus's lips carries the freight of the Name. The case rests only on the loaded uses — the places where the New Testament quotes a text about YHWH and turns it on Jesus (Joel 2:32 → Romans 10:13; Isaiah 45:23 → Philippians 2), gives him "the name that is above every name," and prays to him in that name. It is the transfer of YHWH's own texts, not the bare word "Lord," that does the work.

"The Greek Old Testament didn't even use 'Kyrios' for YHWH" Trickier

Strongest form

Your premise — that Kyrios "is" the divine name — is shakier than you admit. Several pre-Christian copies of the Greek Scriptures preserve the Hebrew Tetragrammaton (or write ΙΑΩ), not Kyrios. So the Kyrios-for-YHWH equation may be a later Christian convention, not the Jewish one you're leaning on.

Reply

Concede the manuscript point outright — the essay states it plainly. What the earliest copies wrote, and what was said aloud, is genuinely disputed. But the argument does not depend on it, because the load-bearing fact is what the New Testament writers themselves do on the page: when they quote a passage where the Hebrew has YHWH, they write Kyrios, and they apply that passage to Jesus. Whatever a first-century synagogue said aloud, the apostles took YHWH-texts and spoke them of Christ.

"1 Corinthians 8:6 sets 'one Lord' alongside the one God, not inside it" Trickier

Strongest form

The scholar James McGrath argues directly against the Bauckham–Wright reading: Paul confesses the "one God" (the Father) and then adds "one Lord, Jesus Christ" alongside that confession, not folded inside it. Jesus is the exalted Messiah, God's supreme agent — a distinct, subordinate figure, not a person of the one God. Reading Nicaea into Paul is anachronism.

Reply

This objection has a factual kernel, and the kernel should be granted precisely: the verse is genuinely contested. But the word Kyrios creates a dilemma for the "alongside" reading. In the Shema Paul is echoing, Kyrios is the creed's own divine title. So if "one Lord" names a being who is not the one God, Paul has confessed — in an argument against idolatry — one God plus a second divine-but-not-God Lord "through whom are all things." That is not the strict monotheism the objection wants to protect; it is a second power. The reading that actually keeps monotheism is the one that confesses the one divine identity under both of the creed's titles.

"'Before Abraham was, I am' is just late Johannine theology, and claims only pre-existence" Trickier

Strongest form

The absolute "I am" sayings are only in John — the latest Gospel, decades on — so they are developed theology, not the historical Jesus. And even at face value, "before Abraham was, I am" claims pre-existence, not the divine name; a determined reader can render it "I have been."

Reply

Two things. First, the "I am" saying is not the foundation — the divine-name case rests on the earlier Pauline material (the YHWH-texts, the Shema); John makes vivid what is already there. Second, on the reading itself: the closest background is the LORD's self-declaration in Isaiah 40–55, "I am he," which the Greek Old Testament renders with the very same absolute egō eimi. Even on the milder "I have been," the reaction — an immediate attempt to stone him (John 8:59) — is hard to square with a tame remark about pre-existence; the hearers plainly heard a claim only God could make. (The tie to Exodus 3:14 is weaker, since the Greek there reads ho ōn.)

"Even if the New Testament applies Yahweh-texts to Jesus, that doesn't prove he is God" Hard

Strongest form

Grant the whole pattern — the YHWH-texts turned on Jesus, the reshaped Shema. All it shows is that some early Christians spoke of Jesus in exalted, name-bearing terms. That is a claim they made, not a claim that is true; and you have built much of it on one creative apostle, two of the texts in a single letter.

Reply

This objection has a factual kernel, and the kernel should be granted precisely. The argument establishes what the earliest Christians confessed — that Jesus bears the divine Name and Yahweh's own texts. And we hold that the confession is true: Jesus is the Son who shares the one divine Name with the Father, fully God and fully man. This argument does not prove all of that. It shows that the earliest Christians confessed Jesus as the LORD, early and on purpose, and it clears the charge that his deity was a later invention. And the honesty about sources is owed: several of the strongest texts are Paul's, two in one letter, so they are not strictly independent. What can be claimed is that they are different kinds of material — a fixed Aramaic prayer, scriptural citations, a creedal reformulation, the Johannine "I am" — at least two of them tradition Paul inherited rather than coined. A critic who wants the pattern gone must explain a Greek-speaking mission preserving, untranslated, an Aramaic prayer to Jesus it did not invent.

So Dunn's dissent does not hold. (Hurtado, an ally, frames the data as devotional practice.)
The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

The reading that Paul reworks the Shema around Jesus — Bauckham’s inclusion of Jesus in the “unique divine identity” of the God of Israel, Wright’s Shema rewritten around Jesus — is influential and genuinely contested. James F. McGrath argues that Paul sets “one Lord” alongside the one God rather than folding him inside the creed; Hurtado, broadly an ally, frames the data as devotional practice rather than in Bauckham’s identity categories. Paul’s application of YHWH-texts to the Lord Jesus is catalogued in detail by David B. Capes; what those texts mean for Jesus’s identity is where the debate lives.

A fairness most defenders skip: Several of the strongest divine-name texts are Paul’s, two of them in a single letter, so they are not, strictly, independent sources. What the earliest Greek copies wrote where the Hebrew had YHWH is genuinely debated — several pre-Christian copies preserve the Tetragrammaton rather than Kyrios. Whether the Philippians hymn invokes the Name specifically is debated, and the tie of John 8:58 to Exodus 3:14 is the weaker link. And the popular argument that echad in the Shema means a “compound” unity is a discredited overreach the case does not use.

The bounded verdict: The earliest Christians — monotheists who would die before bowing to an idol — took the name their people would not pronounce, and the Scriptures that name its bearer as the only God, and gave both to a crucified man, early, some of it in Aramaic, and on purpose. That is not the reverence owed a great teacher, or even a supreme angel; in a Jew it is a claim about the identity of God himself. And we hold that the confession is true: Jesus is the LORD — not a second god beside YHWH, and not the Father under another name, but the Son who shares the one divine Name with the Father; fully God and fully man, two natures in one person. This argument does not prove all of that. It shows that the earliest Christians confessed Jesus as the LORD, and it clears the charge that his deity was a later invention.
