Is Jesus Yahweh? The Divine-Name Argument

The deep dive explains the case. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild from memory how the New Testament gives Jesus the divine Name and Yahweh’s own texts, meet the “it is just an ordinary title” objection, and hold the answer together in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

There was a name the people of Israel would not say aloud — YHWH, the personal name of God. When their Scriptures were put into Greek, that name was rendered Kyrios, "Lord." It belonged to God alone: "I am the LORD; that is my name… my glory I give to no other" (Isaiah 42:8). Yet the earliest Christians — Jewish monotheists who guarded that name as jealously as anyone — took the texts that spoke of YHWH, and the name itself, and applied both to a crucified Galilean.

The load-bearing move is not a word-trick. (The popular echad "compound unity" argument is a discredited overreach, and this case sets it aside.) It is what the New Testament writers actually do with the divine name: where the Hebrew has YHWH they write Kyrios, and then they apply those very texts to Jesus. Joel's "everyone who calls on the name of the LORD" becomes Romans 10:13; Isaiah 45's exclusive oath "to me every knee shall bow" becomes Philippians 2:10–11; Isaiah 8's "honor the LORD of hosts as holy" becomes 1 Peter 3:15; Psalm 102, addressed to the Creator, is spoken by the Father to the Son in Hebrews 1:10–12. Paul even reaches inside the Shema, distributing its two words for the one God — "one God, the Father… and one Lord, Jesus Christ" (1 Corinthians 8:6) — placing Jesus within the one God, not beside him. And in John, Jesus speaks the LORD's own "I am he" of Isaiah: "before Abraham was, I am" (John 8:58).

Honest scope: this is inclusion within the unique divine identity of the one God (Bauckham) — Jesus identified with YHWH, never a second Yahweh, never the Father collapsed into the Son. It does not prove the doctrine of the Trinity from a single verse, and several of its readings are genuinely contested (the split of the Shema above all — McGrath reads "one Lord" as set alongside the creed). Nor does it settle the metaphysics of how the one God is Father, Son, and Spirit. What it establishes is remarkable enough: monotheists gave the crucified Jesus the one name no Israelite would give a creature — early, some of it in Aramaic, and on purpose.

Understand it

Not more prose — the load-bearing structure. See the skeleton, then learn to defend each bone.

The argument, formally

The New Testament gives Jesus the divine Name — and Yahweh's own texts — by design.

P1

Where Israel's Scriptures have the divine name YHWH, the New Testament writers write Kyrios ("Lord") — and they take those very texts and apply them to Jesus: Joel's "call on the name of the LORD" (Romans 10:13); Isaiah 45's exclusive oath "to me every knee shall bow" (Philippians 2:10–11); Isaiah 8's "honor the LORD of hosts as holy" (1 Peter 3:15); Psalm 102, addressed to the Creator, spoken to the Son (Hebrews 1:10–12). He is given "the name that is above every name" (Philippians 2:9), and in John he speaks the LORD's own "I am he" (John 8:58 with Isaiah 43:10). (Not the discredited echad word-play, which the case disowns.)

P2

This is inclusion within the one God, not a second deity: Paul reworks the Shema itself, distributing its two divine words — "one God, the Father… and one Lord, Jesus Christ" (1 Corinthians 8:6) — placing Jesus inside Israel's creed (Bauckham's "divine identity"; Wright). And it is early and Jewish: the untranslated Aramaic Marana tha ("Our Lord, come!", 1 Corinthians 16:22) points behind the Greek mission to the first Judean church. The one escape — that Jesus is a supreme agent (the shaliach) — cannot bear it: no agent was ever given YHWH's oath, written into the Shema, or prayed to.

∴ C

Therefore the New Testament identifies Jesus with YHWH — giving him the divine Name and the Scriptures that belong to its bearer alone. That is a claim about the identity of God himself, not the reverence owed a creature.

This does not prove the doctrine of the Trinity from a single verse, and it does not rest on the discredited claim that echad means a plural unity. Several readings are genuinely contested — the split of the Shema above all — and honesty names them. Its narrower, firmer point: monotheists gave the crucified Jesus the divine Name and Yahweh's own texts, early and by design. How the one God is Father, Son, and Spirit is the further work of the doctrine of the Trinity.

Why "'Lord' is just a title of respect" is the popular skeptic's move.

The strongest form is the agency reading (Jehovah's Witnesses, biblical Unitarians): Kyrios can mean an ordinary master, and Judaism knew exalted intermediaries — angels, personified Wisdom — who bear God's authority, even his name, without being God. The reply grants that not every "Lord" carries the freight of the Name — the argument rests on the loaded uses, the YHWH-texts turned on Jesus — and answers that no such agent was ever prayed to or given YHWH's exclusive oath.

Defending the premises

Why each premise is more plausible than its denial

Support for P1 — the divine Name and Yahweh's texts, applied to Jesus

The name that renders YHWH — a point handled with care

First, disown the overreach: the popular claim that echad ("one" in the Shema) means a "compound unity" is a discredited word-trick — the plurality in "one flesh" comes from the noun, not from echad, the ordinary Hebrew numeral. The real case is about the Name. When Israel's Scriptures were put into Greek, YHWH was rendered Kyrios. What the earliest copies wrote is genuinely disputed — some preserve the Tetragrammaton in Hebrew letters — so the load-bearing point is not what a synagogue said aloud but what the apostles do on the page: where the Hebrew has YHWH, the New Testament writers write Kyrios, and then apply those texts to Jesus.

Yahweh's own texts, spoken of Jesus

Paul tells the Romans "everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved" (Romans 10:13) — quoting Joel, where the Lord is YHWH. The Philippians hymn reaches into Isaiah 45, the chapter that most insists "I am the LORD, and there is no other," where the LORD swears "to me every knee shall bow," and bends that oath to Jesus (Philippians 2:10–11). Peter reworks Isaiah 8:13 ("honor the LORD of hosts as holy") into "honor Christ the Lord as holy" (1 Peter 3:15). Hebrews puts Psalm 102, addressed to the eternal Creator, on the Father's lips as words spoken to the Son (Hebrews 1:10–12). And Jesus is given "the name that is above every name" (Philippians 2:9) — in a Jewish frame, the Name itself. (Whether any single verse invokes the Name specifically is debated case by case; the pattern across them is the point.)

Support for P2 — inside the Shema, early and Jewish, and the agency escape

Inside the Shema — inclusion, not a second god

The boldest instance is one sentence from a former Pharisee, written in an argument against idolatry. In the Greek Shema, Deuteronomy 6:4 carries two words for the one God, theos and Kyrios; Paul distributes them — the "one God" is the Father, the "one Lord" is Jesus Christ (1 Corinthians 8:6). He has not added a second deity alongside the creed; he has placed Jesus within it — Richard Bauckham's "unique divine identity," N. T. Wright's rewritten Shema. The reading is influential, arguably dominant, and genuinely contested: James McGrath reads "one Lord" as set alongside the creed. But because "one Lord" carries the creed's own divine title, the "alongside" reading yields, in an anti-idolatry argument, a second divine power through whom all things were made — the very thing it set out to avoid. And in John, Jesus speaks the LORD's own absolute "I am" (John 8:58 with Isaiah 43:10; the Exodus 3:14 tie is weaker, since the Greek there is ho ōn).

Early and Jewish; the agency escape breaks

The devotion is not a late Greek import (Bousset): Paul preserves untranslated an Aramaic prayer to Jesus, Marana tha, "Our Lord, come!" (1 Corinthians 16:22), pointing behind the Greek mission to the earliest Judean church (Hurtado). The strongest counter-reading is agency — Jesus as God's supreme shaliach, bearing the Name as an ambassador bears his king's. But no agent in Jewish thought was ever prayed to, given YHWH's exclusive oath, or written into the Shema — the worship an angel in the same books refuses (Revelation 19:10). The honest limits: James Dunn grants the early date yet holds that "Lord" distinguishes Jesus from God rather than identifying him — the standing dissent this case must answer; and the strongest texts are Paul's, two in one letter, so they are different kinds of material rather than independent sources.

The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: the agency reading (Jehovah's Witnesses, biblical Unitarians) — that Jesus bears the divine Name the way an ambassador bears his king's, a supreme agent without being God. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the reply below.

"Jesus bears the Name as a supreme agent, not as God himself" Hard

Strongest form

The Jewish principle of agency (the shaliach) holds that "a man's agent is as the man himself." Second Temple Judaism knew exalted intermediaries — great angels, personified Wisdom, exalted patriarchs — who bear God's authority, even his name, without being God. On this reading Jesus is God's supreme agent, given the Name as an ambassador bears his king's; much New Testament language of his being "sent," "given," and "appointed" fits it naturally.

Reply

Grant the sending-language; the reading still has a ceiling. No shaliach in Jewish thought was ever given YHWH's exclusive oath ("to me every knee shall bow," Isaiah 45, applied to Jesus in Philippians 2), written into the Shema (1 Corinthians 8:6), or prayed to — the cultic devotion an angel in the same books refuses ("Worship God!", Revelation 19:10). Agency conferred authority; it never conferred the Name and the worship together. An ambassador speaks for the king; he does not receive the king's own exclusive oath and prayers.

"'Kyrios' just means 'lord' — an ordinary title of respect" Common

Strongest form

Kyrios is an everyday Greek word — "sir," "master." It is used of slaveowners and officials all through the New Testament. Calling Jesus "Lord" no more makes him God than calling a landowner "lord" does.

Reply

Granted, and the argument concedes it: not every "Lord" on Jesus's lips carries the freight of the Name. The case rests only on the loaded uses — the places where the New Testament quotes a text about YHWH and turns it on Jesus (Joel 2:32 → Romans 10:13; Isaiah 45:23 → Philippians 2), gives him "the name that is above every name," and prays to him in that name. It is the transfer of YHWH's own texts, not the bare word "Lord," that does the work.

"The Greek Old Testament didn't even use 'Kyrios' for YHWH" Trickier

Strongest form

Your premise — that Kyrios "is" the divine name — is shakier than you admit. Several pre-Christian copies of the Greek Scriptures preserve the Hebrew Tetragrammaton (or write ΙΑΩ), not Kyrios. So the Kyrios-for-YHWH equation may be a later Christian convention, not the Jewish one you're leaning on.

Reply

Concede the manuscript point outright — the essay states it plainly. What the earliest copies wrote, and what was said aloud, is genuinely disputed. But the argument does not depend on it, because the load-bearing fact is what the New Testament writers themselves do on the page: when they quote a passage where the Hebrew has YHWH, they write Kyrios, and they apply that passage to Jesus. Whatever a first-century synagogue said aloud, the apostles took YHWH-texts and spoke them of Christ.

"1 Corinthians 8:6 sets 'one Lord' alongside the one God, not inside it" Trickier

Strongest form

The scholar James McGrath argues directly against the Bauckham–Wright reading: Paul confesses the "one God" (the Father) and then adds "one Lord, Jesus Christ" alongside that confession, not folded inside it. Jesus is the exalted Messiah, God's supreme agent — a distinct, subordinate figure, not a person of the one God. Reading Nicaea into Paul is anachronism.

Reply

This is the fairest objection and must be conceded carefully: the verse is genuinely contested. But the word Kyrios creates a dilemma for the "alongside" reading. In the Shema Paul is echoing, Kyrios is the creed's own divine title. So if "one Lord" names a being who is not the one God, Paul has confessed — in an argument against idolatry — one God plus a second divine-but-not-God Lord "through whom are all things." That is not the strict monotheism the objection wants to protect; it is a second power. The reading that actually keeps monotheism is the one that confesses the one divine identity under both of the creed's titles.

"'Before Abraham was, I am' is just late Johannine theology, and claims only pre-existence" Trickier

Strongest form

The absolute "I am" sayings are only in John — the latest Gospel, decades on — so they are developed theology, not the historical Jesus. And even at face value, "before Abraham was, I am" claims pre-existence, not the divine name; a determined reader can render it "I have been."

Reply

Two things. First, the "I am" saying is not the foundation — the divine-name case rests on the earlier Pauline material (the YHWH-texts, the Shema); John makes vivid what is already there. Second, on the reading itself: the closest background is the LORD's self-declaration in Isaiah 40–55, "I am he," which the Greek Old Testament renders with the very same absolute egō eimi. Even on the milder "I have been," the reaction — an immediate attempt to stone him (John 8:59) — is hard to square with a tame remark about pre-existence; the hearers plainly heard a claim only God could make. (The tie to Exodus 3:14 is weaker, since the Greek there reads ho ōn.)

"Even if the New Testament applies Yahweh-texts to Jesus, that doesn't prove he is God" Hard

Strongest form

Grant the whole pattern — the YHWH-texts turned on Jesus, the reshaped Shema. All it shows is that some early Christians spoke of Jesus in exalted, name-bearing terms. That is a claim they made, not a claim that is true; and you have built much of it on one creative apostle, two of the texts in a single letter.

Reply

Correct on both counts, and the argument says so. It establishes what the earliest Christians confessed — that Jesus bears the divine Name and Yahweh's own texts — not that the confession is true; whether it is true is the separate work of the resurrection and Jesus's own words. And the honesty about sources is owed: several of the strongest texts are Paul's, two in one letter, so they are not strictly independent. What can be claimed is that they are different kinds of material — a fixed Aramaic prayer, scriptural citations, a creedal reformulation, the Johannine "I am" — at least two of them tradition Paul inherited rather than coined. A critic who wants the pattern gone must explain a Greek-speaking mission preserving, untranslated, an Aramaic prayer to Jesus it did not invent.
