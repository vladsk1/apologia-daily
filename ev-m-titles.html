The Titles of Jesus

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and stay clear and calm in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

The titles the New Testament gives Jesus are not random honorifics. Traced back to their Old Testament sources and read together, a cluster of them — Son of Man, Lord (kyrios), I AM, Son of God — places Jesus in roles, and finally under a name, that reach into the unique identity of the God of Israel.

"Son of Man" sounds humble to modern ears, which is why it is hard to explain as a title the later church coined for worship — yet its address is Daniel 7:13-14, a heavenly figure who comes "with the clouds of heaven" (outside that vision, an activity the Old Testament reserves for YHWH), receives universal dominion, and is served by all peoples — the clouds are the stronger half of the case. "Lord" (Greek kyrios) is the word the New Testament writers themselves use where the Hebrew has the divine name YHWH, and the New Testament repeatedly applies YHWH texts to Jesus. "I AM" recalls the LORD's self-declaration in Isaiah 40–55: "I am he"; when Jesus says "Before Abraham was, I AM" (John 8:58), his hearers reach for stones. "Son of God," though it can carry a royal sense, takes on a unique force in this company.

Honest scope: any single title can be debated — "son of man" is ordinary Hebrew idiom for "human being," and "son of God" was used of Israel's kings. So the argument is deliberately cumulative: it rests on the convergence of all the titles plus the hearers' own reaction (charges of blasphemy, the verdict at his trial), not on pressing one word too hard. And note what the titles do not do: they never cancel his humanity. “Son of Man” in particular names him as truly one of us at the same moment it points to Daniel 7. Fully God and fully man, permanently — that is the confession these titles serve.

Before you start · key terms

Eight words this argument leans on

Learn these first — the objections below turn on them, and most weak versions of the argument come from blurring one of them.

A cumulative claimThe claim under examination is not that any single title, by itself, proves the deity of Christ. The real claim is cumulative: that when the major christological titles are read in their first-century Jewish context, and read together, they place Jesus in roles, and finally under a name, that reach into the unique identity of the God of Israel.
Son of ManJesus’ favourite self-designation — it appears roughly eighty times in the Gospels, almost always on Jesus’ own lips, and almost never as a confession by others. To modern ears it sounds like a statement of humility, and that ordinary sense is real (as in Ezekiel). But the crucial background lies elsewhere — in a vision in Daniel 7, where “one like a son of man” comes “with the clouds of heaven.”
The cloud-rider’s placeRiding the clouds is YHWH’s alone — he “makes the clouds his chariot” (Psalm 104:3), he “is riding on a swift cloud” (Isaiah 19:1). Outside this vision, the Hebrew Scriptures give that way of coming to no one but God. The exclusive element was never the dominion: an argument resting its weight on everlasting rule is resting it on the wrong plank.
Pelach (Daniel’s service-verb)In the Aramaic of Daniel, the verb translated “serve” is throughout a verb of religious service — but not of service to the true God alone. The same book uses it of serving Nebuchadnezzar’s gods, and at 7:27 its object is, on one natural reading, the holy people rather than God himself. The word establishes cultic homage; it does not by itself establish who receives it.
Kyrios (“Lord”)On the surface an ordinary term of respect, like “sir” or “master.” What it had become in Greek-speaking Judaism is more disputed than apologetic summaries usually admit. But the New Testament writers settle the question for their own usage: when they quote a passage where the Hebrew has YHWH, they write Kyrios — and they then apply those very passages to Jesus.
Marana thaAn untranslated Aramaic formula Paul preserves while writing in Greek to a Greek city (1 Corinthians 16:22) — on the standard division, a prayer, “Our Lord, come!” An Aramaic invocation of Jesus as “Lord,” addressed to him in prayer, is very hard to explain as a coinage of the Greek-speaking mission.
The royal titles“Messiah” did not, by itself, mean “divine.” It meant God’s anointed king — the heir of David who would restore Israel; in this idiom, “Son of God” was a royal title. These titles are royal and human before they are anything more. This is not adoptionism — not that Jesus was a mere man later promoted to divine sonship. His unique, eternal Sonship is affirmed; the point is only the titles’ native range.
The absolute “I am”Several times in John, Jesus speaks an “I am” (egō eimi) with no predicate to complete it. The closest and most widely recognised background is the LORD’s self-declaration in Isaiah 40–55: “I am he,” which the Septuagint renders with the very same absolute egō eimi. A link to Exodus 3:14 is often heard as well, but it is the weaker and more contested tie.
The argument, formally

Trace each title to its source and see whose name it is.

P1

The New Testament gives Jesus a cluster of titles — Son of Man, Lord (kyrios), I AM, Son of God — each with a specific Old Testament address.

P2

Traced to their sources and read together, they reach into the unique identity of the God of Israel: Daniel 7's Son of Man comes on the clouds — elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible, God's own conveyance; kyrios is the word the New Testament writers use where the Hebrew has YHWH; I AM is the LORD's self-declaration in Isaiah 40–55: 'I am he.'

∴ C

Therefore the titles constitute a deliberate, cumulative claim to deity.

The argument is cumulative — any single title can be quibbled; the convergence of all of them, plus the hearers' reaction (reaching for stones), settles what was being claimed. Jesus claimed that place, and his first followers confessed it. We hold that this worship is rightly placed — that the one they called Lord is, as Nicaea confessed, true God from true God, one God with the Father and the Spirit — and, as the royal titles never let us forget, fully God and fully man.

The key surprise — his favourite title was the highest one.

'Son of Man' sounds humble to modern ears, and it is awkward for an invention theory: it appears about eighty times on Jesus' lips and almost never in the epistles — a title the church itself stopped using. (Some critics do argue the Danielic sayings are post-Easter — Vielhauer, Perrin, and Casey on the Aramaic. That is a real position. Casey's linguistic version is answered below; the broader form of it this page does not close, and the deep dive is where the trial scene's own disputed status is set out. What any version still has to explain is a title the church abandoned.) But its address is Daniel 7:13-14 — a heavenly figure who rides the clouds (outside that vision, the Old Testament gives that way of coming to no one but God), receives universal, everlasting dominion, and is served by all peoples — press the clouds, not the service-verb. Jesus chose the title whose height is hidden in plain sight — it names him as truly one of us and as Daniel 7's heavenly figure at once.

What follows — and what doesn’t

Don’t read the conclusion — reason to it

Working only from what the argument has established: what does each piece of evidence rule out, and how far does the inference reach? Reason each line out, then reveal.

Daniel’s figure comes “with the clouds of heaven” — and outside this vision, the Hebrew Scriptures give that way of coming to…?
no one but GodRiding the clouds is YHWH’s alone — he “makes the clouds his chariot” (Psalm 104:3), he “is riding on a swift cloud” (Isaiah 19:1). What no other figure is given is the cloud-rider’s place. Collins derives the scene from the Ugaritic Baal cycle, so on his account the image was never YHWH’s exclusive property; but the derivation is read in both directions: on the more common account the Hebrew Scriptures take the epithet from Baal and give it to YHWH polemically, so inside Israel’s own Scriptures it marks the one God off from every rival.
Daniel 7:27 appears to extend an everlasting kingdom to the holy people, and the Psalms promise an everlasting throne to a Davidic king — so everlasting dominion is…?
the wrong plank to rest weight onThe exclusive element was never the dominion: Daniel 7:27 appears to extend an everlasting kingdom to the holy people as well — though the referent of “his kingdom” there is debated — and the Psalms promise an everlasting throne to a Davidic king and pray the homage of the nations for him (Psalm 89:36–37; 72:8–11). It is the manner of his coming rather than the extent of his rule that carries the weight.
Daniel uses its service-verb of serving Nebuchadnezzar’s gods (3:12) — so the verb establishes…?
cultic homage, not who receives itThe verb is throughout a verb of religious service — but not of service to the true God alone, and at 7:27 its object is, on one natural reading, the holy people rather than God himself. Appeals that cite Daniel 3:12 as evidence that the verb denotes service to God alone have that verse backwards: it is the counter-example, not the support. Press the clouds, not the service-verb.
Paul writes Kyrios where the Hebrew has YHWH, then applies Joel’s “everyone who calls on the name of the Lord” to Jesus — so he has…?
applied a YHWH-text to JesusThat last line of Romans 10:9–13 quotes Joel 2:32, where “the Lord” upon whose name one calls is YHWH himself. Paul takes a text about calling on YHWH and applies it, without apology, to calling on Jesus. Whatever a first-century synagogue said aloud, that is what the apostles did in Greek, on the page.

points back behind the Greek-speaking mission (on a further judgment)It points back to the earliest Aramaic-speaking believers in Judea — the very community Bousset claimed had no such devotion. “Messiah,” “Son of David,” and royal “Son of God” — can any one of them, taken alone, settle the question of divinity?
no — they are royal and human first; the case is cumulativeA thoughtful first-century Jew could call a man “Messiah,” “Son of David,” even “Son of God” in the royal sense, while affirming the strictest monotheism. An argument that leaned on any one title in isolation would deserve to fail. What the titles establish is something subtler and, in the end, weightier: a cumulative pattern. Defending Premise 1

The New Testament gives Jesus a cluster of titles — Son of Man, Lord (kyrios), I AM, Son of God — each with a specific Old Testament address.

Precision check: the claim under examination is not that any single title, by itself, proves the deity of Christ. Several of Jesus’ most important titles are, in their native setting, thoroughly human — royal, but human. Premise 1 says only that each title has a specific Old Testament address; what those addresses amount to is Premise 2.

Son of Man: his own self-designation Strong

“Son of Man” is Jesus’ favourite self-designation — it appears roughly eighty times in the Gospels, almost always on Jesus’ own lips, and almost never as a confession by others — and nearly vanishes from the epistles. To modern ears it sounds like a statement of humility, and that ordinary sense is real (as in Ezekiel). But the crucial background lies elsewhere — in a vision in Daniel 7: “one like a son of man” comes “with the clouds of heaven.”

Honest limit: some critics do argue the Danielic sayings are post-Easter — Vielhauer, Perrin, and Casey on the Aramaic. That is a real position. What any version still has to explain is a title the church abandoned.

At the trial: Daniel 7 and Psalm 110 fused Debated hearing

At his hearing Jesus combines Daniel 7 and Psalm 110 — ‘you will see the Son of Man seated at the right hand of Power, coming with the clouds’ — and the high priest tears his robes and rules blasphemy. His enemies understood the claim. Exactly why this counted as blasphemy is itself debated — Darrell Bock argues it was the perceived arrogation of a divine prerogative, taking a seat enthroned in God’s presence, rather than the bare messianic claim — but blasphemy was the charge that stuck.

Honest limit: that hearing is itself disputed — no follower is placed inside the chamber, and critics argue Mark shaped the scene. Set the trial aside entirely and the pattern does not go with it: the same collision is already there in a Jesus who forgives sins on his own authority and draws the identical verdict of blasphemy from the scribes (“Who can forgive sins but God alone?”, Mark 2:5–7). Critics dispute these too — but the objection then has to be run separately against each.

Lord (Kyrios) and an Aramaic prayer Strong

Writing in Greek to a Greek city around AD 53–55, Paul preserves an untranslated Aramaic prayer addressed to Jesus: Marana tha — on the standard division, a prayer, “Our Lord, come!” (1 Corinthians 16:22). A Greek-speaking mission does not invent an Aramaic prayer it then leaves untranslated. It points back to the earliest Aramaic-speaking believers in Judea.

Honest limit: the letters also allow maran atha, “our Lord has come,” but Revelation 22:20’s “Come, Lord Jesus” and Didache 10:6 support the prayer reading.

The absolute “I am” Strong · John’s sayings

Several times in John, Jesus speaks an “I am” (egō eimi) with no predicate to complete it — “unless you believe that I am he you will die in your sins” (John 8:24), and, most sharply, “before Abraham was, I am” (John 8:58), at which his hearers pick up stones (8:59). The closest and most widely recognised background is the LORD’s self-declaration in Isaiah 40–55: “I am he,” which the Septuagint renders with the very same absolute egō eimi.

Honest limit: a link to God’s self-naming to Moses, “I AM WHO I AM” (Exodus 3:14), is often heard as well and may resonate, but it is the weaker and more contested tie. Because these are John’s sayings, the question of that Gospel’s late date and development bears on them as it does on Jesus’ other self-claims.

Messiah, Son of God, Son of David: the royal titles Royal and human first

“Messiah” did not, by itself, mean “divine.” It meant God’s anointed king — the heir of David who would restore Israel. In this idiom, “Son of God” was a royal title; the nation itself is called God’s son (Exodus 4:22). None of these titles, taken in isolation, settles the question of divinity. These titles are royal and human before they are anything more.

Honest limit: this is not adoptionism — not that Jesus was a mere man later promoted to divine sonship. His unique, eternal Sonship is affirmed; the point is only the titles’ native range. And the New Testament does not leave them where it found them: the Gospel writers press the royal sonship toward a unique, ontological sonship — Jesus is not merely a son but the Son, who knows the Father as no creature does (Matthew 11:27). The argument is not that “Son of God” proves deity, but that the New Testament authors expand the title beyond its original banks.

Objection — Bousset: kyrios devotion arose later, in Gentile churches Hardest

In Kyrios Christos (1913), Wilhelm Bousset argued that worship of Jesus as “Lord” did not arise among the first Aramaic-speaking disciples in Palestine. It developed later, in the Hellenistic Gentile churches — at places like Antioch — where Greek-speaking converts, surrounded by the “lords” and divinised rulers of pagan cult, gradually elevated Jesus to a cultic “Lord.” On this thesis, the high Christology of Kyrios is a secondary Greek import, not the faith of Jesus’ first followers.

Reply

The most damaging reply came from Larry Hurtado, whose Lord Jesus Christ (2003) set out to replace Bousset’s book as the standard treatment. His counter-evidence is a single Aramaic word. Marana tha will not behave as Bousset’s theory needs: an Aramaic invocation of Jesus as “Lord” (Mar), addressed to him in prayer, is very hard to explain as a coinage of the Greek-speaking mission. On Hurtado’s reading, devotion to Jesus as Lord is not late and not Greek; it is primitive and Jewish. Two replies are on record and should be stated. Bousset located the Kyrios-cult in the bilingual Syrian centres — Damascus and Antioch — so an Aramaic formula does not by itself reach behind that mission; the weight of Marana tha rests on the further judgment that Paul is quoting something already fixed when he reached it. And James D. G. Dunn, who accepts the early date, denies the inference: for him the kyrios title distinguishes Jesus from God rather than identifying him with God. Our answer to Dunn: an emissary could bear the sender’s name and authority, but not the cultic devotion reserved for God alone — and Marana tha, on the standard division, is prayer addressed directly to Jesus.

Objection — Casey: “Son of Man just means a human being”

The late Maurice Casey of Nottingham, an Aramaic specialist, argued that the phrase bar (e)nash(a) was simply a generic idiom — a roundabout way of saying “a man,” sometimes used by a speaker to refer obliquely to himself. The titular, apocalyptic “Son of Man” is then a creation of the Greek-speaking church. Casey’s view, building on Geza Vermes, is a serious linguistic proposal that has to be met on linguistic ground.

Reply

Paul Owen and David Shepherd argued that the emphatic form bar enasha does not behave as Casey’s general-idiom thesis requires; Casey replied vigorously in print. The dispute is genuine and not fully settled. But the Gospels overwhelmingly use the doubly definite Greek form, ho huios tou anthrōpou — “the Son of the Man” — an oddly specific construction for a generic “someone.” That is the more durable point, though Casey answers it by making the definiteness an artefact of translation into Greek, and that exchange with Owen and Shepherd is where the dispute actually lives. Casey’s view, though a minority position, is not refuted to everyone’s satisfaction.

The room: Dunn grants the early date and denies that kyrios identifies Jesus with YHWH; Casey and Vermes on the Aramaic; Owen and Shepherd press back. On the central matter — whether high devotion to Jesus was early or late — the scholarly tide has largely turned against Bousset’s Hellenistic derivation and toward Hurtado, Bauckham, and Martin Hengel. That is a judgment about the date of the devotion, not a settled verdict on its content.
Defending Premise 2

Traced to their sources and read together, they reach into the unique identity of the God of Israel: Daniel 7’s Son of Man comes on the clouds — elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible, God’s own conveyance; kyrios is the word the New Testament writers use where the Hebrew has YHWH; I AM is the LORD’s self-declaration in Isaiah 40–55: ‘I am he.’

Precision check: the claim is cumulative, and bounded. The titles do not give us a single knockdown proof of his deity, and an argument that leaned on any one title in isolation would deserve to fail. Who Daniel’s own figure refers to remains genuinely unsettled — corporate, angelic and individual-heavenly readings all have serious defenders — and the argument runs through what Jesus did with the scene, not through Daniel’s referent.

The clouds: the stronger half of Daniel 7 Stronger half

Riding the clouds is YHWH’s alone — he “makes the clouds his chariot” (Psalm 104:3), he “is riding on a swift cloud” (Isaiah 19:1). Outside this vision, the Hebrew Scriptures give that way of coming to no one but God. The exclusive element was never the dominion: Daniel 7:27 appears to extend an everlasting kingdom to the holy people as well — though the referent of “his kingdom” there is debated — and the Psalms promise an everlasting throne to a Davidic king and pray the homage of the nations for him (Psalm 89:36–37; 72:8–11). An argument resting its weight on everlasting rule is resting it on the wrong plank. What no other figure is given is the cloud-rider’s place.

Honest limit: Collins derives the scene from the Ugaritic Baal cycle, so on his account the image was never YHWH’s exclusive property to begin with. That derivation is read in both directions: on the more common account the Hebrew Scriptures take the cloud-rider epithet from Baal and give it to YHWH polemically. Collins’s derivation is a live account of where the imagery came from, and it is one reason this page does not claim to settle Daniel’s referent.

The service-verb (pelach) Suggestive, not decisive

In the Aramaic of Daniel, the verb translated “serve” (pelach) is throughout a verb of religious service — but not of service to the true God alone. The same book uses it of serving Nebuchadnezzar’s gods (3:12, 18), and at 7:27 its object is, on one natural reading, the holy people rather than God himself. The word establishes cultic homage; it does not by itself establish who receives it.

What Jesus does with the scene Strong · debated hearing

Second Temple Judaism did exalt intermediary figures very high, so the honest reply is not that no one was ever exalted. It is what Jesus does with the text. Jesus fuses Daniel 7:13 with Psalm 110:1 (Mark 14:62) — and the seat alone is not the point, since Psalm 110 seats a Davidic lord. What is startling is who takes it, and with what: a prisoner in the dock, claiming that seat in God’s own presence and Daniel’s clouds with it. That combination is not a higher rung on the ladder of creatures but the Creator’s own side of the line. An archangel receiving a stewardship on Israel’s behalf is not that claim.

Lord (Kyrios): YHWH-texts applied to Jesus Strong

When the New Testament writers quote a passage where the Hebrew has YHWH, they write Kyrios — and they then apply those very passages to Jesus. Paul writes to the Romans: “if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord… you will be saved… For everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved” (Romans 10:9–13). That last line quotes Joel 2:32, where “the Lord” upon whose name one calls is YHWH himself. Paul takes a text about calling on YHWH and applies it, without apology, to calling on Jesus. And Paul takes Isaiah 45:23, where YHWH swears “to me every knee shall bow,” and bends it to the name of Jesus (Philippians 2:10–11).

Honest limit: what Kyrios had become in Greek-speaking Judaism is more disputed than apologetic summaries usually admit — both what the earliest copies wrote and what was said aloud over them. But the New Testament writers settle the question for their own usage. Whatever a first-century synagogue said aloud, that is what the apostles did in Greek, on the page.

1 Corinthians 8:6: the Shema reworked Influential · contested

Paul appears to take the Shema and split it around two figures: “for us there is one God, the Father… and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things.” Richard Bauckham has argued influentially — though the reading is itself contested, not settled — that Paul is not adding Jesus alongside the one God as a second deity, but including Jesus within the unique identity of the one God of Israel.

Honest limit: James F. McGrath disputes Bauckham’s divine-identity category outright, and Hurtado and Bauckham themselves differ over what carries the weight — devotional practice or divine identity.

The absolute “I am” Natural reading · floor is lower

At its floor the saying claims existence before Abraham; a determined critic can stop there. But the present-tense “I am,” set against Abraham’s aorist “came to be,” more naturally names an existence that never began — and on the Isaianic background his hearers most naturally heard a claim to the divine name itself, which is why they took up stones. The modest reading already sets Jesus before creation; the natural one sets him on the divine side of the Creator/creature line.

Objection — “Daniel 7’s Son of Man is a symbol for Israel” Hard

Daniel interprets the vision himself: the kingdom is given to ‘the saints of the Most High’ (Daniel 7:18, 22, 27). The Son of Man is corporate Israel, not a divine individual. A second reading, John J. Collins’s, takes the figure as an angelic being, most probably Michael. Both are serious readings, and neither is fringe.

Reply

These are rivals, not allies: on the corporate reading the figure is a symbol for the people; on Collins’s, he is a heavenly individual — most probably Michael — who represents them. (That is a question about Daniel’s figure, not about Jesus: the Jehovah’s Witness error is not the name Michael but the creaturehood it carries. Hebrews asks the question the other way: “For to which of the angels did God ever say, ‘You are my Son’?” [1:5].) The corporate reading has to account for an asymmetry in the vision’s own imagery: the beast-kingdoms are given the chaos-sea; this figure is given the clouds. The angelic reading has its own ceiling: a reading that seats a creature in the cloud-rider’s mode of coming relocates the anomaly rather than removing it — it owes an account of the substitution, not merely a source for the imagery. Collins’s angelic reading is the harder one, and the deep dive states it in full — including his own reply. Jewish tradition did read the figure as an individual, and read him high — the Similitudes of Enoch describe a heavenly Son of Man named before the stars were made (1 Enoch 48:2-6), and 4 Ezra 13 an individual deliverer. The Similitudes’ date is disputed and they are unattested at Qumran; 4 Ezra is later than Jesus outright (c. AD 100). Nothing rests on either; what they do show is that the Danielic figure was read in Jewish tradition as an individual, and read very high. This does not settle Daniel’s own referent, and the argument does not need it to.

Objection — “Kyrios proves nothing — Caesar was kyrios too”

Lord was common coin — slaves used it for masters and the empire used it for Caesar. Early Christians calling Jesus kyrios is loyalty language, not the divine name.

Reply

Context selects the register. You can call your master kyrios without blasphemy; you cannot relocate YHWH’s own verses onto a man — unless you mean it.

The room: Bauckham and Hurtado defend the convergence (differing over what carries the weight); Collins, Casey and Dunn press the hardest alternatives; McGrath disputes the divine-identity category. The cumulative form needs a word of its own: stacking individually inconclusive inferences does not by itself produce a strong one — what has to be shown is that they fail independently. Three of the strongest data — Marana tha (1 Corinthians 16:22), Joel applied to him (Romans 10:13), and the Shema reworked around him (1 Corinthians 8:6) — are all Paul’s, and two are in one letter, so they are not independent sources. What can be claimed is narrower and still substantial: they are three different kinds of material — a fixed liturgical formula, a scriptural citation, a creedal reformulation — and at least two are often judged material Paul inherited rather than coined, which is what pushes them behind him. It is also true that titles develop: the question is whether that growth was the invention of something foreign to Jesus and his first followers, or the unfolding of something already present in seed. The titles alone do not prove deity; they locate him inside what Israel reserved for her God — and, as the royal titles never let us forget, he is fully God and fully man.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: Bousset’s thesis — that kyrios devotion arose later, in Greek-speaking Gentile churches. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"Son of God just means a righteous man or king" Common

Strongest form

Israel is called God's son; so are kings and angels. The title is honorific, not metaphysical.

Reply

Correct about the background — which is why the argument never rests on that title alone. But notice what the Gospels do with it: the Son knows the Father exclusively and reciprocally (Matthew 11:27), works on the Sabbath as the Father works (John 5:17-18) — and John tells us the hearers took that as 'making himself equal with God.' The generic title gets filled with non-generic content.

"Jesus never says I am God in plain words" Common

Strongest form

If Jesus were claiming deity, he could have just said it. Instead you need title archaeology — the absence of the plain claim is the tell.

Reply

Say 'I am God' to a first-century Jewish crowd and it lands as 'I am the Father' — a confusion of the persons, and not what Jesus was claiming; to Greek ears it would land as a pagan god-man. Jesus made the claim the precise way his categories allowed: taking YHWH's titles, prerogatives (forgiving sins, judging the world), and name. His audience did not miss it — they picked up stones, twice, and at his hearing blasphemy was the charge that stuck. The plain claim is written in their reaction.

"Kyrios proves nothing — Caesar was kyrios too" Trickier

Strongest form

Lord was common coin — slaves used it for masters and the empire used it for Caesar. Early Christians calling Jesus kyrios is loyalty language, not the divine name.

Reply

Context selects the register. The earliest Christians applied YHWH-texts to Jesus with kyrios in place: 'everyone who calls on the name of the LORD' (Joel 2:32) becomes calling on Jesus (Romans 10:13); the Isaiah 45 every-knee oath lands on him (Philippians 2). You can call your master kyrios without blasphemy; you cannot relocate YHWH's own verses onto a man — unless you mean it.

"Son of Man just means a human being" Trickier

Strongest form

In Ezekiel, son of man simply means mortal — God calls Ezekiel that ninety times. Jesus was using ordinary Aramaic for 'someone like me.'

Reply

Then explain the definite article — Jesus says the Son of Man, a specific figure — and the contexts: coming on the clouds, an everlasting kingdom, the service of all peoples — the seat at the right hand is Psalm 110's, fused in at the trial. That is not Ezekiel's address; it is Daniel 7's — the clouds, the Ancient of Days, the everlasting dominion, in order. (Not word-for-word: Daniel's Aramaic has the indefinite 'like a son of man,' where the Gospels' Greek is doubly definite, 'the Son of the Man' — an oddly specific phrase for a generic 'someone.') The high priest did not tear his robes over the word 'human.' The doubly-definite Greek is the more durable point, though Casey answers it by making the definiteness an artefact of translation into Greek; Owen and Shepherd pushed back on linguistic grounds, Casey answered them in print, and that exchange is where the dispute actually lives.

"The titles grew in the telling — Mark low, John high" Hard

Strongest form

Compare the Gospels: Mark's Jesus is reticent; John's declaims I AM. The titles inflate exactly as legend theory predicts — later equals higher.

Reply

Mark — the earliest Gospel — opens with the LORD's way prepared (Isaiah 40, a YHWH text), has Jesus forgiving sins to cries of blasphemy in chapter 2, walking the sea and saying ego eimi in chapter 6, and claiming Daniel 7 at trial in chapter 14. And earlier than every Gospel, Paul's letters already give Jesus the divine name. The trajectory legend theory needs — low early, high late — is simply not in the data; high is there at the start. It is also true that titles develop: communities reflect, and meanings deepen over decades. The question is whether that growth was the invention of something foreign to Jesus and his first followers, or the unfolding of something already present in seed.

"Daniel 7’s Son of Man is a symbol for Israel" Hard

Strongest form

Daniel interprets the vision himself: the kingdom is given to 'the saints of the Most High.' The Son of Man is corporate Israel, not a divine individual.

Reply

Two rival readings sit behind this, and they are not allies: on the corporate reading the figure is a symbol for the people; on John Collins's, he is a heavenly individual — most probably Michael — who represents them. (That is a question about Daniel's figure, not about Jesus: the Jehovah's Witness error is not the name Michael but the creaturehood it carries.) Take the corporate one first. The saints receive the kingdom with their representative, and king-and-people are standard biblical doubles (David and Israel). But the figure himself rides clouds (imagery the rest of the Old Testament gives to YHWH alone) and receives pelach — in Daniel a verb of religious service. It is suggestive rather than decisive: the same verb covers service to Nebuchadnezzar's gods (3:12), and in wider Aramaic the service owed a sovereign. The clouds are the stronger half. Collins's angelic reading is the harder one, and the deep dive states it in full — including his own reply. And Jewish tradition did read the figure as an individual, and read him high — the Similitudes of Enoch describe a heavenly Son of Man named before the stars were made (1 Enoch 48:2-6), and 4 Ezra 13 an individual deliverer. The Similitudes' date is disputed and they are unattested at Qumran; 4 Ezra is later than Jesus outright (c. AD 100). Nothing rests on either; what they show is that Jesus' use of the figure was provocative, not eccentric.

The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

Several debates are real and ongoing. The exact force of “Son of Man” in Aramaic remains contested among specialists; Casey’s view, though a minority position, is not refuted to everyone’s satisfaction. Who Daniel’s own figure refers to remains genuinely unsettled too — corporate, angelic and individual-heavenly readings all have serious defenders (that is a question about Daniel’s figure, not about Jesus), and the argument does not claim to settle it; it runs through what Jesus did with the scene, not through Daniel’s referent. On the central matter — whether high devotion to Jesus was early or late — the scholarly tide has largely turned against Bousset’s Hellenistic derivation and toward Hurtado, Bauckham, and Martin Hengel.

A fairness most defenders skip: That is a judgment about the date of the devotion, not a settled verdict on its content: Dunn grants the early date and still denies that kyrios identifies Jesus with YHWH, and James F. McGrath disputes Bauckham’s divine-identity category outright. Hurtado and Bauckham themselves differ over what carries the weight — devotional practice or divine identity — so “the tide has turned” should not be heard as one agreed position. And three of the strongest data — an Aramaic prayer addressed to Jesus, a YHWH-text from Joel applied to him, and the Shema reworked around him — are all Paul’s, and two are in one letter, so they are not independent sources; what can be claimed is narrower and still substantial: they are three different kinds of material, and at least two are often judged material Paul inherited rather than coined.

The bounded verdict: The titles of Jesus do not give us a single knockdown proof of his deity, and we should not pretend otherwise. What they establish is a cumulative pattern: read together and in context, they show the first Christians — Jewish monotheists who would die before bowing to an idol — taking Daniel’s cloud-borne “son of man,” the worship-language of Joel, the very confession of the Shema, and the unspeakable Name itself, and applying all of it to a crucified Galilean. They did this early, in Aramaic, in prayer. The titles locate him inside what Israel reserved for her God. Jesus claimed that place, and his first followers confessed it. We hold that this worship is rightly placed — that the one they called Lord is, as Nicaea confessed, true God from true God, one God with the Father and the Spirit — and, as the royal titles never let us forget, fully God and fully man. The titles alone do not prove it; that case is argued from Scripture and the resurrection.

    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    A messianic claim does not appear, on its own, to have counted as blasphemy: about a century later, by rabbinic tradition, the leading rabbi Akiba hailed the rebel Simon bar Kosiba as “King Messiah” and drew a colleague’s ridicule, not a charge of blasphemy. Exactly why Jesus’ answer counted as blasphemy is itself debated — Darrell Bock argues it was the perceived arrogation of a divine prerogative, taking a seat enthroned in God’s presence, rather than the bare messianic claim — but blasphemy was the charge that stuck.
