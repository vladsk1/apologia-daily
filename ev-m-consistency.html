The Internal Consistency of Scripture

The deep dive tells you what the argument is. This track makes you able to use it — reconstruct it from memory, answer its hardest objections, and hold the line in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

The argument from internal consistency observes that Scripture was written by some forty authors across roughly 1,500 years — kings and shepherds, fishermen and physicians, in three languages — who for the most part never met. Despite this, the collection presents a single, developing storyline: creation, fall, the calling of a people, and redemption culminating in Christ. The claim is not wooden uniformity — the writers differ widely in style, genre, and emphasis — but a deeper convergence of one moral vision and one redemptive arc. Such unity across so many hands and centuries is offered as best explained by a single guiding Author working through the human writers.

This argument should be made carefully. Many faiths have unified texts produced by one author or a short tradition, so the force here lies specifically in coherence achieved across vast diversity of authorship and time, not in the mere fact of agreement.

The honest scope: Scripture also contains real tensions and difficult passages, and skeptics point to a long process of canon formation that could have selected for unity. So this is an inference to the best explanation rather than a proof, and like the other reliability arguments it speaks to the character and origin of the texts — not, on its own, to the truth of every claim they contain; whether it is true, up to and including Easter morning, is argued on its own ground, in the historical case for the resurrection.

Before you start · key terms

Eight words this argument leans on

Learn these first — the objections below turn on them, and most weak versions of the argument come from blurring one of them.

A difference is not a contradictionThe law of non-contradiction states that the same proposition cannot be both true and false in the same sense at the same time. Two accounts contradict only if they cannot both be true — not merely if they differ, emphasize different things, or one mentions what the other omits.
A library, not one bookThe Bible is dozens of documents written by perhaps forty authors over roughly fifteen centuries, in Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek, spanning law, chronicle, poetry, prophecy, proverb, letter, apocalypse, and ancient biography. Genre governs what counts as accuracy: a parable is not falsified by being fictional.
Cautionary principlesSelection is not denial; difference is not discrepancy; accuracy is not precision; paraphrase is not misquotation; thematic ordering is not error. A misconstrued referent or a frank mystery is not the same thing as a demonstrated contradiction. The toolkit is for disciplined humility, not a skeleton key that opens every lock.
“Even a possible explanation”The rule, in A. T. Robertson’s words: “even a possible explanation is sufficient to meet the objector.” A possible reconciliation is enough to defeat the charge of a formal contradiction — one need not prove which harmonization is the correct one, only that the two statements can both be true. The humility runs the other way too: it licenses no pretense that every hard case has been tidily solved.
Reading “horizontally”Bart Ehrman’s method: stop reading the Gospels “vertically,” each blended into a single harmonized story, and set parallel accounts side by side, noticing where they conflict. The differences his cases point to are really there in the text.
Ancient biography (bios)The Gospels belong to the genre of Greco-Roman biography. Michael Licona catalogued how Plutarch, telling the same episode more than once, compresses time, transfers a saying, displaces events, simplifies participants, and paraphrases — accepted devices taught in the rhetorical handbooks. The thesis is contested, by skeptics and by some inerrantist scholars alike.
Convergent-yet-varied testimonyAccounts that differ on incidentals but converge on a common core show the pattern of substantially independent witnesses — the pattern courts treat as a mark of authenticity rather than collusion. Independent testimony to a real event almost never lines up perfectly; perfect alignment would smell of collusion.
Removing a defeaterDemonstrating consistency removes a defeater; it does not, by itself, prove the contents true — an internally consistent novel is still a novel. Clearing away the charge of contradiction only earns the texts a fair hearing; it does not win the case.
The argument, formally

Three lines. The whole weight rests on the two premises.

P1

If a body of writings composed by dozens of authors over many centuries nonetheless presents a single, coherent and developing message, that unity is best explained by a guiding mind behind it.

P2

Scripture, written by some forty authors across roughly 1,500 years, displays this unity — partly by deliberate inheritance, but also in forward-leaning patterns the earliest authors could not have engineered.

∴ C

Therefore, the unity of Scripture is best explained by a single guiding Author behind its many human writers — a pointer, not a proof.

The logic is an inference to the best explanation — if both premises hold, the conclusion is the most reasonable account. So every serious objection either denies that the unity is real (P2) or proposes a better explanation than design (P1). Knowing which premise an objection targets is half the battle.

Then the conceptual step — what kind of unity are we claiming?

Not wooden uniformity — the writers differ in style, era, and genre. The claim is a deeper convergence: one storyline, one moral vision, one redemptive arc running from beginning to end.

A single storyline Supporting

From the rupture of Genesis 3 to the restored creation of Revelation 21–22, Scripture tells one continuous story of creation, fall, redemption, and consummation. The garden lost at the start is the city regained at the end.

Theological threads that span the canon Supporting

Covenant, sacrifice, kingship, temple, and a coming Messiah are introduced in the earliest books and developed coherently across centuries of later writing. The New Testament quotes and fulfills the Old hundreds of times — a deliberate, traceable continuity, not coincidence.

Honest limit: Later authors did write within the earlier books, and that inheritance explains some of the unity; what is hardest to explain on inheritance alone is the forward-leaning pattern the earliest authors could not have engineered.

An old question, with old answers Historical

The problem is old, and so are the serious answers. As early as the second century, the pagan critic Celsus and the Christian heretic Marcion both pressed the disunity of the texts — Marcion so far as to reject the Old Testament and most of the New. Against Marcion, Irenaeus argued that one God stood behind both Testaments and the fourfold Gospel.

Objection — “The Bible is full of contradictions”

The resurrection accounts differ on the number of women and angels; the Gospels report Judas’ death differently. A book with internal contradictions can’t claim a unifying Author.

Reply

A difference is not a contradiction. The law of non-contradiction states that the same proposition cannot be both true and false in the same sense at the same time. Two accounts contradict only if they cannot both be true — not merely if they differ, emphasize different things, or one mentions what the other omits. “There was an angel at the tomb” and “there were two angels at the tomb” look like a clash, but they are not formal contradictories: a report of one does not deny a second unless it says “only one.” These are differences in detail and emphasis, not logical contradictions — the kind of variation expected from substantially independent witnesses, which courts treat as a mark of authenticity rather than collusion. The resurrection accounts differ on incidentals but converge on a striking common core: Jesus was buried, the tomb was found empty on the first day of the week, women were the first witnesses, and the risen Jesus appeared. The genuinely harder divergence here is the location of the appearances—Matthew and Mark point the disciples to Galilee, while Luke keeps the appearances in and around Jerusalem—and that tension should be named, not glossed; the usual harmonizations (appearances in both regions over the forty days) are plausible but not airtight. Most alleged biblical contradictions are differences of selection, perspective, or paraphrase. Some, however, are genuinely harder, and we will not pretend otherwise.

Objection — the hard cases: Judas’s death and the day of the crucifixion

The Gospels report Judas’ death differently, and Mark and John differ on the day of the crucifixion. Matthew and Acts even disagree on who bought the field and why it was called the Field of Blood.

Reply

Two are genuinely hard and worth conceding rather than hammering flat. Judas’s death admits a reasonable harmonization, but one we supply rather than either text stating it. The day of the crucifixion is a case where the data underdetermine the answer: many scholars judge that John presents Jesus as the Passover Lamb for theological reasons, while others hold that “day of Preparation” simply means the Friday of Passover week. On either reading, what both report — Jesus crucified under Pilate at Passover — stands untouched. Conceding the hard cases is what makes the rest credible. The honest verdict is not that every difference dissolves, but that the residue of genuinely hard cases is small, peripheral, and powerless to overturn the central narrative they all share.

Objection — “Harmonizing the Gospels means inventing a fifth Gospel”

Ehrman’s deeper claim is that harmonizers cheat. To reconcile the accounts, he argues, you must invent a fifth Gospel that none of the four actually wrote — splicing details together into a composite story that flattens each author’s distinct theological voice.

Reply

The reply begins by conceding Ehrman’s strongest point: crude harmonization can distort the text, and the Evangelists were authors with theological aims, not stenographers. The central empirical point is Licona’s Plutarch comparison case: when we watch a single, respected ancient author narrate the same event two ways, he does precisely what the Gospels do — which means these features are the fingerprints of the genre, not of falsehood or of late legend. Craig Keener has set Suetonius’s and Tacitus’s accounts of the emperor Otho side by side — two respected ancient authors writing within living memory of the same events — and finds them diverging in order, emphasis, and detail as freely as any two Gospels, while converging enough to show that neither invented his material but both drew on earlier sources. Licona’s thesis is itself contested, and not only by skeptics. Some inerrantist scholars, notably Norman Geisler before his death, sharply criticized Licona for conceding that the Evangelists altered details at all, arguing that it surrenders too much. Ehrman, from the other side, would reply that calling a change a “compositional device” rather than a “contradiction” is a relabeling that does not dissolve the underlying tension. Licona’s framework explains why the differences exist and shows they were not regarded as falsehoods; it does not turn every hard case into a smooth one. It lowers the temperature; it does not end the debate.

The room: On the narrower question of Gospel discrepancies, the field is genuinely divided, and the division is honest. Critics like Ehrman hold that some differences are true contradictions that no harmonization repairs without special pleading. Defenders like Blomberg and Licona hold that, read in their ancient context, the differences are overwhelmingly the expected variation of independent and genre-bound testimony. Many scholars who affirm the Gospels’ substantial reliability accept that the Evangelists used ancient compositional conventions, which means conceding more literary freedom than older harmonizers allowed while denying that this amounts to error. Others who hold a stricter view of inerrancy have criticized that position, which shows the debate is live inside the church as well as across its borders; this page reports that intra-Christian debate without adjudicating it. What few working scholars hold is the popular extreme on either end: that the Bible is a hopeless tangle of contradictions, or that it contains no real differences requiring thought.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

This is what separates someone who knows the argument from someone who can defend it. Each gets its best form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: that the canon selected for unity — and that Judas’s death and the crucifixion day remain genuinely hard. The deep dive takes up the hard cases at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"The unity was manufactured by later editors" Trickier

Strongest form

Editors and councils harmonized and curated the texts after the fact, so the apparent unity is an artifact of redaction, not original design.

Reply

Editing can smooth wording; it cannot retroactively plant a coherent, forward-looking storyline into documents written centuries apart. The deep structural unity — earlier texts setting up themes that later ones fulfill — is exactly what editorial polishing cannot fabricate.

"The canon was selected for unity" Hard

Strongest form

The books were chosen because they agreed; unity is an artifact of selection.

Reply

Part of this should be granted: P2 already concedes that some of the unity is deliberate inheritance, and the books were gathered over time. But that gathering was a gradual recognition extending over centuries, not one editor’s choice — no council created the canon (see how the canon formed). Selection can explain why agreeing books sit together; it does not by itself explain the forward-leaning patterns P2 points to, and the inference from those patterns to a single Author is a pointer, not a proof. Many scholars across confessions recognize a real narrative unity, however they explain it.

"Later authors just copied the earlier ones" Trickier

Strongest form

Of course it looks unified — the New Testament writers had read the Old Testament and shaped their stories to match it. The coherence is borrowed, not given.

Reply

Awareness of earlier texts explains continuity of theme, but not the fit between unplanned details — historical events the later writers did not control matching patterns set down long before. And mere imitation tends to produce flat repetition, not the rich development and fulfillment we actually find. Borrowing language is not the same as a single coherent vision unfolding across centuries.

"Other religious texts claim unity too" Hard

Strongest form

Plenty of scriptures present a consistent message. If unity proves divine authorship, it would prove too much — establishing rival faiths equally.

Reply

Most comparable texts come from a single author or a single short period, where unity is unremarkable. Scripture's distinctive feature is unity achieved despite radical diversity of authorship and time. The argument doesn't claim unity alone proves Christianity true; it claims this particular unity demands an explanation, and we argue design is the better one — a pointer, not a proof. Other evidential lines then bear on which faith is true — above all the historical case for the resurrection.

"You're cherry-picking the parts that agree" Hard

Strongest form

The Bible also contains tension — Ecclesiastes' despair, the imprecatory Psalms, James and Paul on faith and works. Highlighting the harmonies while ignoring the friction is selection bias.

Reply

The argument doesn't require uniformity; it predicts a unified vision rich enough to hold real tension within it — wisdom literature wrestling honestly, multiple angles on one truth. James and Paul use "faith" and "works" in different senses and are complementary, not contradictory.

    SR
    Skeptical reader
    "Matthew traces Jesus’ line through David’s son Solomon and says Joseph’s father was Jacob; Luke traces it through Nathan and says Joseph’s father was Heli. Joseph cannot have two fathers. That is not a difference of emphasis — it is a flat contradiction."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    The two genealogies are best read as a real difference with more than one reasonable explanation rather than a flat contradiction. The oldest solution, going back to the African church father Julius Africanus in the third century, appeals to levirate marriage, by which Joseph could be the legal son of one man and the biological son of another. A common modern proposal holds that Matthew gives Joseph’s line while Luke gives Mary’s. Honesty requires admitting that neither solution is certain, and that the texts do not tell us which (if either) is right — the harmonization is plausible but not provable. What can be said is that the two genealogies are not formal contradictories: a line traced legally and a line traced biologically can both be true of the same man. And a possible reconciliation is enough to defeat the charge of a formal contradiction. So the charge of a flat contradiction does not hold — though which explanation is right, the texts leave open.
