The Church in History

Three questions that begin where the resurrection accounts end: how a tiny illegal sect became the majority faith of an empire, what its members actually suffered along the way, and what the movement did to the moral imagination of the world. The first two ask whether the usual sceptical stories hold up. The third asks a different kind of question — not whether Christianity is true, which is argued across the rest of this Library, but whether it was good for the world — and it is careful to keep the two apart: that a faith did good is never, by itself, a reason to think it true.

Growth, persecution & the legacy of the faith

01
How Did the Church Take Over the Roman Empire?
From a few hundred followers to millions while the faith was illegal — and why “Constantine did it” is false for the period that matters
📚 Intermediate
Free
In plain English: In the year 30 the followers of a crucified Galilean numbered perhaps a few hundred, with no wealth, no standing, and no army. Two and a half centuries later, with the faith still illegal and two imperial attempts to suppress it already spent, the state gave up and legalised them. The popular sceptical explanation is one word — Constantine — and it is false as an account of the period in which the church became too large to suppress. But one caution governs everything: growth is not truth. A movement can spread because it met real needs and still be mistaken; this card explains the spread, not the truth of the claims.
The key points
  1. By about AD 300 Christians were a substantial minority — the published estimates run from roughly 2.5 million (Ehrman) to about 6.3 million (Stark’s model), somewhere between four and ten-and-a-half per cent of the empire. The spread between the figures is the honest measure of what we know.
  2. That growth happened while the faith was illegal, with no political advantage on offer — so it cannot be credited to the state.
  3. The mechanism its own enemies singled out was unglamorous: care of the sick at cost, refusal to expose unwanted infants, mutual aid, and conversion along family and household networks.
  4. “Constantine did it” explains the wrong stretch of the curve: the church was already too large to remove before 312, and two persecutions had failed with the machinery of empire engaged.
  5. None of this shows Christianity is true. It shows the imperial-power story is false, and that the behaviour that drove the growth is attested by hostile witnesses.

The sharpest witness comes from an enemy. In 362, fifty years into a Christian empire, the emperor Julian — raised Christian, and the last emperor who was not one — set out to restore the old gods and found he could not. Writing to a pagan priest, he diagnosed why: not that Christianity had better arguments, but that the Christians out-behaved the pagans. “The impious Galilaeans,” he complained, “support not only their own poor but ours as well.” His remedy was to order his own priesthood to copy them. That is an emperor holding the entire apparatus of the state, judging that the thing he had to beat was their charity.

Questions people ask
QDoesn’t the fact that it grew so fast show it must be true? No. Growth shows a movement met needs and held loyalty; it says nothing about whether its claims are true. Islam expanded politically far faster, and Rodney Stark checked his own early-church growth rate against modern Latter-day Saint growth. Any argument of the form “it spread, therefore it is true” proves far too much.
QDidn’t Constantine just hand Christianity the empire? The objection concedes a great deal — everything after 312 is entangled with state power, and the fourth-century majority is not evidence of persuasion. But it explains the wrong period. By 312 the church was already large enough that two attempts to destroy it, under Decius and Diocletian, had failed; Galerius conceded toleration in April 311, a year before the Milvian Bridge. The state gave up first.

Picture the arithmetic. A few hundred people in one corner of one province, and within three centuries a body so large that an empire which had tried twice to stamp it out simply stopped trying. That is a strange thing to have happened, and both the pious and the sceptical accounts of it say more than the evidence permits.

The honest numbers

Nobody counted. Every figure is an estimate, and the most-quoted — Rodney Stark’s roughly six million by AD 300 — is a model, not a measurement, generated by assuming a steady growth rate. Bart Ehrman’s more recent estimate is less than half that. So the responsible thing is a range, not a point: somewhere between about four and ten-and-a-half per cent of the empire, concentrated in cities and unevenly spread. What is not in dispute is the shape — a standing start, and steep sustained growth, while the faith was against the law.

Why the “Constantine” answer misses

The single most important point is a matter of timing. This card does not claim to explain how Christianity became the empire’s majority — that is a fourth-century story, and the state is all over it. It explains how the church became impossible to suppress before the state had any part in it. And the evidence for that period does not depend on any growth model: a Roman governor describing a spreading movement around AD 112, a satirist describing its organised charity around 165, a bishop’s relief roll from 251 listing more than 1,500 dependants supported by one congregation, and two failed imperial persecutions.

The honest limit

Here is where a Christian should be careful, because the temptation is real. None of this is evidence that Jesus rose from the dead. Movements grow for many reasons, and a religion can be admirable in its effects and false in its claims. Anyone who offers the rise of the church as proof of the faith has changed the subject — and handed a reader a confidence that will not survive the first competent objection. What the record does establish is narrower and worth having: the movement grew, at real cost, doing what its founder told it to do, and the people who tried hardest to defeat it said so.

How to explain this simply

Keep two claims apart: that Christianity spread (a historical fact) and that Christianity is true (a separate question settled by other evidence). This card argues only the first, and only for the pre-Constantinian period — that the church became too large to suppress before the state ever helped it.

In conversation: “Suppose I grant that Constantine made Christianity powerful in the fourth century. That still doesn’t explain why it was already too big to stamp out before him — while it was illegal, and two emperors had tried. What did that?”

The model, and why it is only a model

Rodney Stark’s influential table assumes a constant 40 per cent per decade and reaches about 6.3 million by AD 300. Its author called it a model, not a count, and it cuts both ways: he calibrated the rate against modern Latter-day Saint growth, which makes the figures plausible and also shows an exponential curve of this shape is not unique to Christianity or evidence of anything supernatural. Two cautions: the rate cannot be extended (run to 400 it produces more Christians than the empire held), and Keith Hopkins, often cited as independent confirmation, is not — he adopts Stark’s arithmetic. Ehrman’s independent estimate is roughly half Stark’s. Hence a range.

The mechanisms — and an honest rival

Four things recur: care of the sick during the great epidemics (at real cost, since nursing raised the carers’ own exposure); refusal of infant exposure; conversion along household and trade networks; and an unusually socially mixed membership not rationed by status. None is unique in kind — Roman guilds and Jewish communities had mutual aid, and other cults spread by network too. The most serious dissenting account, Ramsay MacMullen’s, credits public demonstrations of power over charity; it is a rival mechanism, not a rival to the thesis, because whatever else it was, it was not imperial power.

The Constantine objection, conceded and answered

The concession is real: everything after 312 is entangled with state power, and the church’s growth after 380 with the legal coercion of non-Nicene worship. But the answer is about timing. Pliny’s letter, Lucian’s satire, and Cornelius’s relief roll all sit before Constantine; and the state’s own two attempts to bring Christians to heel — Decius’s universal sacrifice order of 250 and Diocletian’s persecution from 303 — had already failed, with Galerius conceding toleration in April 311. Whatever Constantine did, he did it to a church two emperors could not remove.

What the Early Church Actually Suffered
Nero to Diocletian: the popular picture is inflated, the sceptical correction overshoots, and a real, law-backed capital liability remains
📚 Intermediate
Free
In plain English: The popular picture — three centuries of relentless hunting, believers always in the catacombs, an empire straining every nerve to stamp them out — is badly out of proportion, and the honest thing is to say so at the front, because a believer handed the Hollywood version will meet a sceptic who dismantles it. But the fashionable correction overshoots in turn. Between the two is the truth: persecution was intermittent and mostly local, yet the cost of being a Christian was real, recurring, and backed by law — and, crucially, martyrdom establishes the martyrs’ sincerity, never the truth of what they believed.
The key points
  1. Candida Moss’s deflation is largely right and should be granted: the “ten great persecutions” scheme is a later construction, most Christians most of the time were not being hunted, and the martyr stories are largely a devotional genre.
  2. But grant all of it and a solid body of evidence remains — much of it from hostile Roman officials, not Christian storytellers.
  3. Trajan’s rescript (c. AD 112) set the shape: Christians were not to be sought out, but those accused and unrepentant were executed — a standing legal liability any neighbour could turn lethal.
  4. The empire-wide measures from Decius (250) onward were real and enforced: the sacrifice certificates survive on papyrus.
  5. The strongest evidence is the church’s own embarrassment — fifty years of bitter disputes over how to treat the Christians who complied, which proves both that the pressure was real and that it was survivable.

The best evidence is not a martyr story but an administrative headache. After the persecutions, the churches tore themselves apart for decades over the lapsi — the lapsed — those who had sacrificed, or bribed their way to a certificate, or fled. That controversy proves two things at once, in opposite directions. A church does not build a fifty-year discipline for apostates unless there was real pressure and real apostates — so the persecution was genuine. And the lapsed were a mass phenomenon, not a scattering of cowards — so it was survivable, and many did not endure it. The early church was not made of a stronger grade of person; it was made of people who mostly wanted to live, and then had to work out how to forgive the ones who had.

Questions people ask
QWasn’t the persecution mostly a myth? It is exaggerated in popular retellings, and this card concedes most of Moss’s case. But what she grants still refutes the flatter claim: Trajan’s rescript authorising execution for the name, the Decian sacrifice order whose certificates survive, and Diocletian’s Great Persecution. She disputes Nero and disputes Rome’s intent; grant both and a recurring, state-backed capital liability remains.
QDoesn’t the martyrs’ willingness to die prove Christianity is true? No — and the card says so at the top and the bottom. Martyrdom establishes sincerity, not truth; people die for false beliefs constantly. What dying rules out is deliberate fraud, and only for those in a position to know whether they were lying — a narrower argument that concerns the apostles specifically, made in the companion essay on the disciples’ belief.

Almost every element of the Hollywood picture — lions in the Colosseum, an empire hunting Christians without pause — is wrong or badly out of proportion. Saying so is not a concession dragged out under cross-examination; it is the first duty of an honest account, because the inflated version is the one a sceptic can take apart, and a believer who has leaned on it is left wondering what else will not survive contact.

The deflation, granted — and where it overshoots

The historian Candida Moss has pressed the strongest modern case for deflation, and most of it should simply be granted: the tidy scheme of “ten persecutions” is a later invention, the periods of active hunting were a small fraction of the era, and the martyr acts are largely a devotional genre rather than court reporting. But the fashionable conclusion — that the whole thing is essentially a Christian myth — overshoots what even Moss grants.

What the record still shows

Grant the deflation and a solid, largely hostile body of evidence remains. Tacitus on Nero (a contested case, and set aside here). Pliny and Trajan on the legal machinery around 112. The Decian sacrifice certificates, still readable on Egyptian papyrus. And the church’s own records of mass compliance under Decius and Diocletian — the surest proof that the pressure was real, precisely because it is the church admitting its own failure rather than advertising its heroism.

The honest limit

Two limits keep the argument sound. Martyrdom proves sincerity, not truth — it rules out deliberate fraud, nothing more. And the record establishes cost, not distinctiveness: Rome suppressed other groups too, so “they were persecuted” does not by itself set Christianity apart. Why this movement grew under that cost when others did not is a separate question, taken up in the companion card on the church’s growth.

How to explain this simply

Concede the popular exaggeration up front, then hold the line where the evidence is firm: a Roman rescript that made being a Christian a capital charge on accusation, sacrifice certificates that survive on papyrus, and the church’s own fifty-year fight over the lapsed. The martyrs’ deaths prove they were sincere, not that they were right.

In conversation: “You’re right that the ‘lions everywhere’ picture is overblown. But we have the actual sacrifice certificates people had to buy, and fifty years of church records agonising over those who caved. You don’t build that unless the danger was real.”

Moss’s case, at full strength

The most forceful modern challenge is Candida Moss’s. She argues the “ten persecutions” scheme is a later construction; that imperial initiatives killed Christians for fewer than ten years in total across the whole period; that the martyr acts are largely late and edited; that what happened was mostly prosecution under general law rather than persecution of a belief; and that the myth has since been misused. The first points are largely uncontested among specialists; the prosecution-vs-persecution thesis is answered below; the misuse claim is about modern churches and is not adjudicated here.

Nero — the contested exhibit

Tacitus reports Nero blaming the Christians for the fire of 64. But whether this was a persecution of a distinct group is a live scholarly dispute — Brent Shaw has argued the evidence will not bear it, and Moss herself disputes it. Nothing in this card depends on Nero; it is set out as the contested item it is.

The prosecution-vs-persecution answer

Grant that Roman magistrates thought of themselves as enforcing public order, not suppressing a religion. A defendant executed for refusing to sacrifice — where sacrificing would have ended the matter on the spot — still faces a religiously specific test; and Trajan’s rescript makes recantation the whole hinge of the procedure, which ordinary criminal law would not. The liability was real whatever the magistrate believed he was doing.

The objection this card cannot answer

Rome also suppressed the Bacchanalia, banned the druidic religion, expelled Jews from the city, and issued a rescript against the Manichaeans. Christians were not uniquely targeted, so this record establishes cost, not distinctiveness — an honest limit, conceded rather than hidden.

Did Christianity Change the World?
The case that universal human dignity is a traceable inheritance — with the full ledger of the church’s crimes, and why none of it proves the faith true
🎓 Advanced
Free
In plain English: You probably believe every human being is of equal worth and that the weak have a claim on the strong simply by being human. You probably think that is obvious. For most of history almost no society was built on it. This card makes a genealogical claim — that this conviction has a traceable history in which Judaism is the root and Christianity a major part of the route into the Western world — and it is scrupulous about one thing above all: none of this is evidence that Christianity is true. A belief system can be enormously beneficial and entirely false. Christianity’s claim on anyone is that it is true, not that it is useful.
The key points
  1. The conviction that every person has equal, inviolable worth is not self-evident and was not the default of the ancient world — it has a history.
  2. That history runs through a Jewish doctrine (the image of God) carried by Christianity into the Gentile world and welded to an unlikely centre: a God executed as a criminal.
  3. The claim is bounded: Christianity was a necessary ingredient in a particular combination, not a sufficient cause — which is why the decisive changes came late and unevenly.
  4. The ledger against is real and not incidental — Crusades, inquisitions, the slave-owners’ Bible, colonial mission, anti-Judaism, apartheid, the abuse crisis — and the genealogy must never be used to soften any of it.
  5. And the whole thing proves nothing about the faith’s truth. If Christianity were false, its social benefits would be a reason to be grateful for a mistake, not a reason to believe it.

The abolition of slavery shows both halves of the honest case at once. The organised British campaign from 1787 drew heavily on Christian conviction — Quakers, Clarkson, the Clapham circle around Wilberforce. But when the isolated proof-texts were read on their own, the pro-slavery side had the easier of them, and its most sophisticated defences were also Christian; the American conflict became, in Mark Noll’s account, a crisis of biblical interpretation itself. What the pro-slavery reading did not have was the whole: the abolitionists read from creation in God’s image, from the exodus, from the condemnation of the man-stealer, and argued the canon read entire runs against an institution its parts merely regulate. Abolition was a Christian achievement that had to be won against Christians.

Questions people ask
QDoesn’t all the good Christianity did prove it’s true? No, and this card refuses that inference explicitly. Social benefit is not a truth-claim about God; if the faith were false its benefits would be a reason to be grateful for a mistake, not to believe it. The case for its truth rests on the historical and philosophical arguments made elsewhere in this Library.
QWhat about the Crusades, the inquisitions, slavery, and the abuse crisis? They are set out in full here, not managed — and with the harder point added: the sophisticated defences of slavery were themselves Christian. The genealogical argument is never used as mitigation. That the church supplied a moral standard does not shorten what that standard measures; being handed it aggravates the failure to keep it.
QIsn’t it really Enlightenment thinking, or the Stoics, that gave us human rights? Both are engaged. The churches were often on the wrong side of the Enlightenment’s fights — granted — but its moral premises were not self-generated. The Stoics reasoned about a common humanity, but theirs was a universalism of reason that grades, and it produced no movement to end slavery. Samuel Moyn presses the sharpest counter — that the long genealogies are retrojection — and that is left standing, not pretend-refuted.

Start with something you almost certainly believe: that a person’s value does not depend on their usefulness, their birth, or their strength. It feels like plain moral sense. It is not. For most of recorded history almost no one built a society on it, and the civilisations that gave us Western philosophy did not.

The claim — and what it is not

The defensible claim is genealogical: this conviction has a traceable pedigree, in which Judaism is the root and Christianity a major part of the route into Western law and sentiment. It is not the claim that Christianity made the world nicer, and above all it is not evidence that Christianity is true. That firewall has to stand, because the moment “look what it did for the world” becomes “therefore it is true,” the argument has committed a plain error a thoughtful sceptic will name at once.

The ledger has to be honest

An account that stopped at the good would be advertising, not history. The Crusades, the inquisitions, the witch trials, colonial forced conversion, centuries of anti-Judaism, the theological defence of apartheid, the abuse crisis — each is a settled matter of record, cutting across every tradition. And the genealogy must never soften them: that the church supplied the ruler does not shorten what the ruler measures, and being handed a standard aggravates the failure to keep it rather than excusing it.

Where it lands

So the verdict runs in both directions. It shows a widespread modern assumption — that basic moral equality is just what a decent person naturally thinks — is false; the conviction was won late, partially, and against the grain. It does not show that Christians have been good, and it does not show the faith is true. The honest thing a Christian can say is not that the church has been better than others, but that it was handed something better than itself.

How to explain this simply

Separate two questions: is Christianity true (argued elsewhere) and was it good for the world (argued here). The second never establishes the first. The good-for-the-world case is itself bounded — a claim about where a moral standard came from, made honestly alongside the full record of the church’s failures to keep it.

In conversation: “I’m not going to argue Christianity is true because it did good — that would be a bad argument. I’m saying the equality you take for granted isn’t obvious or ancient; it has a history, and a lot of that history is Jewish and Christian. Where do you think it came from?”

The thesis, and its careful form

Tom Holland’s Dominion is the most-discussed recent statement: the West received its instinct for universal dignity from a particular history rather than deducing it from reason, and the most unlikely thing in that history — a God executed as a criminal — is at its root. Holland is a popular historian, not a specialist in every period he crosses, and the strong causal claim is genuinely debated among historians even where the underlying facts are not.

The strongest objection: it took eighteen centuries

If universal dignity was in the deposit from the first century, why did abolition and toleration wait until the eighteenth, and arrive only in north-western Europe, when Byzantine, Russian and other Christendoms produced none of it? The answer is the bounded one the whole card rests on: Christianity was a necessary ingredient in a particular combination, not a sufficient cause. A standard held and disobeyed for centuries is evidence about human beings, not about where the standard came from.

The counter-genealogies

The Stoics and Roman jurists reasoned about a common humanity — but theirs was a universalism of reason that grades, and it built no institution and freed no one. The Enlightenment credited itself with rights won against the church, and the churches were often on the wrong side — but its premises were already in circulation. Samuel Moyn argues that modern human rights are a twentieth-century phenomenon and the long genealogies are retrojection; that is a serious position, and it is left standing rather than pretend-refuted.

The bound, restated

None of this is offered as evidence that Christianity is true, and none of it shows Christians have been good. What it shows is that a moral assumption modern people treat as self-evident is a specific inheritance with a traceable history — contested in its strong forms, defensible in its careful one.
