How Did the Church Take Over the Empire?
From a few hundred followers to millions while the faith was illegal — and why “Constantine did it” misses the period that matters
📚 Intermediate
Free
In plain English: In the year 30 the followers of a crucified Galilean numbered, at most, a few hundred people — no wealth, no standing, no army, in one corner of one province. Three centuries later the emperor himself was a Christian, and the faith was on its way to becoming the majority religion of the Mediterranean world. And it happened while the faith was illegal, with no political advantage on offer and two imperial attempts to destroy it already spent. Even outside observers noticed — and the church’s own relief rolls confirm — the spread: a Roman governor alarmed by it around AD 112, a pagan satirist mocking it around 165, a bishop’s relief roll of 251 feeding more than 1,500 people from one congregation. That is the remarkable fact to sit with first — and it is why the popular one-word answer, Constantine, misses the stretch that actually matters: the church was already too large to suppress before he arrived.
The key points
  1. By about AD 300 Christians were a substantial minority — the published estimates run from roughly 2.5 million (Ehrman) to about 6.3 million (Stark’s model), somewhere between four and ten-and-a-half per cent of the empire. The spread between the figures is the honest measure of what we know.
  2. That growth happened while the faith was illegal, with no political advantage on offer — so it cannot be credited to the state.
  3. The mechanism its own enemies singled out was unglamorous: care of the sick at real cost, refusal to expose unwanted infants, socially mixed communities, and conversion along family and household networks rather than public spectacle.
  4. “Constantine did it” explains the wrong stretch of the curve: the church was already too large to remove before 312, and two persecutions had failed with the full machinery of empire engaged.
  5. None of this by itself shows Christianity is true — that case rests on other evidence, above all the resurrection, argued elsewhere in this Library. What the record does establish is narrower and worth having: the imperial-power story is false, and the behaviour that drove the growth is attested even by hostile witnesses.

One of the sharpest testimonies comes from an enemy holding the whole apparatus of the state. In 362, fifty years into a Christian empire, the emperor Julian — raised Christian, and the last emperor to abandon the faith — set out to restore the old gods and found he could not. He diagnosed why: not that Christianity had better arguments, but that the Christians simply out-behaved the pagans, caring even for strangers and for the poor whom the pagan priesthood neglected. His remedy was telling — he ordered his own pagan priesthood to fund and imitate the very charity he could not defeat. Julian is far too late to be a witness to what caused the earlier growth, and it must not be pretended otherwise; but he is a witness to something worth having: an emperor with an empire at his disposal, judging that the thing he had to beat was Christian charity. (The vivid line often quoted — that the “impious Galilaeans support not only their own poor but ours as well” — is from a letter whose authenticity is disputed; Julian makes the same complaint in the undisputed Fragment of a Letter to a Priest.)

Questions people ask
QDoesn’t the fact that it grew so fast prove Christianity is true? No, and this argument does not claim it. Rapid growth shows a movement was profoundly attractive and its people deeply convinced; popularity is not a truth-detector, and other movements have spread fast and been mistaken. The case that Jesus actually rose rests on different evidence — above all the early eyewitness creed in 1 Corinthians 15:3–7, argued elsewhere in this Library. The growth is what you would expect if that case is sound and the first witnesses were sincere — not a substitute for it.
QDidn’t Constantine just hand Christianity the empire? The objection concedes a great deal — everything after 312 is entangled with state power, and the fourth-century majority is not evidence of persuasion. But it explains the wrong period. By 312 the church was already large enough that two attempts to destroy it, under Decius and Diocletian, had failed; Galerius conceded toleration in April 311, a year before the Milvian Bridge. The state gave up first.

Picture the arithmetic. A few hundred people in one corner of one province, and within three centuries a body so large that an empire which had tried twice to stamp it out simply stopped trying. That is a strange thing to have happened, and both the pious and the sceptical accounts of it tend to say more than the evidence permits.

The honest numbers

Nobody counted. Every figure is an estimate, and the most-quoted — Rodney Stark’s roughly six million by AD 300 — is a model, not a measurement, generated by assuming a steady compound rate. Bart Ehrman’s more recent estimate is less than half that. So the responsible thing is a range, not a point: somewhere between about four and ten-and-a-half per cent of the empire, concentrated in cities and unevenly spread. What is not in dispute is the shape — a standing start, and steep sustained growth, while the faith was against the law.

Why the “Constantine” answer misses

The single most important point is a matter of timing. This argument does not claim to explain how Christianity became the empire’s majority — that is a fourth-century story, and the state is all over it. It explains how the church became impossible to suppress before the state had any part in it. And the evidence for that period does not depend on any growth model: a Roman governor describing a spreading movement around AD 112, a satirist describing its organised charity around 165, a bishop’s relief roll from 251 listing more than 1,500 dependants supported by one congregation, and two failed imperial persecutions.

The honest limit

Here is where a Christian should be careful, because the temptation is real. None of this is evidence that Jesus rose from the dead. Movements grow for many reasons, and a religion can be admirable in its effects and false in its claims. Anyone who offers the rise of the church as proof of the faith has changed the subject — the case that Christianity is true rests on different evidence, above all the resurrection. What the record does establish is narrower and worth having: the movement grew, at real cost, doing what its founder told it to do, and the people who tried hardest to defeat it said so.

How to explain this simply

Keep two claims apart: that Christianity spread (a historical fact) and that Christianity is true (a separate question settled by other evidence). This argument makes only the first, and only for the pre-Constantinian period — that the church became too large to suppress before the state ever helped it.

In conversation: “Suppose I grant that Constantine made Christianity powerful in the fourth century. That still doesn’t explain why it was already too big to stamp out before him — while it was illegal, and two emperors had tried. What did that?”

How many Christians were there? An honest answer

Nobody counted. There is no census of Christians in the Roman Empire, and every figure in every book on the subject is an estimate built from fragments. The most influential attempt is Rodney Stark’s, in The Rise of Christianity (1996): assume a steady compound rate, anchor it to a starting figure and an endpoint historians roughly accept, and see what the exponential curve produces. Begin with about 1,000 Christians in the year 40 and let the movement grow at 40 per cent per decade, and you reach roughly 7,500 by 100, about 218,000 by 200, and about 6.3 million by 300 — something over a tenth of an empire of perhaps sixty million.

The force of the model is what it rules out. Forty per cent a decade is not a stampede; it is what you get if, over a decade, two or three believers together bring in one new person — no mass conversions, no coercion, neither of which existed in this period anyway. And the honest thing to say about the table is that it is a model, not a census, and its author said so. Stark fixes the rate from his two endpoints, then observes that it is close to the documented twentieth-century growth of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints — a plausibility check on a rate already chosen, not an input to it. That comparison cuts both ways: it makes the figures credible, and it concedes that an exponential curve of this shape is not unique to Christianity or evidence of anything supernatural.

Two cautions, and why the answer is a range

First, the rate cannot be extended: run it on to the year 400 and it produces more Christians than the empire had inhabitants — a fair measure of what a constant-rate model is and is not. Second, the agreement among historians is thinner than it looks. Keith Hopkins, often cited as independent confirmation, is not: he adopts Stark’s arithmetic as his starting point and states his debt in the article. Bart Ehrman, working the same problem, puts the year-300 figure at roughly two and a half to three million — less than half Stark’s. So the responsible summary is a range: somewhere between about four and ten-and-a-half per cent of the empire. What is not in dispute is the shape — a standing start, and steep sustained growth, while the faith was illegal.

What growth looked like from outside

The most valuable evidence is not Christian, because Christian sources have an obvious interest in reporting success. Around AD 112 Pliny the Younger, governing Bithynia-Pontus, wrote to Trajan that the “contagion of this superstition” had spread not only through the cities but through the villages and countryside, affecting every age and rank and both sexes, with the temples nearly deserted before his crackdown — and he reports many recanting under pressure, which is not what a man exaggerating his triumph would invent. Half a century later the satirist Lucian — who thought Christians credulous fools — described them mobbing a jailed leader, bribing the guards, and sending delegates from the cities of Asia at collective expense. And from inside the illegal period comes a hard administrative number: writing about 251, Cornelius of Rome listed his church’s establishment for a colleague — forty-six presbyters, seven deacons, and more than fifteen hundred widows and persons in distress supported by the one congregation.

The mechanisms — and an honest rival

Three things recur across the sources. Care of the sick during the great epidemics, at real cost, since nursing raised the carers’ own exposure — Dionysius of Alexandria says they died of it while frightened neighbours abandoned their own. Refusal of infant exposure — the Didache forbids the killing of a newborn in the same breath as murder, and the Epistle to Diognetus singles out Christians as people who “do not destroy their offspring.” And conversion along family, household and trade networks rather than public preaching to strangers — the book of Acts shows exactly this, converting whole households at a stroke. None is unique in kind, and the most serious dissenting reconstruction, Ramsay MacMullen’s, credits public demonstrations of power — healing and exorcism — over charity; but it is a rival mechanism, not a rival to the thesis, because whatever exorcism was, it was not imperial power.

The Constantine objection, conceded and answered

The concession is substantial: everything after 312 is entangled with state power, and the church’s growth after 380 with the legal suppression of rival worship. The steepest part of the curve in raw numbers is the fourth century, and anyone who cites the fourth-century majority as evidence of persuasive power is trading on a period when the state’s thumb was on the scale. But the answer is about timing, and it does not rest on the growth model. Pliny’s letter, Lucian’s satire and Cornelius’s relief roll all sit before Constantine; and the state’s own attempts to bring Christians to heel — Decius’s universal sacrifice order of 250, Valerian’s edicts of 257 and 258, and Diocletian’s persecution from 303 — had already failed, with Galerius conceding toleration in April 311, a year before the Milvian Bridge. The state conceded first. Whatever Constantine did, he did it to a church two emperors could not remove.

A hostile witness — and the honest bound

One of the sharpest testimonies comes from an enemy holding the whole apparatus of the state. In 362 the emperor Julian, raised Christian and the last emperor to abandon the faith, set out to restore the old gods and could not. He is not evidence about what caused the earlier growth — he is far too late for that, and it must not be pretended otherwise; he is evidence of something else worth having: that an emperor with the whole machinery of state judged Christian charity the thing he had to beat. He blamed pagan decline on the priesthood’s neglect of the poor and on Christian care for strangers, and funded a pagan imitation of it (the undisputed Fragment of a Letter to a Priest; the vivid line that the “impious Galilaeans support not only their own poor but ours as well” comes from a letter whose authenticity is disputed). Yet none of this is evidence that Jesus rose. Movements grow for many reasons, and a religion can be admirable in its effects and false in its claims; the case that Jesus actually rose rests on other evidence, above all the early creed of 1 Corinthians 15. What the record establishes is narrower and worth having: the imperial-power story is false, and the behaviour that drove the growth is attested by the very people who tried hardest to defeat it.

What the Early Church Suffered — and Why It Held
A hunted, illegal faith held under Rome — the courage of the martyrs, and the hostile witnesses who could not explain it
📚 Intermediate
Free
In plain English: The early Christians were not victims of a manhunt they could not escape. They were people who were handed an exit and refused to take it. Around AD 112 the Roman governor Pliny interrogated Christians three times on pain of death and released everyone who would offer a pinch of incense to the emperor and curse Christ. The ones who died, died because they would not. And a steady stream would not — an aging bishop, a nursing mother, a slave girl, tried in dated records that survive to this day. The strongest evidence comes from their enemies: hostile pagan writers who watched Christians face death without a sword in hand, found it genuinely baffling, and said so in writing. A faith its enemies could not kill out of existence, and its own people would not buy their way out of, is a historical fact that wants explaining.
The key points
  1. The crime was the name. Under Trajan’s policy a Christian was not charged with arson or sedition — only with being a Christian and admitting it aloud. A small public gesture won release; refusing it cost your life.
  2. We are not depending on legend. Roughly forty-six certificates of sacrifice from the Decian order of 250 survive on papyrus, and the earliest martyr accounts — Polycarp, the Scillitan martyrs, Lyons, Perpetua — are sober, dated, and singled out even by the most sceptical specialists.
  3. The best evidence is hostile. Pliny, Tacitus, Lucian and Galen — none of them friends of the church — record the executions and the Christians’ startling readiness to face them. They concede the courage; they cannot explain it.
  4. Rome kept assuming its cruelty would find the weakest link — a slave, a girl, a mother. Again and again the weakest link held.
  5. This does not by itself prove Christianity true; dying for a belief proves sincerity, not truth. What it rules out is cynical pretence — and why these people were so sure belongs with the evidence for the resurrection, argued elsewhere in this Library.

The oldest named witness is Polycarp of Smyrna, an old bishop who as a young man had known people who had known the apostles. Around 155 he was arrested and pressed by the proconsul to swear by the emperor’s fortune and curse Christ. His reply is one of the oldest recorded sentences of Christian courage: “Eighty and six years have I served Him, and He never did me any injury: how then can I blaspheme my King and my Saviour?” He was burned in the stadium, and the letter his church wrote to describe it is the earliest surviving account of a Christian martyrdom.

Questions people ask
QDoesn’t the fact that people died for it prove Christianity is true? Not by itself. People die for beliefs that turn out to be false, so martyrdom proves sincerity, not truth. What it rules out is cynical pretence — you do not face the beasts to keep up an act. So the martyrs show, at minimum, that these Christians genuinely believed Jesus was risen and Lord, and were no cynics. Whether the very first witnesses could have died for a story they knew to be false — a still sharper question — belongs with the evidence for the resurrection, argued elsewhere in this Library.
QWeren’t Christians really persecuted for 300 years straight — isn’t that a myth? Honest history trims the picture, and we should say so. For most of the first three centuries, most Christians in most places were not being actively hunted; persecution was usually episodic and local, and only Decius (250), Valerian (257–258), and Diocletian (from 303) mounted anything empire-wide. The popular scheme of “ten great persecutions” is a later simplification. But the correction cuts the frequency, not the fact: to be a known Christian was legally to risk death for the whole period, and men, women and slaves paid that price rather than deny Christ. A danger that is intermittent is still a danger.

Sometime around the year 112, the Roman governor of Bithynia sat in judgment over people accused of a strange new religion, and he had a problem: he did not know what the crime was. So Pliny the Younger did what a careful administrator does — he asked them, on pain of death, whether they were Christians, and asked again, and a third time. Those who kept saying yes he sent to execution, not because he had proved them guilty of anything, but because their “stubbornness and inflexible obstinacy” surely deserved punishment. His letter to Trajan is the earliest surviving Roman government document on how to handle Christians — and it matters for one reason above all: the people in front of him could have walked free at any moment.

The name itself was the crime

Trajan’s reply became imperial policy for over a century, and its logic is chilling in its simplicity. Christians were not to be hunted down; anonymous accusations were to be ignored. But anyone properly accused who would not recant was to be punished. There is no charge of arson or sedition — the offence is the name, being a Christian and admitting it aloud. To be delivered from danger a believer did not have to renounce Christ in his heart or betray anyone. He had only to perform a small public gesture and refuse to say three words, “Christ is Lord,” in reverse. The steady stream of people who would not perform that gesture is the whole phenomenon.

The certificates in the sand

We are not depending on pious legend. In 250 the emperor Decius ordered every inhabitant of the empire to sacrifice to the gods and obtain a signed, witnessed certificate — a libellus — proving it. Roughly forty-six of these have been recovered from the dry sands of Egypt and published. They are mundane, bureaucratic, and utterly damning: so-and-so has “always sacrificed to the gods,” and “now, in your presence, in accordance with the edict, I have sacrificed and poured a libation and tasted the offerings.” The edict was universal — it fell on everyone — but on Christians alone as an impossible demand. Behind every surviving certificate stands the unwritten record of those who would not sign, and faced the magistrate instead.

The honest limit

Here is where a Christian should be careful. Dying for a belief proves sincerity, not truth. People have laid down their lives for causes that were mistaken, and courage is not a truth-detector. What martyrdom does rule out is cynical pretence — you do not go to the beasts to keep up an act. These deaths prove that these Christians sincerely believed what they had been taught, that Jesus is Lord and risen, and held it with their eyes open. The case that Christianity is true does not rest here; it rests, above all, on the resurrection, argued at length elsewhere in this Library. This is a window onto what that conviction cost the people who held it first. Watch what they were willing to pay, and then go and weigh why they were so sure.

How to explain this simply

Keep two things apart: that the early Christians suffered and held (a historical fact, attested by their own enemies) and that Christianity is true (a separate question settled by the resurrection evidence). This card argues the first — that a hunted, illegal faith could not be killed out of existence, and that the courage was not a myth.

In conversation: “Pliny offered every one of them an exit — a pinch of incense and they walked free. The ones who died chose to. And it’s the Romans who despised them — Pliny, Tacitus, Galen — who tell us they faced death without flinching. What do you make of people like that?”

The named witnesses

We can name them — not all, for most died anonymous, but enough, in accounts sober enough that even the historians most sceptical of martyr literature single them out. The Scillitan martyrs — seven men and five women from a town in North Africa — were tried at Carthage on 17 July 180; the court record survives and is the earliest dated document of the Latin-speaking church. Offered thirty days to reconsider, their spokesman Speratus said that in a matter so plain there was nothing to think over; asked what was in his satchel, he said: the letters of Paul. In 203 at Carthage, a young well-born nursing mother named Perpetua was imprisoned with her pregnant slave Felicity; part of the surviving account is Perpetua’s own prison diary, the earliest known writing by a Christian woman, and it records her father begging her again and again to think of her baby and simply deny the name. She would not. Master and slave went into the arena together.

A generation earlier, in 177, a savage outbreak at Lyons produced a letter so vivid that Eusebius copied it out whole. Its hero is not a bishop or a scholar but Blandina, a slave girl her fellow Christians feared would be too frail to hold out. She outlasted them all — tortured until her executioners gave up exhausted, then hung on a stake, then thrown to a bull — and the letter says the others watching “saw in the form of their sister him who was crucified for them.” Rome kept assuming its cruelty would find the weakest link. Again and again the weakest link held.

What Rome could not understand

The strongest part of the evidence comes not from Christians but from their cultured despisers. The satirist Lucian of Samosata thought Christians gullible fools, yet even mocking them recorded that “the poor wretches have convinced themselves… that they are going to be immortal… in consequence of which they despise death and even willingly give themselves into custody.” The physician Galen — the most famous doctor of the ancient world — noted the same thing more admiringly, remarking that the followers of Christ show a “contempt of death… patent to us every day” and a self-discipline “not inferior to that of genuine philosophers.” Even the earliest and most brutal episode, Nero’s scapegoating after the fire of 64, is known mainly through Tacitus, who despised the sect yet recorded that “an immense multitude” was condemned and killed with a cruelty that stirred even Roman pity. Notice what the hostile sources concede: they do not deny the courage; they cannot explain it. To a Roman, a willingness to die quietly for a conviction was not heroism but a kind of madness — and that incomprehension is worth more than a hundred friendly testimonials.

The honest objection, weighed

Popular Christian memory has often overstated all this, and a serious scholar has called the bluff. The historian Candida Moss, in The Myth of Persecution, argues that the cherished picture of a church under relentless three-century assault — the tidy scheme of “ten great persecutions,” an unbroken “Age of Martyrs” — is a later construction. Specialists broadly agree, and it is worth conceding plainly: for most of these centuries most Christians in most places were not being hunted; persecution was usually episodic and often local, and some later martyr legends are embroidered. But look at what the concession does and does not touch. It trims the frequency and the legend; it leaves the core exactly where it was. Moss herself singles out a small set of accounts — Polycarp, the Scillitan martyrs, Lyons, Perpetua and Felicity — as the earliest and closest to authentic. She disputes how much narrative detail is later shaping, and there she has a real point; but even she does not deny that these people really died rather than renounce Christ. A danger that is intermittent is still a danger — that the sword fell occasionally rather than daily did not make it less sharp for Polycarp. As the historian Wolfram Kinzig has urged in reply, the sources will not let us shrink the persecutions to nothing.

What this does, and does not, prove

It was in that atmosphere, around 197, that the North African lawyer Tertullian flung his famous line at Rome. It is almost always misquoted; what he actually wrote is sharper: “The oftener we are mown down by you, the more in number we grow; the blood of Christians is seed.” It was a partisan’s taunt, not a demographic law — persecution did not mechanically multiply believers. But as a report of something Tertullian could see with his own eyes, a movement executions were failing to extinguish, it was simply true. Two honest limits remain. Dying for a belief proves sincerity, not truth; and the case that Christianity is true does not rest here but on the resurrection of Jesus, made at length elsewhere in this Library. The early church did not think of its courage as its own achievement. Writing from his own imprisonment, Paul told the Philippians it had been “granted” to them, as a gift, not only to believe in Christ but also to suffer for his sake — ordinary people, slaves and mothers and aging bishops and teenagers, given the grace to stand when standing cost everything, and who found, as Blandina’s friends found watching her, that they were not standing alone.

Where Did Human Equality Come From?
Equal worth is a value, not a biological fact — and it entered the West through the image of God, first taught in Jewish Scripture
📚 Intermediate
Free
In plain English: You believe — almost without having to be told — that every human being is of equal worth: the newborn and the dying, the genius and the person with a profound disability, the celebrated and the destitute. That conviction is not obvious and not natural. As a measurement it is plainly false, because humans are radically unequal in every capacity you can name — strength, health, intellect, usefulness. “Equal worth” is a value laid over that inequality, and for most of recorded history almost no one held it; Aristotle taught that some people are “marked out” from birth for rule and others for subjection. The conviction entered the West through a claim made first in Jewish Scripture: God made humanity — male and female alike — in his own image, so that worth is conferred, not earned, and identical in each. This card traces where that conviction came from.
The key points
  1. Equal worth is a value, not a biological fact. Human beings are unequal in every capacity you can weigh, count or test; “equal worth” is a claim about what a person is, laid over a mass of evidence that they are not equal in what they can do.
  2. The ancient Mediterranean assumed the opposite. Its hierarchy was an account of being: Aristotle held that “from the hour of their birth, some are marked out for subjection, others for rule.” Ancient justice ranked persons; modern justice levels them — nearly opposite ideas wearing the same word.
  3. The conviction of equal, unearned worth entered the West through a claim whose root is Jewish, not first of all Christian: the opening page of Genesis says God made humanity, male and female alike, in his own image. The church did not invent it; it carried it into the Gentile world at scale.
  4. Christians welded it to the cross — they worshipped as God the Son, in his true human nature, executed by Rome’s instrument for un-personing slaves. A civilisation that came to worship the crucified Son as God could no longer coherently hold that the humiliated are contemptible.
  5. None of this shows the faith is true. It is a genealogy of a conviction, not a proof of a creed; the case for the faith’s truth rests on the resurrection and the arguments made elsewhere in this library.

What did the old hierarchy cost an actual person? In AD 61, the Roman historian Tacitus records, a senator named Pedanius Secundus, prefect of the city of Rome, was murdered by one of his own slaves. Ancient custom prescribed the remedy: when a master was killed under his own roof, the entire household of slaves — here around four hundred men, women and children, nearly all of them innocent — was to be executed together. A crowd gathered to stop it and the Senate debated, but the jurist Gaius Cassius carried the day, arguing that so mixed a mass of slaves could be held down “only by terror,” and that — in the sentence that lays the whole logic bare — “in every great precedent there is some injustice, which is compensated, as against individuals, by the public advantage.” The four hundred went to their deaths. That is the precise inversion of the modern intuition: the individual is real but negligible, and an injustice done to particular persons is simply the price of the system.

Questions people ask
QIsn’t human equality just obvious — something any decent person can see? It feels obvious, and that feeling is itself the thing to explain. As a measurement, equal worth is plainly false: humans differ in strength, health, intellect and usefulness in every way you can test. It is a value laid over that inequality, not a fact read off from it — which is exactly why the atheist historian Yuval Noah Harari can say, honestly, that “there are no such things as rights in biology.” For most of history most societies did not believe it, and the ancient world often believed the reverse. A conviction that had to be won against the grain of the world is not the default setting of the human mind.
QDoesn’t this argument prove Christianity is true? No, and it isn’t offered as proof. This is a genealogy of a conviction, not a demonstration of a creed. A belief can have deep and honourable roots and still be false; that equal human worth has Christian and Jewish origins tells you where the standard came from, not whether God exists. The case for the faith’s truth rests on other grounds — the resurrection and the arguments made elsewhere in this library. And the church has often betrayed this very doctrine, which is precisely why its own standard could be turned back against it by the reformers who cited Genesis against Christian slaveholders.

Strip away rank, usefulness, intelligence and strength, and you assume a bare human worth remains, the same in each — so much so that we call the denial of it “dehumanising,” treating it not as a mistake of fact but as a kind of blasphemy. Here is the surprise: that conviction is not obvious and not natural. It is a value, not a measurement, because as a measurement it is plainly false. So where did it come from?

The world that ran the other way

The ancient Mediterranean was hierarchical in its very account of being. Some human beings were held to be higher and others lower not by accident but by nature, from birth. Aristotle states it without embarrassment: “from the hour of their birth, some are marked out for subjection, others for rule.” On this view justice did not mean levelling persons but keeping each in the tier nature had assigned him. Individual voices dissented — Seneca could call a slave a fellow human being; the jurist Ulpian recorded that by natural law all are born equal — but none of it became a movement, a law, or a settled conviction. Seneca kept his slaves; Ulpian’s maxim sat inside a code that enforced slavery.

The claim that broke it

Into that world came a conviction with a different shape, and its root is not first of all Christian but Jewish: the opening page of Genesis says God made humanity — male and female alike — in his own image. Worth is not earned or measured but conferred, bestowed on every human being by the Maker, and therefore identical in each. That doctrine developed in Jewish scripture and interpretation and stands on its own feet; the church did not invent it. What the church did was carry it into the Gentile world at scale, and weld it to a claim about the cross — that the death Rome reserved for slaves and rebels, engineered to strip its victim of all dignity, had become the object of worship.

The honest limit

It is worth being exact about the size of the claim. This is a genealogy of a conviction, not a proof of a creed. That the equal-worth intuition has deep Christian and Jewish roots does not, by itself, show that Christianity is true; a belief can have a noble pedigree and still be false, and the case for the faith’s truth rests elsewhere — on the resurrection and the historical and philosophical arguments made throughout this library, not on its social fruit. What the record does establish is narrower and worth having: the load-bearing premise that every person carries an equal, unearned worth entered the Western imagination through the image of God, and had no other stable home in the ancient world.

What the old stories took for granted

To feel how strange the equal-worth intuition is, look at what the ancient world’s origin stories assumed a human being was for. In the Babylonian creation epic Enuma Elish, humanity is manufactured from the blood of a slain god and charged with the service of the gods, so that the gods may be at ease. The cross — the claim that struck the ancient ear as insane

Crucifixion was Rome’s instrument for un-personing a human being: the death reserved for slaves and rebels, engineered to strip its victim of dignity in public. And Christians said that God the Son, in his true human nature, had been executed by that very method. Paul knew how it sounded — he called the message of a crucified Messiah “a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles.” But follow the logic: the empire’s supreme act of contempt, its way of declaring a human being worthless, had become the object of worship. Inside the church the same reversal was stated as membership — “there is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free, there is no male and female, for you are all one in Christ Jesus” — the very axes on which the ancient world ranked persons, named precisely in order to be relativised before God. The philosopher Nicholas Wolterstorff has argued that this trajectory, not Greek or Roman antiquity, is the real seedbed of the modern idea of natural, inherent human rights: rights grounded in a worth that is bestowed.

The honest atheist witness

You do not have to be a believer to accept the diagnosis. The historian Yuval Noah Harari, an atheist, puts it with candour in Sapiens: examine the Declaration of Independence’s claim that “all men are created equal,” and biology gives it no support — “there are no such things as rights in biology,” and “Homo sapiens has no natural rights, just as spiders, hyenas and chimpanzees have no natural rights.” Harari draws a bleak conclusion from this that Christians do not share; the point of quoting him is not his despair but his honesty about the premise. He confirms, from the far side of the aisle, exactly what this card has argued: equal human worth is not written into nature and cannot be read off from it. It is a value that had to come from somewhere.

What this shows, and what it doesn’t

The church has been a repeated traitor to its own doctrine: it blessed slaveholding, tolerated caste, and looked away from cruelties its own scriptures condemned. Those failures are real. Nor did the conviction travel a straight or purely churchly road — much of its modern legal and political articulation came through the Enlightenment, sometimes phrased against the church in a deliberately secular idiom of natural rights. But we can call the church’s betrayals betrayals only because there was a standard to betray, and it was, in no small part, the church’s own standard that the reformers turned back against it: many abolitionists indicted Christian slaveholders from Genesis and the image of God, even as other Christians marshalled Scripture to defend slavery, in a genuine crisis of biblical interpretation. The ideal judged its own failures and armed its own critics. So when you find yourself certain that a trafficked child and a titan of industry are of exactly equal worth, you are standing at the end of a long and improbable history, most of which ran the other way.

The preconditions thesis — and its medieval lineage

Historians such as Reijer Hooykaas and Peter Harrison argue that three theological convictions supplied science’s intellectual preconditions. A free Creator makes the world contingent — it could have been otherwise, so its workings cannot be deduced from an armchair; you have to interrogate nature, not merely contemplate it. If the warfare thesis has one battle it can always name, it is Galileo — and honesty requires conceding what must be conceded. The Roman Catholic authorities were wrong to condemn him, wrong to bind the interpretation of Scripture to a particular astronomy, and wrong to silence a man for being, as it turned out, right. In plain English: For almost the whole of recorded history, in almost every civilisation, one human being owning another was not a scandal, not a debate, not even a question — it was simply how the world worked. Slavery is not the aberration that needs explaining; abolition is. And the ugliest chapter of that story belongs to Christians. Between the sixteenth century and the nineteenth, more than twelve million Africans were shipped across the Atlantic in ships owned and captained and insured overwhelmingly by baptised Christians, and the trade was defended from pulpits and in learned treatises. Say that first. Yet chattel slavery was abolished not once but twice in the history of the West, and both times the church supplied indispensable pressure. This card explains how — and refuses to tidy up the part of the story that shames the people telling it.
The key points
  1. Say the guilt first. The Atlantic trade was industrial-scale evil carried out by the church’s own — owners, captains and investors overwhelmingly professing Christians — and Scripture was used to defend it for centuries. Being handed the standard is an aggravating circumstance for the person who breaks it, never an excuse.
  2. Slavery was the universal norm, not the anomaly. “All known societies above the very primitive level have been slave societies” (Rodney Stark). The conviction that a person is not a thing was a minority report in world history — a discovery, and a late, bitterly contested one.
  3. The first abolition is almost forgotten: as the Roman order dissolved, the chattel slave of antiquity gave way to the serf, and outright ownership of persons had largely faded from the northern kingdoms by the high Middle Ages. Economic and technological change did much of the work; one strand was religious — it grew harder to own a person you baptised and communed beside.
  4. The second abolition had to be organised and fought. From the 1787 London society, through Wilberforce and the Clapham circle, to the enslaved who read the Bible and out-read their masters — Christian conviction was, in Christopher Leslie Brown’s careful judgement, necessary to the campaign but not by itself sufficient; it took a particular imperial moment, and slave risings, to become law.

No one saw the paradox more clearly than Frederick Douglass, who was born into slavery and became one of its greatest preacher-adversaries. Douglass drew a line no proof-text could blur: between “the slaveholding religion of this land” and “the Christianity of Christ,” which he called as opposite as heaven and hell. The churches that sanctified slavery he named without mercy — they had, he wrote, made themselves “the bulwark of American slavery.” And yet he condemned the slaveholder’s religion in the name of the slaveholder’s own Scripture. The people best placed to test whether the Bible was a slaveholder’s book were the enslaved who read it — and overwhelmingly they did not find in it their own justification. They found a God who heard slaves groaning in Egypt and broke an empire to free them.

Questions people ask
QBut the Bible never abolishes slavery — doesn’t that settle it? The sceptic has a real case, and it deserves its strongest form: the Bible regulates slavery rather than banning it, the household codes tell slaves to obey their masters, and honesty requires granting that when the isolated proof-texts are set side by side, the pro-slavery side had the easier of them. That is why the American churches faced a genuine crisis of interpretation, with Scripture marshalled on both sides and no quick knockdown for either (Mark Noll). The reply is not to deny this but to notice who answered it: the enslaved themselves, reading the same book, found the Exodus, the death penalty against man-stealing (Exodus 21:16), the condemnation of slave-traders (1 Timothy 1:10), and “neither slave nor free” in Christ. Read whole, the canon runs against an institution its parts merely tolerate.
QDoes the fact that Christians ended slavery prove Christianity is true? No, and the essay refuses that inference explicitly. A movement can do enormous good and still rest on false beliefs; social achievement is not evidence about God. The case for the truth of Christianity rests on the resurrection and is argued elsewhere in this library, not here. What the history of abolition shows is narrower: that the equal, unownable worth of every person was not humanity’s default assumption but a hard-won conviction with a traceable home — one that had to be turned against the church’s own failures before it won.

We forget how strange abolition is because we have inherited the answer and lost the memory of the question. The conviction that no human being may be owned feels to us like a moral floor, something any decent mind can simply see. It is not. It is a discovery, and a late and bitterly contested one. So the honest question is not “why did slavery finally end?” but “where on earth did anyone get the idea that it should?”

Say the guilt first

Because the ugliest chapter belongs to Christians, and any account that saves it for a footnote has already failed. The Atlantic trade shipped more than twelve million Africans into bondage in ships owned and captained overwhelmingly by baptised Christians, worked on plantations owned by baptised Christians, in an economy that made Christian nations rich — and the trade was defended from pulpits and in learned treatises. This is not a stain at the edge of the picture; it is evil, industrial in scale, done by the church’s own. One could note that the very standard by which we now condemn it is itself largely a Christian inheritance — but that does not soften the crime, it deepens it. Being handed the standard is an aggravating circumstance for the one who breaks it, never a mitigating one.

Abolished twice

Chattel slavery was abolished not once but twice in the West. The first abolition is almost forgotten: as the Roman order dissolved, the slave of antiquity gave way to the serf, and outright ownership of persons had largely faded from the northern kingdoms by the high Middle Ages — economic and technological change did much of the work, but it also grew steadily harder to hold as property a person you baptised and confessed a brother in Christ. Then the Atlantic economy revived slavery on a scale the ancient world never dreamed of, and the second abolition had to be fought all over again — through the 1787 London society, Wilberforce and the Clapham circle, and the enslaved who forced the question. The right claim is modest and durable: Christian conviction was an indispensable ingredient in a particular combination, not a lone cause and not a guarantee.

None of this proves Christianity true. That a movement did great good is no argument that its beliefs are correct; a false faith could inspire a noble campaign, and the case for the resurrection — where the truth of the faith actually rests — is made elsewhere in this library. The church’s complicity was real, vast and grievous, and is not cancelled by the abolitionists any more than a debt is cancelled by a later gift. The claim that remains is narrower, and it is enough: the idea that a person is not a thing had to be born somewhere, and it was substantially born here.

How to explain this simply

Keep the two halves of the story together and in order. First, the guilt: the trade was run and defended by professing Christians, and Scripture was used to justify it. Second, the achievement: the conviction that no human may be owned found a settled home in the doctrine that each person bears the image of God, and abolition, when it came, was driven decisively by Christians turning that conviction against Christian slaveholders. Guilt and achievement are both real; neither cancels the other.

In conversation: “Yes — Christians ran the slave trade, and that is worse, not better, for being done in the name of the faith that condemns it. But the idea that a person is not a thing wasn’t humanity’s default; slavery was. So where did anyone get the idea it should end — and why did the people who actually dismantled the Atlantic system keep quoting a Galilean carpenter?”

The abolition nobody remembers

Its most striking single voice is Gregory of Nyssa, a bishop in Cappadocia, who around 379 preached a sermon on Ecclesiastes that turns aside to attack slave-owning with a fury without parallel in the ancient world: to price a human being is to set a value on the one creature made in God’s image, whom the whole earth could not buy. Two honesties are owed. Gregory was an outlier even among Christians — most Fathers accepted slavery and merely urged masters to kindness — and his sermon is a meditation on the vanity of possessions, not a call for abolition as law. The seed is there; the harvest is centuries away. And yet the harvest came: across the early-medieval centuries the chattel slave gave way to the serf, and in England outright ownership of persons was effectively gone by 1200. The causes were many and are debated, and no honest account makes the church the sole engine — but one strand was specific: it became harder to own a person you baptised, communed beside, and confessed a brother in Christ. Slavery did not end because someone proved it wrong; it eroded, in part, under the pressure of a sacrament.

The campaign that had to be organised

In May 1787 twelve men — nine Quakers and three Anglicans, among them Granville Sharp and the tireless Thomas Clarkson — met in a London printing shop and founded the Society for Effecting the Abolition of the Slave Trade. In Parliament their cause found its voice in the evangelical convert William Wilberforce, and around him gathered the Clapham circle. Their methods invented modern activism: the mass petition, the boycott of slave-grown sugar, and Wedgwood’s medallion of a kneeling, chained African under the words “Am I not a man and a brother?” The trade was outlawed by Britain in 1807; the Act abolishing slavery itself passed the Commons days before Wilberforce died in 1833. David Brion Davis — the pre-eminent historian of New World slavery, and no apologist for the church — judged that “religion was the central concern of all the British abolitionist leaders.”

But the story must not be told as a providential pageant, and the best historians do not tell it that way. Christopher Leslie Brown’s Moral Capital complicates the heroism: Quaker and evangelical objections had existed for generations without ending anything; what changed in the 1780s was as much political as spiritual, as a Britain that had just lost the American colonies found in abolition a way to recover a sense of national virtue. Religious conviction, Brown argues, was necessary to the campaign and not by itself sufficient. And the enslaved were never merely waiting to be freed — the revolution in Haiti and the great risings in the Caribbean forced the question onto the empire as insistently as any committee in London.

The hardest objection: isn’t the Bible pro-slavery?

Here the sceptic has a real case, and it deserves its strongest form. The Bible nowhere commands abolition; the law of Moses regulates slavery; the household codes tell slaves to obey their masters (Ephesians 6:5; Colossians 3:22), even under unjust suffering (1 Peter 2:18); Paul sends the runaway Onesimus back to his owner. For eighteen centuries most Christians read this as permission, and the most sophisticated defences of slavery ever written came from Christian clergy. An honest reader has to grant that when the isolated proof-texts are set side by side, the pro-slavery side had the easier of them — and Mark Noll’s study shows the American Civil War arriving as a genuine crisis of biblical interpretation, Scripture marshalled on both sides with no quick knockdown for either. The reply is not to pretend the difficulty away but to notice who found the answer: the enslaved who read the same book found the Exodus, the capital charge against man-stealing (Exodus 21:16), the condemnation of slave-traders — andrapodistai — among the lawless (1 Timothy 1:10), and “neither slave nor free” in Christ (Galatians 3:28). What the abolitionist reading had was not a single decisive verse but the whole canon read entire, running against an institution its parts merely tolerated.

What this does and does not settle

The Enlightenment produced its own abolitionists too — Condorcet, Thomas Paine, the French amis des noirs — and revolutionary France abolished slavery in 1794; whether that secular conviction was itself an inheritance of the Christian claim it had begun to forget is a real and contested question, and one this card leaves open. What is not in doubt is that the Anglo-American movement that actually dismantled the Atlantic system was, at its root, a religious one. So “who ended slavery?” has no tidy single name: not the church, whose hands are bloody; not the enslaved alone, though they forced it; not the politics alone, though they closed it. It was, more than anything, a conviction — that a person is not a thing because a person is made in the image of God — held at last against the people who had betrayed it worst. The believer can take from that no boast, only a sober gratitude: that the faith was, in the end, truer than its followers.

The line King made famous was not his own coinage. The abolitionist minister Theodore Parker wrote it in 1853, and his original words are more honest than the compression: “I do not pretend to understand the moral universe; the arc is a long one… I cannot calculate the curve and complete the figure by the experience of sight; I can divine it by conscience. But from what I see I am sure it bends towards justice.” He knew it was a claim of faith, not of sight. King trimmed the hesitation and kept the hope. This is about where that hope came from.

The wheel, not the arrow

Step outside the modern West and the confidence that history improves largely evaporates. The Greek poet Hesiod, around 700 BC, gave the classic downhill slope: humanity began in a Golden Age and descended through Silver, Bronze, and the heroes to his own Iron race, doomed to labour and grief. Where the golden age lies behind you, the task of the wise is memory, not reform. Others saw a wheel: the Stoics taught that the cosmos passes through vast cycles, each ending in fire and beginning again — repeating exactly, the same Socrates marrying the same wife, endlessly. Nothing is ever finally gained or lost, because everything comes round again. None of this is to say the ancients imagined no improvement at all — Greek and Roman writers could celebrate advances in the arts and sciences. What the dominant pictures lacked was the further idea: that history as a whole is a story with a plot, morally judged, headed toward a goal it has not yet reached.

The Bible gave history an arrow

What the ancient world lacked, the Hebrew Scriptures supplied: a story that runs in a straight line and is going somewhere — creation, a call to Abraham, a rescue from slavery in Egypt, a journey to a promised land, prophets who summon the nation back to its covenant, and a promised kingdom in which the wrongs of history are finally set right. And that road is judged, measured against a standard ahead of it rather than behind. The prophets do not tell Israel to return to a golden age; they hold up a future in which justice arrives and indict the present for falling short. “Let justice roll down like waters,” thunders Amos, “and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream.” This is why King’s cadences sound the way they do: when he dreamed of the day when “every valley shall be exalted,” he was quoting Amos and Isaiah, preaching the prophets’ own vision of a history moving toward a justice that has not yet arrived.

The honest limit

Two limits keep this in its lane. None of it is evidence that Christianity is true: that a conviction has biblical roots tells you where it came from, not whether the faith is right about God, and the case for the faith’s truth is made elsewhere in this library, resting above all on the resurrection. And Christians have repeatedly been on the wrong side of the very reforms this hope demands. The point is genealogical, not congratulatory: the belief that history can bend toward justice is an inheritance, not a self-evident fact — and the honest next step is not to file the source off, but to go and ask whether the source is real.

The scholarship, and where it disagrees

The idea that a society could look at its own past, judge it wanting, and set out deliberately to be better is a specific and unusual conviction, and scholars who have mapped its pedigree do not all agree. Karl Löwith argued that the modern philosophy of progress is a secularized form of biblical eschatology — Judeo-Christian hope in worldly dress; it is a contested reading, and worth naming as one, for Hans Blumenberg replied that the idea of progress grows from within history, out of the open-ended experience of science and modern self-assertion, not from any borrowed eschatology. Robert Nisbet, unlike Löwith, credited the Greeks and Romans with real ideas of progress of their own. What is not seriously disputed is narrower, and enough for the point here: the biblical narrative gave the West a distinctive and load-bearing picture of time as a line with a destination — a picture the dominant ancient images of the cycle and the decline did not supply.

The secular borrowing — and the question it leaves exposed

There is nothing dishonest in a secularist reaching for prophetic language, and nothing to sneer at. But filing off the source leaves a real question exposed, and it is fairer to state it than to score off it. An arc has to bend toward something. Parker could name what: a justice fixed by conscience, which for him meant fixed by God. Pinker offers instead a more objective measure — the long tables of declining violence and rising health. Those tables are real, and this essay does not dispute a line of them. But a measurement of where we have gone cannot, by itself, tell us which direction counts as forward. For that you need a fixed point standing above history to be measured against — a standard the story cannot generate purely from within itself. That the moral law needs such a ground is the argument of a companion essay, not re-run here; the point here is only historical, and narrower: the confidence that history bends toward justice was born believing in that fixed point, and has not obviously found a replacement for it.

Progress without a pole star

That is not merely an academic worry, because the twentieth century tested it. The word “progress” names a movement but not a destination; it says forward without saying toward what — and a century that believed fervently in progress discovered that “forward” can be made to point almost anywhere. The philosopher Isaiah Berlin, in a 1953 lecture originally titled “History as an Alibi,” warned precisely against this: the notion that history has a guaranteed direction becomes a way of excusing whatever is done in its name, dissolving the moral responsibility of the people who do it. When the tide of history is thought to sanctify the deed, the deed no longer has to answer to anything higher than the tide.

The UDHR: “what,” not “why”

What is telling is where the century turned when it recoiled from its own worst chapters — it reached backward for a footing it could no longer supply from within. The framers of the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights did not open by deducing human dignity from progress or from nature; they opened by naming the pit they meant to climb out of — “barbarous acts which have outraged the conscience of mankind.” It states, with magnificent clarity, what we must never do again; it says far less, from its own resources, about why — why this creature carries a dignity that no majority and no future may override. Whether a secular account can supply that why from within is the question of a companion essay; the historical observation here is only that the drafters reached for the language of conscience outraged, not for a derivation from progress or from nature.

The mountaintop — and the honest bound

On his last night, in Memphis in 1968, King preached that he had “been to the mountaintop.” He was standing, deliberately, in the sandals of Moses — the old prophet shown the promised land from the summit of Nebo, who would not himself cross into it. The Christian arc is not a smooth upward line; it runs down into suffering before it rises, its clearest emblem a cross before an empty tomb. Progress, on this telling, is not the automatic drift of history but a road that plumbs the depths before it climbs — and that can be walked with confidence only because the destination is secured by the One who set it there. The person who believes the arc bends toward justice is, whether they know it or not, keeping faith with an old and specific hope. The honest next step is not to file the source off, but to go and ask whether the source is real.
