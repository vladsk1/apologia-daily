Old Testament Foundations of the Trinity

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and stay clear and calm in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

Christians hold that God is one being in three persons — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — fully one God, not three gods. The Old Testament is fiercely monotheistic, so the question here is whether its pages leave room for, and even hint toward, the fuller revelation of God's threeness that arrives in Christ. The honest answer is that the Trinity is not taught in the Old Testament, but its soil is prepared there.

Several threads point this way — and one famous one does not. Genesis 1:26's "Let us make man in our image" is best left out of the case entirely: the plural has natural explanations in its own ancient setting, and even trinitarian scholars of Israel's heavenly council read it as God addressing that court and warn against using it as a proof-text. The threads that do carry weight lie elsewhere. The mysterious "Angel of the LORD" is both distinguished from God and identified as God. God's Word, Wisdom, and Spirit act as living agents of creation and revelation. And Genesis 19:24 sets the LORD on earth beside the LORD in heaven, and Daniel 7:13-14 shows a heavenly figure coming with the clouds, elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible God's own conveyance, to receive everlasting dominion (who that figure is remains debated). None of these forces the doctrine on its own, but together they show that Israel's God is richer than a bare, undifferentiated unity.

Critics rightly press that each of these threads can be read another way, and no single one of them is decisive. The Christian claim is correspondingly modest: the Old Testament does not contradict the Trinity but anticipates it, so that the New Testament reveals more fully the one God Israel already worshipped. So the objection that the Old Testament rules out the Trinity does not hold. And this is what we hold to be true: the one God exists eternally as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

Before you start · key terms

Eight words this argument leans on

Learn these first — the objections below turn on them, and most weak versions of the argument come from blurring one of them.

Strict monotheism (the Shema)"Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4) — the heartbeat of Israel's faith, confessed against a world crowded with gods. Any honest discussion of the Trinity and the Old Testament has to begin here, on Israel's own ground, and refuse to leave it. On Richard Bauckham's reading, Second Temple monotheism was "strict," defined not by bare numerical oneness but by the unique identity of the one God: the only Creator, the only sovereign, the only rightful recipient of worship.
Progressive revelationScripture's own category (Hebrews 1:1–2): God's self-disclosure unfolds over time, seed to flower — the suffering Messiah, the bodily resurrection, the inclusion of the Gentiles are each foreshadowed in the older books and revealed fully only later. On this view the Old Testament gives shadows, not statues — but shadows are cast by something. The claim is not that Moses taught Nicaea.
The Angel of the LORD (mal'akh YHWH)The figure who appears to Hagar, to Abraham, to Moses at the burning bush, and to Jacob — who speaks as the LORD, is identified as the LORD, and bears the Name ("my name is in him," Exodus 23:21), yet is somehow distinct from the LORD who sends him. Identity and distinction held together in a single character.
The two-YHWH textsPassages that set the LORD beside the LORD: Genesis 19:24, "the LORD rained... fire from the LORD out of heaven" — the LORD on earth and the LORD in heaven side by side; Psalm 110:1, "The LORD says to my Lord"; and Zechariah 2, where on one natural reading YHWH says YHWH has sent him, though the sender/sent referent here is debated. None of these, alone, demands two persons; together they form a pattern the Old Testament never resolves and never disowns.
Word, Wisdom, and SpiritGod's Word by which the heavens were made (Psalm 33:6), the Wisdom beside him as a master craftsman at creation (Proverbs 8), and the Spirit who can be "grieved" and rebelled against (Isaiah 63:10) and is named alongside God as sending a speaker (Isaiah 48:16) — not three persons yet, but the canon repeatedly drawing distinctions within the one God.
Two powers in heaven (shtei reshuyot ba-shamayim)The early rabbinic category of heresy Alan Segal documented — readings that found a second divine figure beside the LORD. The decisive point is methodological: you do not legislate against an interpretation no one holds, so the controversy is evidence that the texts themselves generated binitarian readings inside Judaism, many of them apparently before and apart from the church.
The divine-council readingThe strongest explanation of Genesis 1:26's "Let us make" — God addressing his heavenly court, the "sons of God" and angelic council attested across the Old Testament — favored by many critical scholars and held by the trinitarian Michael Heiser, who warns against using the verse as a Trinity proof-text. The next verse's singular "God created" shows that only God does the creating. This reading is granted; the case does not rest on the verse.
Daniel 7's heavenly figureThe "one like a son of man" who comes "with the clouds of heaven" — elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible God's own conveyance — and receives an everlasting dominion, a second figure in the one heavenly scene that the vision never explains. Who the figure is remains genuinely debated: an exalted angelic representative (Collins: most likely Michael), or a heavenly individual (Boyarin, a contested minority). The clouds are the stronger half of the case; the service-verb (pelach) can denote either religious worship or the service owed to a king.
The argument, formally

The plurality was on the page before the church existed.

P1

The Hebrew Bible repeatedly presents plurality within the one YHWH: the Angel of the LORD who speaks as YHWH and bears the Name (Ex 23:21) yet is sent by YHWH; 'the LORD rained fire... from the LORD out of heaven' (Gen 19:24); the Word, Wisdom and Spirit acting as God with God; Daniel 7's heavenly figure coming with the clouds to receive an everlasting dominion (Dan 7:13-14). Genesis 1:26's 'Let US make' is deliberately not on this list — see the objections below.

P2

These are not Christian retro-readings: early Jewish interpretation wrestled with the same texts — the 'two powers in heaven' controversies, Daniel 7's thrones (note the plural in 7:9), the Targums' creating Memra — many of them, it appears, before and apart from the church.

∴ C

Therefore the Trinity does not collide with the Old Testament — it answers questions the Old Testament itself raised.

Calibrate the claim: the OT does not teach the developed doctrine — it seeds it. Shadows, not statues; but shadows are cast by something.

The key concept — progressive revelation, not retrofit.

Scripture's pattern is seed-then-flower: Messiah, resurrection, and the Gentile mission all unfold this way. The Trinitarian readings claim no more — that when the Son arrived, the strange grammar of the older books (a LORD sent by the LORD, a Word that creates, a Spirit who is grieved) finally had a face.

Defending Premise 1

The Hebrew Bible repeatedly presents plurality within the one YHWH: the Angel of the LORD who speaks as YHWH and bears the Name (Ex 23:21) yet is sent by YHWH; ‘the LORD rained fire... from the LORD out of heaven’ (Gen 19:24); the Word, Wisdom and Spirit acting as God with God; Daniel 7's heavenly figure coming with the clouds to receive an everlasting dominion (Dan 7:13-14). Genesis 1:26's ‘Let US make’ is deliberately not on this list — see the objections below.

Precision check: does the Old Testament teach the doctrine of the Trinity? No — and we should say so plainly. Ancient Israel was monotheistic, and no Israelite prophet ever set out a doctrine of three persons in one essence. The narrower question is whether the Old Testament, within its fierce monotheism, leaves a kind of unexplained plurality that the New Testament later names. The claim is not that Moses taught Nicaea: the Old Testament gives shadows, not statues. The foundations point toward plurality-in-unity; they do not, by themselves, secure the coequality and coeternity that the creeds rightly insist on, and they certainly do not teach three gods (tritheism), or one person in three masks (modalism), or a lesser second god (subordinationism).

The Angel of the LORD Suggestive · a pattern, not a proof

He speaks in first person as God (‘I am the God of Bethel’), bears the Name (Ex 23:21: “my name is in him”), prompts Manoah to say ‘we shall surely die, for we have seen God’ (Judges 13:22) — yet is also sent by YHWH. He speaks to Moses from the burning bush, and the text says it was “the LORD” and “God” who called (Exodus 3:2–6). Jacob says, “I have seen God face to face” (Genesis 32:30). Again and again this figure speaks as the LORD, is identified as the LORD, and yet is somehow distinct from the LORD who sends him — identity and distinction held together in a single character.

A LORD sent by the LORD — the two-YHWH texts Suggestive

Gen 19:24’s LORD-from-the-LORD: “the LORD rained on Sodom and Gomorrah sulfur and fire from the LORD out of heaven” — a sentence in which the LORD on earth and the LORD in heaven sit side by side. Psalm 110’s ‘The LORD said to my Lord’ — the Old Testament verse the New Testament quotes or echoes most often; and Zechariah 2, where on one natural reading YHWH says YHWH has sent him. Together they form a pattern that the Old Testament never resolves and never disowns.

Honest limit: the second “Lord” of Psalm 110:1 is adoni, a title also used of human masters, so the verse alone does not make David’s lord divine; its weight comes from how Jesus pressed it in Mark 12:35–37. The sender/sent referent in Zechariah is debated. None of these, alone, demands two persons.

Word, Wisdom, and Spirit Suggestive · not yet three persons

“By the word of the LORD the heavens were made” (Psalm 33:6). In Proverbs 8, Wisdom speaks in the first person as having been with God “at the beginning of his work,” a master craftsman beside him at creation. The Spirit of God can be “grieved” and rebelled against (Isaiah 63:10), can instruct (Nehemiah 9:20), and in Isaiah 48:16 a speaker says the Lord GOD “and his Spirit” have sent him — the Spirit named alongside God, not merely wielded by him. Taken together it is more than metaphor strained to its limit; it is the canon repeatedly drawing distinctions within the one God.

Honest limit: none of this yet amounts to three persons. Nor does Proverbs 8:22 make the Son a creature, as the Arians argued from the Septuagint’s “created me”: the Hebrew verb (qanah) is disputed and is often rendered “possessed” or “acquired,” and the New Testament nowhere cites the verse to teach that the Son was created.

The two figures of Daniel 7 Suggestive rather than decisive

The “Ancient of Days” takes his throne; then “one like a son of man” comes “with the clouds of heaven,” is presented before the Ancient of Days, and receives “dominion and glory and a kingdom, that all peoples, nations, and languages should serve him” — an everlasting dominion that shall not pass away (Daniel 7:9–14). Riding the clouds is, throughout the Old Testament, an image elsewhere used of God. The most that can be said from the text alone is that the scene is strikingly suggestive: here are two figures in the one heavenly scene — one ancient and enthroned, one human-like, coming with the clouds and given everlasting dominion — and Daniel does not explain how a second figure comes to receive such universal, everlasting homage. He simply records the vision.

Honest limit: this is suggestive rather than decisive: many critical scholars (Collins among them) read the human-like figure as an exalted angelic representative, most likely Michael, rather than as a second divine person, and on that reading the figure need not be divine at all. The Aramaic verb for “service” (pelach) can denote either religious worship or the service owed to a king, and which sense is intended is genuinely debated.

Convergence — the same shape from independent devices The core warrant · convergence

Take the threads singly and each has an innocent explanation; the question is why the same shape keeps emerging from independent devices. The Angel, the Word, the Wisdom, the Name, the Glory, the Spirit, the second figure of Daniel 7 — different vocabularies, different genres, different centuries — all keep generating one silhouette: something that is of God, with God, and acting as God, distinguishable from the LORD who sends it yet never a second god. The strongest objection to one thread is not, in the end, an objection to the pattern the threads compose.

Objection — “Let us make” is the royal we, or God addressing angels

The standard scholarly options for Genesis 1:26 are well known and entirely natural within an ancient Near Eastern setting: a plural of deliberation or self-address; a plural of majesty; or — most favored by many critical scholars — God addressing his heavenly court, the “sons of God” and angelic council attested across the Old Testament. On the divine-council reading, “let us make” is God speaking to his angelic attendants, exactly as a king addresses his court — and the very next verse, with its singular “God created,” shows that only God does the creating. No Trinity is needed or implied.

Reply

Largely granted — and this is exactly where popular apologetics overreaches. The divine-council interpretation is a serious, perhaps the most defensible, reading of that single verse; Michael Heiser, who has done as much as anyone to revive the divine-council framework, takes “let us” as God’s address to his heavenly host and warns against using it as a Trinity proof-text. Anyone who claims Genesis 1:26 “proves” three persons has overreached, and should stop. So the verse carries no weight here and we do not ask it to. The council reading does not erase the pattern; it relocates the question: it still leaves Daniel 7’s heavenly figure coming with the clouds — elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible God’s own conveyance — to receive an everlasting dominion, a scene the council reading of Genesis does nothing to explain.

Objection — the Angel is just an envoy speaking in the sender’s name

In the ancient world, an emissary (Hebrew shaliach) spoke and acted in the full authority of the one who sent him; to receive the king’s messenger was to receive the king. That convention, not a hidden second person, explains why the Angel speaks as the LORD. And Wisdom in Proverbs 8 is plainly personified poetry — the same book personifies Folly as a woman (9:13) without anyone supposing a goddess of Folly.

Reply

The envoy convention really does explain a great deal of the Angel’s speech, and Wisdom in Proverbs 8 really is, in its first sense, personification. But the envoy convention explains speech, and conventional envoys do not bear the divine Name within them — and of this figure God says, uniquely, “my name is in him” (Exodus 23:21) — and they do not prompt a man to say he has seen God’s face and lived. Personified Wisdom is poetry, but personified Folly is never placed beside God at creation. If the case rested on any one of these threads, it would fail. It does not rest on any one of them.

The room: Michael Heiser holds the divine-council reading of Genesis 1:26 and warns against the proof-text; Collins reads Daniel’s figure as an exalted angelic representative; Hebraists defend the “plural of deliberation.” The divine-council reading is held by serious scholars and may well be the plain sense — the Jewish alternatives are not desperate.
Defending Premise 2

These are not Christian retro-readings: early Jewish interpretation wrestled with the same texts — the ‘two powers in heaven’ controversies, Daniel 7's thrones (note the plural in 7:9), the Targums' creating Memra — many of them, it appears, before and apart from the church.

Precision check: that a community read a second power out of Daniel 7 shows the texts were suggestive, not that the suggestion was correct. “The texts generated binitarian readings” is not the same as “the texts are best explained by a true Trinity.” This is a real limit, and we should not pretend otherwise.

Two powers in heaven — Segal Strong · as history

Rabbinic literature records and combats ‘two powers in heaven’ readings (shtei reshuyot ba-shamayim) — Segal’s classic study. In his landmark 1977 study, the Jewish scholar Alan F. Segal showed that “two powers” was an early and basic category of heresy by which the rabbis perceived dangerous interpreters — those who read Scripture to find a second divine figure beside the LORD. You do not legislate against interpretations no one holds; the texts generated them inside Judaism — Daniel 7’s thrones (note the plural in 7:9), the Angel, the LORD-from-the-LORD passages.

Honest limit: Jewish scholars who document “two powers” readings emphatically do not endorse them as true.

Boyarin’s binitarian Judaism Debated

The Jewish historian Daniel Boyarin has argued that a “binitarian” theology — belief in a second divine figure, often connected to God’s Logos or Wisdom — was, on his reading, widespread in the Judaism of Jesus’s day, and that the idea of a divine-human redeemer is itself a Jewish idea rooted in Daniel 7, not a later Gentile import.

Honest limit: Boyarin’s claim is itself debated — Peter Schäfer and others judge it an overreading — but even his critics grant that the conceptual room for such readings existed.

Word and Wisdom — the Targums and Philo Supporting

Proverbs 8’s Wisdom beside God at creation; the Targums’ MEMRA creating and covenanting where the Hebrew has YHWH. The ancient Aramaic Targums, paraphrasing the Scriptures for synagogue use, frequently put the Memra (“Word”) of the LORD where the Hebrew has the LORD himself — creating, saving, covenanting. And the Jewish philosopher Philo of Alexandria, a contemporary of Jesus, could call the divine Logos a “second God” (deuteros theos). The conceptual slots, in other words, predate Bethlehem. John’s prologue did not invent the category; it claimed that a Jewish category, long open, had been filled.

Objection — “Generated” is not “true”: suggestive texts are not a Trinity Hardest

The Christian has shown only that the Old Testament is suggestive and that some ancient Jews found it so — which is a long way from showing that the suggestion is true, and the leap from “unexplained plurality” to “three coequal persons” is supplied entirely by the New Testament, not the Old.

Reply

As a description of what the Old Testament can do on its own, that is accurate, and we concede it. The Old Testament foundation does not, on its own, get anyone to Nicaea. What it does is more limited and more important: it shows that the Trinitarian God is the kind of God Israel’s Scriptures were already describing — so that the Christian confession is the completion of Israel’s story rather than a betrayal of it. The doctrine is established on other grounds; the Old Testament shows it has deep and native roots.

Objection — the Shema: “The LORD is one” rules out any plurality

The Old Testament does not merely fail to teach the Trinity; it relentlessly teaches the opposite. “The LORD is one.” “I am the LORD, and there is no other” (Isaiah 45:5). “I am the first and I am the last; besides me there is no god” (Isaiah 44:6). Isaiah even insists that the LORD stretched out the heavens “alone” and spread out the earth “by myself” (44:24). On the most important fact about God, the canon hammers undifferentiated oneness on page after page. A doctrine of plurality smuggled into that polemic is not a discovery; it is a contradiction.

Reply

The Old Testament is monotheistic, and any reading that turns the Shema into a confession of three gods has misread it catastrophically. Isaiah’s “alone” and “by myself” exclude every rival and every creature — the heavenly council included — from the work of creation; they do not legislate on the inner life of the one Creator. So the objection that the Old Testament rules out the Trinity does not hold: the Trinity does not contradict the Old Testament; it answers questions the Old Testament itself raised. The positive case rests on revelation and on the historical case for who Jesus was.

The room: Segal showed—and Boyarin argues more ambitiously—that the conceptual room for a second divine figure existed within Judaism itself; Richard Bauckham argues for a “strict” monotheism defined by the unique identity of the one God, and Larry Hurtado documents how the earliest Christians, themselves Jews, included Jesus in their devotion. There is real, ongoing disagreement — Bauckham and the “intermediary figures” school dispute how elastic Jewish monotheism was.

The objections you will actually meet

"Gen 19:24 is stylistic repetition — one LORD, restated" Trickier

Strongest form

Hebrew repeats divine names for emphasis, like 'the king built the palace of the king.' Two persons from style is desperate.

Reply

Sometimes repetition is style — but the SETUP resists it here: in the preceding scene YHWH stands on earth talking with Abraham while two angels go to Sodom; then fire falls from YHWH 'out of heaven.' YHWH below, YHWH above, in the narrative's own staging. No one text alone demands two persons; the pattern does. And ancient Jewish discussion flagged this verse in the two-powers controversies — the difficulty is native, not imported.

"Wisdom is poetry and Memra is a buffer — not persons" Trickier

Strongest form

Proverbs personifies Wisdom like it personifies Folly; the Targums' Memra just avoids anthropomorphism. You are stacking metaphors into a person.

Reply

Granted singly — the argument rests on convergence: device after device (Word, Wisdom, Name, Glory, Spirit, Angel) independently develops the same shape — something of God, with God, acting as God — until Philo can write 'second God' and rabbis must police two powers. When a tradition's mere devices keep generating one theological silhouette, the silhouette is data. John 1 did not invent the slot; it announced who filled it.

"Ruach is breath and power — no third person in the OT" Hard

Strongest form

Ruach means wind, God's energy at work, parallel to his hand or arm. OT pneumatology gives you a force.

Reply

Often — and then the texts overrun the category: the Spirit is GRIEVED and rebelled against (Isaiah 63:10 — you cannot mutiny against weather), instructs (Nehemiah 9:20), and in Isaiah 48:16 a speaker is sent by the Lord GOD 'AND his Spirit' — alongside, not wielded. More than force, not yet fully unveiled — exactly what progressive revelation predicts, completed when lying to the Spirit is lying to God.

"If the Trinity were there, the prophets would have said so — the silence is denial" Hard

Strongest form

On the most important fact about God, the whole OT manages not to teach it while hammering oneness. The omission IS the doctrine.

Reply

The OT hammers oneness against the nations' gods — and within that polemic still writes a LORD sent by the LORD, a creating Word, a grievable Spirit, a figure coming with the clouds in Daniel 7. Silence about the FORMULATION is the normal shape of revelation before its hour — the suffering Messiah and the resurrection are likewise seeded, debated by Israel, unveiled late: 'at many times and in various ways... in these last days by his Son.' The claim was never that Moses taught Nicaea; it is that the Trinity answers questions the OT itself raised.

A fairness most defenders skip: The Jewish alternatives are not desperate and must be met at full strength. John J. Collins reads Daniel's figure as an exalted angelic representative, most likely Michael; Hebraists defend the "plural of deliberation"; Michael Heiser holds the divine-council reading of Genesis 1:26. Daniel Boyarin's bolder claim — that a "binitarian" Judaism was widespread and that the divine-human redeemer is itself a Jewish idea rooted in Daniel 7 — is contested, and Peter Schäfer and others judge it an overreading, though even his critics grant that the conceptual room existed. Richard Bauckham and Larry Hurtado reframed the field around a "strict" monotheism of unique identity and an early "binitarian/dyadic" devotion to Jesus; how elastic Jewish monotheism was is a genuine live dispute. Jewish scholars who document "two powers" readings emphatically do not endorse them as true.

The bounded verdict: The foundations are not the house. The Old Testament does not teach the doctrine of the Trinity — ancient Israel was monotheistic, the developed dogma is later, and the plurals and the Angel can each be read otherwise; anyone who claims Genesis 1:26 "proves" three persons has overreached and should stop. But it does present the one God as internally rich and self-differentiated in ways Israel never explained away, and it left those tensions live enough that Israel's own interpreters had to argue about "two powers" long before any Christian creed. So the objection that the Old Testament rules out the Trinity does not hold: it does not contradict the Trinity; it answers questions the Old Testament itself raised. The doctrine is established on other grounds — revelation and the historical case for who Jesus was — and this is what we hold to be true: the one God exists eternally as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, three coequal, coeternal persons, one God.

    Gold gate · the unseen objection

    One you weren’t coached on

    Everything above rehearsed objections the page showed you. Here is the real test of mastery: an objection we deliberately did not drill. Reason it out cold from what the argument established, then reveal how it actually handles it. An honest “nailed it” is the first half of Gold.

    SK
    Skeptic
    "Fine — grant that the Old Testament hints at a second divine figure. But the earliest Christians who read it that way, like Justin Martyr, made that figure a lesser, subordinate "second God" under the Maker of all. So your Old Testament "foundations" don't lead to your coequal Trinity at all — they lead to a demoted second god. You've proved subordinationism, not the Trinity."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    That is honest, and it marks a real limit — the early Christian use of these texts was sometimes theologically rougher than later orthodoxy. When Justin Martyr argued from the Angel and from Genesis 18–19 that there is "another God and Lord" beside the Maker of all, he did frame that second figure as in some sense subordinate — language the church later judged inadequate and corrected at Nicaea, which confessed the Son as "true God from true God... of one being (homoousios) with the Father." So the concession stands: the Old Testament foundations point toward plurality-in-unity, but they do not, by themselves, secure the coequality and coeternity that the creeds rightly insist on, and they certainly do not teach three gods (tritheism), or one person in three masks (modalism), or a lesser second god (subordinationism). Those refinements come from the fuller New Testament revelation read as a whole. But notice what the convergence actually keeps throwing off: a figure "distinguishable from the LORD who sends it yet never a second god" — of God, with God, acting as God, within Israel's fierce monotheism. That is precisely the shape a coequal Trinity completes, not the shape of a demoted deity bolted beside the first. The doctrine is established on other grounds — revelation and the resurrection; the Old Testament shows only that it has deep and native roots.
