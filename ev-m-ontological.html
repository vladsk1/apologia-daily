The Ontological Argument

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and locate exactly where its weight rests.

The argument in brief

The ontological argument is the most unusual of the classic arguments for God: it tries to move from a concept to reality using logic alone, without appealing to evidence from the world. First framed by Anselm of Canterbury in the eleventh century and given its best-known modern modal form by philosopher Alvin Plantinga, it begins with the idea of a "maximally great being" — one that is all-powerful, all-knowing, perfectly good, and exists necessarily in every possible world.

Plantinga's version runs through the logic of possibility and necessity (the modal system known as S5). If it is even possible that such a maximally great being exists — that is, if its existence is not incoherent — then by definition it exists necessarily, and a necessary being that exists in any possible world exists in all of them, including the actual one. So the entire argument rests on a single load-bearing claim: that a maximally great being is genuinely possible.

In candor, even Plantinga grants this is not a proof that should compel every rational person; a skeptic can reply that maximal greatness is only possibly impossible, and the symmetry is hard to break by logic alone. Its honest value is more modest: it shows that belief in God can be rational, and that if God's existence is possible at all, it is necessary — there is no "maybe" with a being like that. In our judgment the two possibility claims are not perfectly symmetric — maximal greatness is a positive, coherent concept whose impossibility would need a hidden contradiction no one has produced — which tilts the scales without compelling. And even granted in full, the argument reaches a maximally great being, not, by thought alone, the specifically Christian God; that this being is Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, and that the Son was made flesh in Jesus and raised from the dead, is known through revelation and defended on historical grounds.

The argument, formally

Three lines that reason from a concept to a reality.

P1

It is possible that a maximally great being (necessary, omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good) exists.

P2

A necessary being possible in any world exists in every world — that is what necessary means.

∴ C

Therefore a maximally great being exists — in this world too. God exists.

The modal version is logically valid (it uses the standard modal system S5). So the entire argument rests on one claim: the possibility premise (P1). Almost every objection is really a challenge to whether a maximally great being is genuinely possible — spot that and you are halfway to the answer.

Honestly stated: the modal version is valid, so the whole dispute is the possibility premise — and because its mirror (“possibly no maximally great being exists”) seems an epistemic par, the argument persuades only someone who already grants that such a being is possible. Plantinga, its foremost defender, never billed it as a proof that compels: it shows theistic belief is rational, not that an atheist is irrational for declining. And even granted in full it reaches a maximally great being — necessary, perfect in power, knowledge and goodness — not the triune God by thought alone; that this being is Father, Son and Holy Spirit, incarnate in Jesus and raised from the dead, is known through revelation and the historical case for the resurrection. It is one strand, held as rational acceptability rather than certainty.

Defending Premise 1

It is possible that a maximally great being (necessary, omnipotent, omniscient, perfectly good) exists.

Precision check: the premise claims only that God’s existence is possible — you need not prove God exists. A being has maximal excellence in a given possible world if it is omniscient, omnipotent, and morally perfect in that world, and maximal greatness if it has maximal excellence in every possible world. The inference is valid in S5 and not seriously disputed; the entire weight sits on this premise, the possibility premise.

No evident incoherence The one load-bearing premise

Maximal greatness shows no evident incoherence — unlike “the largest number” or “a square circle,” its defining properties (power, knowledge, goodness, necessary existence) are positive and jointly statable. Its impossibility would require a hidden contradiction no one has produced.

Honest narrowing: this is exactly where the argument reaches its limit. The mirror premise — “possibly, no maximally great being exists” — seems just as conceivable, and in S5 it is just as potent (it would make God impossible). Mere conceivability is a feeble guide (Goldbach’s conjecture seems possible either way, yet one is impossible). So the premise’s advantage over its mirror must be argued, not assumed, and the argument cannot coerce a doubter who declines it.

A positive, coherent, unified concept Tilts, does not compel

A defender may argue that maximal greatness is a positive, coherent, intrinsically unified concept (Gödel’s formalized version tries to prove the relevant properties are jointly consistent), whereas its impossibility would require a hidden contradiction no one has produced — so the two premises are not perfectly symmetric after all. In our judgment that reply tilts the scales toward the possibility premise; it does not compel.

Honest limit: Gödel’s own proof has in turn been charged with entailing modal collapse.

What the dispute is really about Not an empty result

The argument shows the theist and the atheist are not disputing a contingent fact but a modal one: whether a maximally great being is so much as possible. If it is, it exists necessarily; the atheist must claim not merely that God happens not to exist, but that God cannot — a far heavier burden than atheism usually admits.

Honest limit: to establish that premise turns out to be as difficult as establishing the conclusion itself, because the premise already contains necessary existence inside it.

Plantinga’s own claim: rational acceptability The honest ceiling

It is a credit to Plantinga’s intellectual honesty that he never billed his argument as a proof that should compel any rational person. His verdict on the family of ontological arguments is modest and precise: “They cannot, perhaps, be said to prove or establish their conclusion. But since it is rational to accept their central premise, they do show that it is rational to accept that conclusion.”

Honest limit: the argument, on his own account, does not move an atheist from disbelief to belief. What it does is show that if the possibility premise is rationally acceptable — and Plantinga thinks it is — then accepting the existence of a maximally great being is rational, not a failure of reason. That is a real result, but a far humbler one than “proof of God.”

Objection — the symmetry parody Hardest

The theist asserts: “Possibly, a maximally great being exists.” But consider the mirror claim: “Possibly, a maximally great being does not exist.” This too seems perfectly conceivable — there is no obvious contradiction in a godless world. Yet in S5 this mirror claim is just as potent: if it is even possible that a maximally great being fails to exist, then it fails to exist necessarily. The two possibility claims cannot both be true, and they appear to be on an exact epistemic par. Each merely says “I can detect no contradiction in this scenario.”

Reply

This is the strongest objection, and it shows the argument cannot coerce a skeptic — it is only as strong as the possibility premise. But the two premises are not perfectly symmetric: maximal greatness is a positive, coherent concept whose impossibility would need a hidden contradiction no one has produced. In our judgment that tilts the scales toward the possibility premise; it does not compel. And note what the mirror costs the atheist: he must claim not merely that God happens not to exist, but that God cannot — a far heavier burden than atheism usually admits.

Objection — the premise is as hard to establish as the conclusion

The argument is valid, but it persuades only someone who already grants that a maximally great being is possible. To establish that premise turns out to be as difficult as establishing the conclusion itself, because the premise already contains necessary existence inside it.

Reply

Here the argument reaches the limit of its persuasive power: it transfers the dispute to a premise the doubter can decline, and whose advantage over its mirror image must be argued rather than assumed. That is a real limit — but not an empty result. And, more modestly, the defender can simply lower the claim — which is exactly what Plantinga himself does.

The room: Alvin Plantinga’s modal version and Kurt Gödel’s formalized proof (itself charged with entailing modal collapse) defend the possibility premise. J. L. Mackie and Graham Oppy press the symmetry objection against it. The possibility premise is contested with no consensus — and many theists themselves are unpersuaded the argument adds anything.
Defending Premise 2

A necessary being possible in any world exists in every world — that is what necessary means.

Precision check: necessary existence means existence in all worlds. The engine is a principle of the modal system S5: if something is possibly necessary, then it is necessary. Granted P1, the conclusion follows; the validity is not where the fight is. One caveat: applying S5 to metaphysical modality (rather than merely to logical or epistemic modality) is itself a substantive commitment — standard and well-motivated, but one a critic can in principle reject.

The inference — S5 does the rest Valid, not seriously disputed

Necessary existence just means existence in every possible world. So if a necessarily-existing being exists in even one possible world, S5 carries it to all of them, including ours. Granted P1, the conclusion follows; the validity is not where the fight is.

Honest narrowing: applying S5 to metaphysical modality (not merely logical or epistemic) is a standard, well-motivated commitment — but a substantive one a critic can in principle reject, choosing to fight the logic rather than the possibility premise.

Anselm’s seed: a being that cannot be conceived not to exist Historical root

Anselm presses further: such a being cannot even be conceived not to exist, for a being whose non-existence is unthinkable is greater than one whose non-existence is thinkable. God exists not merely actually but necessarily. This second formulation — about necessary existence — is the seed from which the modern versions grow.

What survives: no coherent middle Conditional result

What survives is genuine and not nothing: the argument demonstrates that if a maximally great being is so much as possible, then it actually and necessarily exists — there is no coherent middle position in which God is a merely contingent or merely possible being.

Honest limit: even granted in full, the argument reaches a maximally great being — necessary, and by its own definition perfect in power, knowledge, and goodness — but it does not, and cannot, reach the specifically Christian God by thought alone. That this necessary and perfect being is Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, and that the Son was made flesh in Jesus of Nazareth and raised from the dead, is not something the bare concept of maximal greatness yields; classical Christianity holds it to be the same God, but one known as triune and incarnate only through revelation and defended on historical grounds.

Objection — Kant: existence is not a real predicate Hard

Kant denies a hidden assumption he finds in the Cartesian proof, and that many readers find in Anselm too: that existence is a property or “perfection” a thing can have or lack, such that having it makes a thing greater: “Being,” he writes, “is obviously not a real predicate; that is, it is not a concept of something which could be added to the concept of a thing.” A hundred real coins contain no more as a concept than a hundred merely possible coins. Existence adds nothing to what a thing is; it merely posits that it is. So when Descartes packs “existence” into the concept of a perfect being as one more perfection, he has, says Kant, simply smuggled the conclusion into the premise.

Reply

Here we should concede openly: against any form that treats bare existence as one more perfection, Kant’s objection has real force, and many philosophers judge it decisive — though whether it touches Anselm’s own reasoning is itself disputed. This is precisely why the modern argument abandons “existence is a perfection” and shifts to necessary existence — a modal status, a matter of how a thing exists across possible worlds — which many hold is a genuine great-making feature even if bare existence is not. Whether this fully escapes Kant is itself debated, but the modal version is a deliberate, honest retreat from the ground Kant captured.

Objection — Gaunilo’s lost island

Imagine the most excellent island conceivable. By Anselm’s reasoning, since an island that exists in reality is greater than one existing only in thought, the perfect island must exist. But this is absurd; no such island is out there. So the form of reasoning must be defective. If Anselm’s logic conjures God, it conjures perfect islands, perfect pizzas, perfect anything — and that cannot be right.

Reply

Anselm’s reply, in his Reply to Gaunilo, is that the parody misfires because an island is the wrong kind of thing. Islands are by nature contingent and bounded: nothing about the concept of an island includes necessary existence — and, as Alvin Plantinga later added, there is no intrinsic maximum to island-greatness (a larger one, with more palms, is always conceivable). The argument was never meant to apply to “greatest possible Xs” in general, but only to a being than which none greater can be conceived at all — a being whose very concept, uniquely, contains necessary existence. Whether this reply fully succeeds is contested — many philosophers think Gaunilo located a real worry about defining things into existence — but the disanalogy between contingent and necessary beings is genuine and remains the standard defense.

The room: the argument runs from Anselm (Proslogion) through Descartes (the Fifth Meditation’s “existence is a perfection” version — the one Kant targets) to the modal version. The critics are weighty: Gaunilo (the lost island) and Kant (existence is not a real predicate). The validity of the modal version is not seriously disputed.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

This argument feels like a trick to many people — so the objections matter more than usual. Strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged below.

The one that matters most: the reverse-possibility parody — if a maximally great being is possibly impossible, it is impossible. The deep dive weighs it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"Gaunilo’s perfect island" Common

Strongest form

The same logic conjures a greatest conceivable island, a perfect pizza, anything — which is absurd. So the form is broken.

Reply

Islands have no intrinsic maximum — there is always room for one more palm tree — and their great-making features are contingent, not necessary. Maximal greatness applies only to a being with a real upper bound on its essential properties, including necessary existence. An island cannot have necessary existence, so the parody does not generalise. Whether that reply fully succeeds is contested, but the disanalogy between contingent and necessary beings is genuine.

"Existence is not a predicate" Hard

Strongest form

Kant argued that existence adds nothing to a concept. Exists is not a great-making property, so the argument equivocates.

Reply

Here we should concede openly: against any form that treats bare existence as one more perfection, Kant’s objection has real force, and many philosophers judge it decisive — though whether it touches Anselm’s own reasoning is itself disputed. That is why the modal version shifts the ground to necessary existence as a modal property, though whether it fully escapes Kant is itself debated.

"Run it in reverse for God’s impossibility" Hard

Strongest form

It is possible that no maximally great being exists. By parallel reasoning, then it exists in no world — so God is impossible. Symmetry sinks the argument.

Reply

This is the strongest objection, and it shows the argument cannot coerce a skeptic — it is only as strong as the possibility premise. But the two premises are not perfectly symmetric: maximal greatness is a positive, coherent concept whose impossibility would need a hidden contradiction no one has produced. In our judgment that tilts the scales toward the possibility premise; it does not compel. And note what the mirror costs the atheist: he must claim not merely that God happens not to exist, but that God cannot — a far heavier burden than atheism usually admits.

"You are defining God into existence" Common

Strongest form

You cannot conjure a real thing from a definition. This is a verbal trick.

Reply

The argument does not define God as existing; it argues that if a necessary being is even possible, the modal logic entails its actuality — a substantive logical result, not a stipulation. The real action is the possibility premise, which is an open metaphysical claim, not a definition.

"Possible just means I can imagine it" Trickier

Strongest form

Epistemic possibility (for all I know) is not metaphysical possibility (true in some world). Imagining God does not make him metaphysically possible.

Reply

Agreed — the premise needs metaphysical possibility, which is exactly why the argument is modest and does not claim to compel everyone. Mere conceivability is a feeble guide, so we claim less: maximal greatness is a positive, coherent concept whose impossibility would need a hidden contradiction no one has produced. That tilts the scales; it does not compel.

"It only gives a bare perfect being" Trickier

Strongest form

Even if sound, the conclusion is an abstract maximally great being, not the Trinity or the God of the Bible.

Reply

Even granted in full, the argument reaches a maximally great being — necessary, and perfect in power, knowledge, and goodness — but it does not, and cannot, reach the specifically Christian God by thought alone. That this necessary and perfect being is Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, and that the Son was made flesh in Jesus of Nazareth and raised from the dead, is not something the bare concept of maximal greatness yields; classical Christianity holds it to be the same God, but one known as triune, and as incarnate in the Son, only through revelation and the historical case for the resurrection.

Touch the originals

Where this comes from — primary sources, not our summary

Anselm of Canterbury11th c. · Proslogion
The original argument: God as that than which nothing greater can be conceived, who must therefore exist in reality, not only in the mind.
Immanuel Kant18th c. · Critique of Pure Reason
Source of the famous objection that existence is not a predicate — which the modal version is built to evade.
Alvin Plantingacontemporary · The Nature of Necessity
Recast the argument with possible worlds and necessary existence, making it valid and locating its whole weight on the possibility premise — while claiming only rational acceptability, not proof.
René Descartes17th c. · Meditations
The Fifth Meditation’s version — “existence is a perfection” — the form Kant’s objection targets, and the reason the modern argument shifts the ground to necessary existence.
The honest state of the scholarship

Where the argument really stands

The validity of the modal version is not seriously disputed; the inference is valid in S5. The entire debate has migrated to the possibility premise, and there it has largely stalled. Critics — Mackie, Graham Oppy (a leading contemporary critic of natural theology), and others — maintain that no non-question-begging reason has been given to prefer “possibly God exists” over “possibly God does not exist,” leaving the argument inert as a tool of persuasion. Defenders such as Plantinga, and in a formalized key the work surrounding Kurt Gödel’s modal proof, reply that the concept of maximal greatness is coherent and its denial unproven.

The real fork: whether the possibility premise is more reasonable than its mirror. It is fair to call that contested, with no consensus in sight — and many theists themselves are unpersuaded that the argument adds anything to what other arguments and experience already supply.

The bounded verdict: the ontological argument does not prove God’s existence to a neutral inquirer, and its foremost modern defender never claimed it does. What survives is genuine and not nothing: if a maximally great being is so much as possible, then it actually and necessarily exists — there is no coherent middle in which God is a merely contingent or merely possible being — and theistic belief can be held rationally. Even granted in full it reaches a maximally great being, not the triune God by thought alone. Anselm’s Proslogion is, tellingly, a prayer — “faith seeking understanding,” not faith manufactured from a syllogism. Held as rational acceptability, one strand in a cumulative case that rests, above all, on the historical evidence for the resurrection.

    Gold gate · the unseen objection

    One you weren’t coached on

    Every objection above challenged the possibility premise. Here is the real test: a critic who grants the premise and attacks the logic instead. Reason it out cold, then reveal how the argument handles it. An honest “nailed it” is the first half of Gold.

    ML
    Modal logician
    "Fine — I'll grant your possibility premise for the sake of argument. I just deny your logic. You're applying S5 (whatever is possibly necessary is necessary) to metaphysical modality. That's a substantive, optional commitment, not a law of thought. Drop S5 for metaphysical necessity and your inference from 'possibly necessary' to 'actual' simply collapses — no God required."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    Fair — and the honest defender names this up front rather than being blindsided by it: applying S5 to metaphysical modality is a substantive commitment, not a triviality, and a critic can in principle fight the logic rather than the possibility premise. But two things. First, S5 for metaphysical modality is the standard and well-motivated choice: “necessary” here just means “true in every possible world,” and the characteristic S5 principle — that what is possible or necessary does not itself vary from world to world — is close to what we mean by metaphysical necessity; the critic who rejects it owes an account of a necessity that is itself only contingently necessary. Second, notice what the move concedes: it grants the possibility premise — the one place the argument is usually thought weakest — and retreats to contesting a widely accepted modal logic instead. Either way the argument’s real limit is the one its defender already grants: on Plantinga’s own account it shows theistic belief is rational, not a proof that compels. It never claimed more.
