The Church in History

Three questions that begin where the resurrection accounts end: how a tiny illegal sect became the majority faith of an empire, what its members actually suffered along the way, and what the movement did to the moral imagination of the world. The first two ask whether the usual sceptical stories hold up. The third asks a different kind of question — not whether Christianity is true, which is argued across the rest of this Library, but whether it was good for the world — and it is careful to keep the two apart: that a faith did good is never, by itself, a reason to think it true.

Growth, persecution & the legacy of the faith

01
How Did the Church Take Over the Roman Empire?
From a few hundred followers to millions while the faith was illegal — and why “Constantine did it” is false for the period that matters
📚 Intermediate
Free
In plain English: In the year 30 the followers of a crucified Galilean numbered perhaps a few hundred, with no wealth, no standing, and no army. Two and a half centuries later they were a substantial minority of the Roman Empire — millions strong — and it happened while the faith was illegal, with no political advantage on offer and two imperial attempts to destroy it already spent. Even hostile witnesses record the spread: a Roman governor alarmed by it around AD 112, a pagan satirist mocking it around 165, a bishop’s relief roll of 251 feeding more than 1,500 people from one congregation. That is the remarkable fact to sit with first — and it is why the popular one-word explanation, Constantine, is false for the period that actually matters: the church was already too large to suppress before he arrived. But one caution governs everything: growth is not truth. A movement can spread because it met real needs and still be mistaken; this card explains the spread, not the truth of the claims.
The key points
  1. By about AD 300 Christians were a substantial minority — the published estimates run from roughly 2.5 million (Ehrman) to about 6.3 million (Stark’s model), somewhere between four and ten-and-a-half per cent of the empire. The spread between the figures is the honest measure of what we know.
  2. That growth happened while the faith was illegal, with no political advantage on offer — so it cannot be credited to the state.
  3. The mechanism its own enemies singled out was unglamorous: care of the sick at cost, refusal to expose unwanted infants, mutual aid, and conversion along family and household networks.
  4. “Constantine did it” explains the wrong stretch of the curve: the church was already too large to remove before 312, and two persecutions had failed with the machinery of empire engaged.
  5. None of this shows Christianity is true. It shows the imperial-power story is false, and that the behaviour that drove the growth is attested by hostile witnesses.

One of the sharpest testimonies comes from an enemy. In 362, fifty years into a Christian empire, the emperor Julian — raised Christian, and the last emperor who was not one — set out to restore the old gods and found he could not. He diagnosed why: not that Christianity had better arguments, but that the Christians out-behaved the pagans, caring even for the poor the pagan priesthood neglected. His remedy was to fund a pagan imitation and order his own priesthood to copy them. (The vivid line most often quoted — that “the impious Galilaeans support not only their own poor but ours as well” — is from a letter whose authenticity is disputed; Julian makes the same complaint in the undisputed Fragment of a Letter to a Priest.) That is an emperor holding the entire apparatus of the state, judging that the thing he had to beat was their charity.

Questions people ask
QDoesn’t the fact that it grew so fast show it must be true? No. Growth shows a movement met needs and held loyalty; it says nothing about whether its claims are true. Islam expanded politically far faster (though conversion of the conquered populations took centuries), and Rodney Stark checked his own early-church growth rate against modern Latter-day Saint growth. Any argument of the form “it spread, therefore it is true” proves far too much.
QDidn’t Constantine just hand Christianity the empire? The objection concedes a great deal — everything after 312 is entangled with state power, and the fourth-century majority is not evidence of persuasion. But it explains the wrong period. By 312 the church was already large enough that two attempts to destroy it, under Decius and Diocletian, had failed; Galerius conceded toleration in April 311, a year before the Milvian Bridge. The state gave up first.

Picture the arithmetic. A few hundred people in one corner of one province, and within three centuries a body so large that an empire which had tried twice to stamp it out simply stopped trying. That is a strange thing to have happened, and both the pious and the sceptical accounts of it say more than the evidence permits.

The honest numbers

Nobody counted. Every figure is an estimate, and the most-quoted — Rodney Stark’s roughly six million by AD 300 — is a model, not a measurement, generated by assuming a steady growth rate. Bart Ehrman’s more recent estimate is less than half that. So the responsible thing is a range, not a point: somewhere between about four and ten-and-a-half per cent of the empire, concentrated in cities and unevenly spread. What is not in dispute is the shape — a standing start, and steep sustained growth, while the faith was against the law.

Why the “Constantine” answer misses

The single most important point is a matter of timing. This card does not claim to explain how Christianity became the empire’s majority — that is a fourth-century story, and the state is all over it. It explains how the church became impossible to suppress before the state had any part in it. And the evidence for that period does not depend on any growth model: a Roman governor describing a spreading movement around AD 112, a satirist describing its organised charity around 165, a bishop’s relief roll from 251 listing more than 1,500 dependants supported by one congregation, and two failed imperial persecutions.

The honest limit

Here is where a Christian should be careful, because the temptation is real. None of this is evidence that Jesus rose from the dead. Movements grow for many reasons, and a religion can be admirable in its effects and false in its claims. Anyone who offers the rise of the church as proof of the faith has changed the subject — and handed a reader a confidence that will not survive the first competent objection. What the record does establish is narrower and worth having: the movement grew, at real cost, doing what its founder told it to do, and the people who tried hardest to defeat it said so.

How to explain this simply

Keep two claims apart: that Christianity spread (a historical fact) and that Christianity is true (a separate question settled by other evidence). This card argues only the first, and only for the pre-Constantinian period — that the church became too large to suppress before the state ever helped it.

In conversation: “Suppose I grant that Constantine made Christianity powerful in the fourth century. That still doesn’t explain why it was already too big to stamp out before him — while it was illegal, and two emperors had tried. What did that?”

How many Christians were there? An honest answer

Nobody counted. There is no census of Christians in the Roman Empire, and every figure in every book on the subject is an estimate built from fragments. The most influential attempt is Rodney Stark’s: assume a steady compound rate, anchor it at an endpoint historians roughly agree on and a deliberately conservative starting assumption, and see what falls out. Starting from about 1,000 Christians in the year 40 and growing at 40 per cent per decade, you reach roughly 7,500 by 100, about 218,000 by 200, and about 6.3 million by 300 — something over ten per cent of an empire of perhaps sixty million.

The honest thing to say about that table is that it is a model, not a measurement, and its author said so. The rate is not derived from ancient data; Stark fixes it from his two endpoints and then observes that it is close to the rate the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints has averaged over the past century — a plausibility check on a rate already chosen, not an input to it. That comparison cuts both ways: it makes the figures credible, and it shows an exponential curve of this shape is not unique to Christianity or evidence of anything supernatural.

Two cautions, and why the answer is a range

First, the rate cannot be extended: run at 40 per cent per decade to the year 400 and it produces more Christians than the empire had inhabitants — a fair measure of what a constant-rate model is and is not. Second, the agreement among historians is thinner than it looks. Keith Hopkins, often cited as independent confirmation, is not one: he adopts Stark’s arithmetic as his starting point and states his debt in the article. Bart Ehrman, working the same problem, puts the year-300 figure at roughly two and a half to three million — less than half Stark’s. So the responsible summary is a range: somewhere between about four and ten-and-a-half per cent of the empire. What is not in dispute is the shape — a standing start, and steep sustained growth, while the faith was illegal.

What growth looked like from outside

The most valuable evidence is not Christian, because Christian sources have an obvious interest in reporting success. Around AD 112 Pliny the Younger, governing Bithynia-Pontus, wrote to Trajan that the “contagion of this superstition” had spread not only through the cities but through the villages and countryside, affecting every age and rank and both sexes, with the temples nearly deserted before his crackdown. Half a century later the satirist Lucian — who thought Christians credulous fools — described them mobbing a leader’s prison, bribing the guards, and sending delegates from the cities of Asia at collective expense. And from inside the illegal period comes a hard administrative number: writing about 251, Cornelius of Rome listed his church’s establishment for a colleague — forty-six presbyters, seven deacons, and more than fifteen hundred widows and persons in distress supported by the congregation.

The mechanisms — and an honest rival

Four things recur across the sources. Care of the sick during the great epidemics, at real cost, since nursing raised the carers’ own exposure and Dionysius of Alexandria says they died of it. Refusal of infant exposure — the Didache forbids it in the same breath as murder. Conversion along family, household and trade networks rather than public preaching to strangers. And an unusually socially mixed membership, not rationed by status. None is unique in kind: Roman guilds and Jewish communities had mutual aid, and the cults of Isis and Mithras spread by network too. The most serious dissenting reconstruction, Ramsay MacMullen’s, credits public demonstrations of power — healing and exorcism — over charity; it is a rival mechanism, not a rival to the thesis, because whatever else it was, it was not imperial power.

The Constantine objection, conceded and answered

The concession is substantial: everything after 312 is entangled with state power, and the church’s growth after 380 with the legal coercion of non-Nicene worship. The steepest part of the curve in absolute numbers is the fourth century, and anyone who cites the fourth-century majority as evidence of persuasive power is trading on a period when the thumb was on the scale. But the answer is about timing, and it does not rest on the growth model. Pliny’s letter, Lucian’s satire and Cornelius’s relief roll all sit before Constantine; and the state’s own two attempts to bring Christians to heel — Decius’s universal sacrifice order of 250, which required all inhabitants to sacrifice rather than targeting Christians by name, and Diocletian’s persecution from 303 — had already failed, with Galerius conceding toleration in April 311, a year before the Milvian Bridge. The state gave up first. Whatever Constantine did, he did it to a church two emperors could not remove.

A hostile witness — and the honest bound

One of the sharpest testimonies comes from an enemy holding the whole apparatus of the state. In 362 the emperor Julian, raised Christian and the last emperor who was not one, set out to restore the old gods and could not. He is not evidence about what caused the earlier growth; he is evidence of something else worth having — that an emperor with the whole machinery of state judged Christian charity the thing he had to beat. He blamed pagan decline on the priesthood’s neglect of the poor and on Christian care for strangers, and funded a pagan imitation of it (the undisputed Fragment of a Letter to a Priest; the vivid line that the “impious Galilaeans support not only their own poor but ours as well” comes from a letter whose authenticity is disputed). Yet none of this is evidence that Jesus rose. Movements grow for many reasons, and a religion can be admirable in its effects and false in its claims. What the record establishes is narrower and worth having: the imperial-power story is false, and the behaviour that drove the growth is attested by the very people who tried hardest to defeat it.

What the Early Church Actually Suffered
Trajan to Diocletian: a real, recurring, law-backed capital liability — proven from Rome’s own records and the church’s own — behind a popular picture that is genuinely inflated in its familiar form
📚 Intermediate
Free
In plain English: Yes — and it was the law of Rome that did it. For the first three centuries, being a Christian was a capital charge you could be executed for. From about AD 112 a Roman emperor put it in writing that a Christian who was accused and would not recant was to be put to death; in 250 the emperor Decius ordered every inhabitant of the empire to sacrifice to the gods and carry a certificate proving it — and dozens of those certificates still survive on papyrus today. The surest proof is the church’s own records: for fifty years afterwards it tore itself apart over the mass of Christians who had caved under the pressure — you do not build that unless the danger was real and widespread. Most of this comes from hostile Roman officials and the church’s own embarrassed records, not from Christian storytellers. The popular “lions in the Colosseum, three centuries hiding in catacombs” picture is overblown, and it is worth conceding that before a sceptic forces it — but strip the Hollywood layer away and this hard, documented core is untouched. One honest limit governs it all: martyrdom proves the martyrs were sincere, never that what they believed was true.
The key points
  1. Being a Christian was a capital charge you could be executed for on accusation. Trajan’s rescript (c. AD 112) set the shape and it held for a century and a half: Christians were not to be hunted down, but those accused and unrepentant were put to death — a standing legal liability any neighbour could turn lethal.
  2. The first empire-wide measure, under Decius in 250, forced everyone to sacrifice and obtain a certificate — and between forty and fifty of those certificates survive on papyrus, naming the people who complied. This is physical evidence, not pious legend.
  3. The strongest evidence is the church’s own embarrassment: fifty years of bitter disputes over the mass of Christians who complied. You do not build a fifty-year discipline for apostates unless the pressure was real — and the sheer number of them proves it was widespread.
  4. Much of this comes from hostile Roman officials and the church’s own embarrassed records, not from Christian storytellers — which is exactly why it is so hard to wave away.
  5. Only then the honest qualifiers: the popular picture is inflated (the tidy “ten great persecutions” scheme is a later construction, and Candida Moss is largely right about that), and martyrdom proves the martyrs’ sincerity, not the truth of what they believed. Neither dents the documented core.

The best evidence is not a martyr story but an administrative headache. After the persecutions, the churches tore themselves apart for decades over the lapsi — the lapsed — those who had sacrificed, or bribed their way to a certificate, or fled. That controversy proves two things at once, in opposite directions. A church does not build a fifty-year discipline for apostates unless there was real pressure and real apostates — so the persecution was genuine. And the lapsed were a mass phenomenon, not a scattering of cowards — so it was survivable, and many did not endure it. The early church was not made of a stronger grade of person; it was made of people who mostly wanted to live, and then had to work out how to forgive the ones who had.

Questions people ask
QWasn’t the persecution mostly a myth? No — exaggerated in popular retellings, yes; a myth, no. What even Candida Moss grants still refutes the flatter claim: Trajan’s rescript authorising execution for the name, the Decian sacrifice order whose certificates survive, and Diocletian’s Great Persecution. She disputes Nero and disputes Rome’s intent; grant both and a recurring, state-backed capital liability remains.
QDoesn’t the martyrs’ willingness to die prove Christianity is true? No — and the card says so at the top and the bottom. Martyrdom establishes sincerity, not truth; people die for false beliefs constantly. What dying rules out is deliberate fraud, and only for those in a position to know whether they were lying — a narrower argument that concerns the apostles specifically, made in the companion essay on the disciples’ belief.

Start with what is not in doubt, because it is written in Rome’s own hand: for the first three centuries, being a Christian carried a real and recurring risk of execution, and the state is the one that imposed it. This is not a Christian legend about heroic martyrs — it is Roman administrative record, imperial law, and surviving physical documents.

What the record shows

Three things carry the weight. Trajan’s rescript to Pliny, around AD 112, set imperial policy for a century and a half: Christians were not to be sought out, but any who were accused and refused to recant were to be executed — so being a Christian was a capital charge any neighbour could trigger. Then in 250 the emperor Decius required every inhabitant of the empire to sacrifice and obtain a libellus, a certificate proving it — and between forty and fifty of those certificates survive on Egyptian papyrus to this day. And the church kept its own record of the cost: for fifty years after the persecutions it tore itself apart over the lapsi, the mass of Christians who had sacrificed or bought their way out. You do not build a fifty-year discipline for apostates unless the pressure was real, and the number who caved proves it was widespread. Most of this evidence is Roman and hostile, not Christian and self-serving — which is exactly what makes it hard to dismiss.

The popular picture, honestly

Now the honest correction, offered before a sceptic can spring it. The Hollywood version — lions in the Colosseum, an empire hunting Christians without pause for three centuries, believers permanently in the catacombs — is badly out of proportion. The historian Candida Moss has pressed this case hardest, and most of it should simply be granted: the tidy scheme of “ten persecutions” is a later invention, and active hunting was intermittent and local. But conceding the exaggeration does not touch the core above — it strips away a myth and leaves the documented reality standing. (Tacitus on Nero, AD 64, is a genuinely contested case, and nothing here rests on it.)

The honest limit

Two limits keep the argument sound. Martyrdom proves sincerity, not truth — it rules out deliberate fraud, and nothing more; people die for false beliefs too. And the record establishes cost, not distinctiveness: Rome suppressed other groups as well, so “they were persecuted” does not by itself set Christianity apart. Why this movement grew under that cost when others did not is a separate question, taken up in the companion card on the church’s growth.

How to explain this simply

Lead with the hard evidence, not the myth: being a Christian was a capital charge on accusation (Trajan’s rescript), the Decian sacrifice certificates survive on papyrus, and the church’s own fifty-year fight over those who complied proves the pressure was real and widespread. Only then concede that the popular “lions everywhere” picture is overblown — the concession costs nothing once the documented core is in place. And hold the bound: the martyrs’ deaths prove they were sincere, not that they were right.

In conversation: “We have the actual sacrifice certificates people had to buy to prove they had renounced Christ, and fifty years of church records agonising over the ones who caved. You don’t build that unless the danger was real. The ‘lions everywhere’ version is overblown, sure — but the documented core isn’t.”

Trajan’s rescript: the legal shape of the whole period

The single most important document is the exchange between Pliny and Trajan around AD 112. Trajan’s reply set the policy that held for a century and a half: Christians are not to be sought out (conquirendi non sunt) and anonymous denunciations are to be disregarded, but those properly accused and unrepentant are to be executed. It cuts two ways at once. In the church’s favour, barring anonymous accusation meant an accuser had to give his name and risk a charge of malicious prosecution — a real deterrent, and several historians read the rescript as substantially moderating. Against, the liability never went away: it was a standing exposure any neighbour, creditor or rival could turn lethal. A law enforced only when someone informs on you is not a mild law — it makes every relationship a potential exposure.

Decius, and the certificates that survive

The first genuinely empire-wide measure came under Decius in 250: all inhabitants — not Christians specifically — had to sacrifice and obtain a certificate, a libellus, before a commission. What makes this decisive is that the certificates survive: between forty and fifty papyrus slips, naming the petitioner and attesting that he has sacrificed. They come entirely from Egypt, most from a single Fayum village, so what they establish beyond argument is that the requirement was issued, administered and generating paperwork ordinary people had to obtain; that the order was empire-wide is a secure inference from the decree’s terms together with Cyprian at Carthage, Dionysius at Alexandria, and the Pionius material at Smyrna.

The church’s own embarrassment — the best evidence

The strongest evidence is not a martyr story but an administrative headache. For fifty years after the persecutions the churches tore themselves apart over the lapsi — those who had sacrificed, bought a certificate, or fled — and over the traditores, clergy who had handed over the scriptures. This proves two things at once. A church does not build a fifty-year discipline for apostates unless there was real pressure and real apostates — so the persecution was genuine. And the lapsed were a mass phenomenon, not a scattering of cowards — so it was survivable, and many did not endure it. The early church was not made of a stronger grade of person; where believers did stand firm, the tradition itself credited grace rather than their own reserves (Philippians 1:29), honouring the confessors as recipients rather than heroes.

The strongest challenge, steelmanned: Moss’s case

That is the documented core. The most forceful modern challenge to the larger picture is Candida Moss’s The Myth of Persecution, and it deserves to be put at its strongest rather than waved at. She argues, first, that the familiar scheme of “ten great persecutions” is a later Christian construction, not a Roman policy. Second, that for most of the first three centuries Christians were not being pursued at all — on her reckoning imperial initiatives killed Christians for fewer than ten years in total across the whole period. Third, that the martyr acts are largely later compositions, edited and in some cases invented. Fourth — the claim that gives the book its title — that what happened was mostly prosecution under general law rather than persecution of a belief. And fifth, that the myth has since been misused. The first two are not really contested among specialists, and this case grants them freely; the third is mainstream in principle since Delehaye, though Moss presses it further than many who share it; the fourth is answered below; the fifth is about modern churches and is not adjudicated here. Note what none of this touches: Trajan’s rescript, the surviving libelli, and the lapsi controversy all stand.

Nero, AD 64 — the contested exhibit

The earliest episode is reported not by a believer but by Tacitus, who blames Nero for scapegoating “a class hated for their abominations” after the fire of Rome and describes their deaths in lurid detail. But whether this was a persecution of a distinct, identifiable group is a live scholarly dispute: Brent Shaw has argued the Tacitean evidence will not bear the weight placed on it — that Christians were not yet a distinct target in 64 — and Christopher Jones has replied; the exchange is unresolved, and Moss herself is sharpest here. Nothing in the case depends on Nero, and it is set out as the contested item it is. What the passage supports, if Tacitus is credited, is that Christians could be killed for belonging to the group — not that a legal category of “the name” yet existed.

The prosecution-vs-persecution answer

Grant Moss’s central point — that Roman magistrates thought of themselves as enforcing public order rather than suppressing a religion. It does not dissolve the cost. A defendant executed for refusing to sacrifice, where sacrificing would have ended the matter on the spot, still faces a religiously specific test; and Trajan’s rescript makes recantation the whole hinge of the procedure, which is precisely what a charge of ordinary criminality would not do. The liability was real whatever the magistrate believed he was doing.

The objection this card cannot answer

One challenge the record itself invites, and which this case does not defeat: Rome also suppressed the Bacchanalia, banned the druidic religion, expelled Jews from the city, and issued a rescript against the Manichaeans. Christians were not uniquely targeted, so “a group was periodically illegal in Rome” is not by itself evidence about that group. The honest reply is a concession: what the record establishes is cost, not distinctiveness. Why this movement grew under that cost when others did not is a different question, taken up in the companion card on the church’s growth — not settled by the persecutions.

Did Christianity Change the World?
The case that universal human dignity is a traceable inheritance — with the full ledger of the church’s crimes, and why none of it proves the faith true
🎓 Advanced
Free
In plain English: You probably believe every human being is of equal worth, and that the weak have a claim on the strong simply by being human. You probably think that is obvious. It is not: for most of recorded history almost no society was built on it, and the civilisations that gave us Western philosophy ran on slavery and the exposure of unwanted infants. The conviction that overturned that has a traceable history — a Jewish doctrine, the image of God, carried by Christianity into the Gentile world and fought out over centuries into the care of the abandoned and the sick, the abolition of slavery, and the very idea of universal human dignity. That is a real inheritance, and this card makes the case for it. It is also scrupulous about one thing above all: none of this is evidence that Christianity is true. A belief system can be enormously beneficial and entirely false; Christianity’s claim on anyone is that it is true, not merely that it did good.
The key points
  1. The conviction that every person has equal, inviolable worth is not self-evident and was not the default of the ancient world — it has a history.
  2. That history runs through a Jewish doctrine (the image of God) carried by Christianity into the Gentile world and welded to an unlikely centre: God the Son, executed as a criminal.
  3. The claim is bounded: Christianity was a necessary ingredient in a particular combination, not a sufficient cause — which is why the decisive changes came late and unevenly.
  4. The ledger against is real and not incidental — Crusades, inquisitions, the slave-owners’ Bible, colonial mission, anti-Judaism, apartheid, the abuse crisis — and the genealogy must never be used to soften any of it.
  5. And the whole thing proves nothing about the faith’s truth. If Christianity were false, its social benefits would be a reason to be grateful for a mistake, not a reason to believe it.

The abolition of slavery shows both halves of the honest case at once. The organised British campaign from 1787 drew heavily on Christian conviction — Quakers, Clarkson, the Clapham circle around Wilberforce. But when the isolated proof-texts were read on their own, the pro-slavery side had the easier of them, and its most sophisticated defences were also Christian; the American conflict became, in Mark Noll’s account, a crisis of biblical interpretation itself. What the pro-slavery reading did not have was the whole: the abolitionists read from creation in God’s image, from the exodus, from the condemnation of the man-stealer, and argued the canon read entire runs against an institution its parts merely regulate. Abolition was a Christian achievement that had to be won against Christians.

Questions people ask
QDoesn’t all the good Christianity did prove it’s true? No, and this card refuses that inference explicitly. Social benefit is not a truth-claim about God; if the faith were false its benefits would be a reason to be grateful for a mistake, not to believe it. The case for its truth rests on the historical and philosophical arguments made elsewhere in this Library.
QWhat about the Crusades, the inquisitions, slavery, and the abuse crisis? They are set out in full here, not managed — and with the harder point added: the sophisticated defences of slavery were themselves Christian. The genealogical argument is never used as mitigation. That the church supplied a moral standard does not shorten what that standard measures; being handed it aggravates the failure to keep it.
QIsn’t it really Enlightenment thinking, or the Stoics, that gave us human rights? Both are engaged. The churches were often on the wrong side of the Enlightenment’s fights — granted — but its moral premises were not self-generated. The Stoics reasoned about a common humanity, but theirs was a universalism of reason that grades, and it produced no movement to end slavery. Samuel Moyn presses the sharpest counter — that the long genealogies are retrojection — and that is left standing, not pretend-refuted; it tells against the thesis in its strong form, not the bounded genealogical claim this card actually makes.

Start with something you almost certainly believe: that a person’s value does not depend on their usefulness, their birth, or their strength. It feels like plain moral sense. It is not. For most of recorded history almost no one built a society on it, and the civilisations that gave us Western philosophy did not.

The claim — and what it is not

The defensible claim is genealogical: this conviction has a traceable pedigree, in which Judaism is the root and Christianity a major part of the route into Western law and sentiment. It is not the claim that Christianity made the world nicer, and above all it is not evidence that Christianity is true. That firewall has to stand, because the moment “look what it did for the world” becomes “therefore it is true,” the argument has committed a plain error a thoughtful sceptic will name at once.

The ledger has to be honest

An account that stopped at the good would be advertising, not history. The Crusades, the inquisitions, the witch trials, colonial forced conversion, centuries of anti-Judaism, the theological defence of apartheid, the abuse crisis — each is a settled matter of record, cutting across every tradition. And the genealogy must never soften them: that the church supplied the ruler does not shorten what the ruler measures, and being handed a standard aggravates the failure to keep it rather than excusing it.

Where it lands

So the verdict runs in both directions. It shows a widespread modern assumption — that basic moral equality is just what a decent person naturally thinks — is false; the conviction was won late, partially, and against the grain. It does not show that Christians have been good, and it does not show the faith is true. The honest thing a Christian can say is not that the church has been better than others, but that it was handed something better than itself.

Separate two questions: is Christianity true (argued elsewhere) and was it good for the world (argued here). The second never establishes the first. The good-for-the-world case is itself bounded — a claim about where a moral standard came from, made honestly alongside the full record of the church’s failures to keep it.

In conversation: “I’m not going to argue Christianity is true because it did good — that would be a bad argument. I’m saying the equality you take for granted isn’t obvious or ancient; it has a history, and a lot of that history is Jewish and Christian. Where do you think it came from?”

The thesis, and its careful form

Tom Holland’s Dominion is the most-discussed recent statement of the case: the West received its instinct for universal dignity from a particular history rather than deducing it from reason, and the most unlikely thing in that history — a God executed by torture as a criminal — is at its root. Holland’s route to it was not a believer’s: the more closely he studied Greece and Rome, the less he found he shared their moral universe. Roman power rested on chattel slavery of an ordinariness that has few parallels; crucifixion was engineered to strip a human being of dignity in public. Holland is a popular historian, not a specialist in every period he crosses, and the strong causal claim is genuinely debated among historians even where the underlying facts are not.

The image of God, carried to a crucified God

Two convictions do most of the work, and the first is not Christian in origin. That every human being bears the image of God (Genesis 1:27) is a Jewish doctrine, developed in Jewish scripture long before and independently of the church; Judaism’s account of universal worth stands on its own feet, and this case does not claim it. What Christianity did was carry that Jewish conviction into the Gentile world at scale and weld it to a second claim Judaism does not make — that God himself, in the person of the Son, had been executed by the method Rome reserved for slaves. Paul knew how it sounded, calling it a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles. A civilisation that came to worship a crucified man as God had, in principle, lost the ability to say that the humiliated are contemptible — though in practice it went on saying so for centuries, which is the ledger.

Care of the sick — the hospital as an institution

The cleanest concrete example is also one where the precise claim is stronger than the usual apologetic overstatement. Christians did not invent medicine, and the ancient world had healing shrines and military infirmaries; the emperor Julian, trying to compete, complained that they cared even for the poor who were not their own. What has no clear ancient precedent is the hospital as an institution — a permanent, charitably funded foundation open to the sick poor as such and staffed to care for them. Basil of Caesarea’s complex outside that city, established around 372, is the standard early example. The point is contested at the edges — some historians urge caution about how sharp and how early the break was — but institutional priority in the charitable care of the sick poor is what survives the debate.

Abolition: the case in miniature

Slavery was near-universal and defended by Aristotle as natural; its abolition was not. The organised British campaign from 1787 drew heavily on Christian conviction — Quakers, Clarkson, the Clapham circle around Wilberforce. But the causation is disputed, and the best modern account is not providential: Christopher Leslie Brown argues antislavery long predated the campaign and that what changed in the 1780s was political. And the honest sentence is the next one: the most sophisticated defences of slavery were also Christian, and when the isolated proof-texts were read on their own the pro-slavery side had the easier of them — a crisis, in Mark Noll’s account, of biblical interpretation itself. What that reading lacked was the whole: the abolitionists read from creation in God’s image, from the exodus, from the condemnation of the man-stealer, and argued the canon read entire runs against an institution its parts merely regulate. Abolition was a Christian achievement won against Christians.

The ledger against — and why the genealogy cannot soften it

An account that stopped at the good would be advertising, not history. The record includes the Rhineland massacres of 1096 and the sack of Jerusalem in 1099, the inquisitions and the burning of heretics, the witch trials in Protestant and Catholic territories alike, the expulsion of the Jews from Spain and centuries of Christian anti-Judaism, colonial forced conversion, the theological defence of apartheid, and the abuse of children by clergy and its concealment. It cuts across every tradition. And there is a move that must be refused: one could observe that the standard by which we condemn these things is itself substantially a Christian inheritance, and let that function as mitigation. It does not. Where a standard came from and whether it was obeyed are logically independent — and being handed the standard is an aggravating circumstance for the people who broke it, not a mitigating one.

The strongest objection: it took eighteen centuries

If universal dignity was in the deposit from the first century, why did abolition and toleration wait until the eighteenth, and arrive then only in north-western Europe, when Byzantine, Russian, Ethiopian and Iberian-American Christendoms produced none of it? The answer is the bounded one the whole case rests on: Christianity was a necessary ingredient in a particular combination, not a sufficient cause. A standard held and disobeyed for centuries is evidence about human beings, not about where the standard came from — and that the changes came late, and in one place, shows Christianity by itself did not produce them, not that they would have come without it.

The counter-genealogies, engaged

Two counter-accounts press from opposite directions. The classical one: the Stoics and Roman jurists reasoned about a common humanity — Seneca on the shared humanity of slaves, Ulpian’s principle that by natural law all are equal. But theirs was a universalism of reason, which grades, where the image of God does not; and it built no institution and freed no one. The modern one credits toleration and rights to Enlightenment thinkers who fought clerical power, and the churches were often on the wrong side — granted — but the Enlightenment’s moral premises were not self-generated. The sharpest version cuts the other way: Samuel Moyn argues human rights are a twentieth-century phenomenon and the long genealogies are retrojection. That is a serious position, it is not refuted here, and a reader weighing this case should weigh it too.

The bound, restated

None of this is offered as evidence that Christianity is true, and none of it shows Christians have been good — the ledger is the answer to that. What it shows is that a moral assumption modern people treat as self-evident is a specific inheritance with a traceable history, in which Judaism is the root and Christianity a major part of the route — contested in its strong forms, defensible in its careful one. The honest thing a Christian can say is not that the church has been good, but that it was handed something better than itself.
