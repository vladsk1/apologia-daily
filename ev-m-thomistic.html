The Thomistic Cosmological Argument

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and keep the one key distinction clear under pressure.

The argument in brief

Thomas Aquinas's cosmological argument is often confused with the Kalam, but it makes a very different point. It is not about who lit the fuse at the beginning of time; it is about what holds everything in being right now. Aquinas noticed that things in the world are constantly being changed, moved, and kept in existence by something other than themselves. A coffee cup rests on a table, which rests on the floor, which rests on the earth — and the support has to be supplied at every instant, not just once in the past.

The key distinction is between a per se series (causes acting simultaneously, here and now) and a per accidens series (causes strung out over time, like fathers begetting sons). A per se series of sustainers cannot stretch back to infinity, because every member is borrowing its causal power from a prior member; remove a first source and there is nothing to borrow from. So there must be a being that does not derive its existence or activity from anything else — pure actuality, unchanging, self-existent. "And this," says Aquinas, "everyone understands to be God."

Honestly, the argument requires effort to follow, and it leans on a classical metaphysics of act and potency that not everyone shares. But it has the striking feature that it works even if the universe is eternal — the question is not when things began, but why they continue to be. Note the scope: this argument reaches a purely actual First Cause sustaining the world — not yet the Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Identifying that being with the God revealed in Christ is the work of the other arguments, of revelation, and of the historical evidence for the resurrection. We judge that the argument succeeds where it matters most: rightly read, the First and Second Ways survive their famous objections and give strong reason to believe that a purely actual cause holds the world in being now. That is a serious, still-living case — not a knockdown proof — held with confidence rather than certainty.

Before you start · key terms

The argument, formally

Three lines — and the crucial idea is hidden in the word essentially.

P1

Some things are being sustained in existence right now — changed, moved, kept in being by something else.

P2

An essentially ordered (here-and-now) series of sustaining causes cannot regress to infinity — it needs a first sustainer.

∴ C

So there is a purely actual, unchanging First Cause sustaining all things now — "and this everyone understands to be God."

This is not the Kalam. It is not about a beginning in the past, but about what holds things in being right now — even if the universe were eternal. The key distinction is per se versus per accidens causation; miss it and every objection looks stronger than it is.

Defending Premise 1

Some things are being sustained in existence right now — changed, moved, kept in being by something else.

Precision check: motus is not merely local motion (change of place) but any actualization of a potential — change of quality, quantity, substance. And the causal series Aquinas has in mind does not stretch backward in time to a first event.

P1 — things are being sustained right now Observed, here and now

The cup on the table on the floor; the stone moved by the stick moved by the hand. Each is, at this instant, having a potential actualized by something already actual — cold water does not heat itself; the flame does. “Nothing can be reduced from potentiality to actuality, except by something in a state of actuality.”

The stone moves because the stick moves it; the stick moves because the hand moves it. Remove the hand and the whole sequence collapses at once — not eventually, but now. Aquinas chose exactly this image to anchor the first and most famous of his arguments for God: “the staff moves only because it is put in motion by the hand.”

Why whatever changes is changed by another Metaphysical principle · contested

Aquinas’s answer rests on Aristotle’s distinction between potency and act. A thing changes when a potential it has becomes actual — cold water becomes hot. But a mere potential cannot actualize itself; the cold water does not heat itself. Something already actual (the flame) must do it. The Thomistic response is that the principle “a potential cannot actualize itself” is not an empirical generalization like “fire is hot” but a metaphysical truth about the nature of potency and act.

Honest limit: a fully adequate defence requires defending an entire realist metaphysics against empiricism and transcendental idealism — more than any essay can do. Whether the replies to Hume and Kant below fully succeed is contested, and honesty requires saying so.

Objection — Newton’s inertia Hardest

Aquinas’s premise “whatever is moved is moved by another” looks like exactly what Newtonian physics overturned. By the principle of inertia, a body in uniform motion stays in motion with no mover at all. Anthony Kenny, in his skeptical study The Five Ways (1969), pressed the point hard, concluding that the First Way fails in light of modern dynamics, which dissolves the medieval premise that sustained motion needs a sustaining mover. If the paradigm Way rests on dead physics, that is a grave problem.

Reply

The reply turns on what “motion” means. For Aristotle and Aquinas, motus is not merely local motion (change of place) but any actualization of a potential — change of quality, quantity, substance. Inertia concerns only local motion, and — on this view — even there it does not touch the deeper principle. Inertia, the Thomist replies, describes that bodies persist in motion; it does not explain what grounds the existence of that motion, or of the body, or of the law itself, from moment to moment. The Thomist relocates the argument from “what keeps the cannonball moving?” to “what sustains in being, right now, every actualized potential — including the persistence the law describes?” Thomists add a second point: Newtonian physics itself treats uniform motion as a state, not a change, so inertia is no counterexample to the claim that change requires a changer. The reply is not a dodge: it reflects what Aquinas meant by change all along, and in our judgment it answers the objection. What remains open is the deeper question of potency and act, not whether Newton refuted Aquinas.

Objection — Hume and Kant

David Hume argued that causation is not a rationally intuited necessary connection but a habit of mind: we see one billiard ball strike another and infer a “power,” but we observe only constant conjunction. If so, the causal principle behind the Ways loses its self-evidence. Immanuel Kant, in the Critique of Pure Reason (1781), pressed a different line: the cosmological argument, in inferring from a necessary being to the supremely real being, secretly relies on the ontological argument, which Kant thought already refuted — and in any case the categories of cause and necessity apply only within possible experience, not beyond it.

Reply

These are formidable, and a fully adequate reply requires defending an entire realist metaphysics against empiricism and transcendental idealism — more than any essay can do. The Thomistic response is that Hume’s analysis describes how we come to know causal connections without showing there are none, and that the principle “a potential cannot actualize itself” is not an empirical generalization like “fire is hot” but a metaphysical truth about the nature of potency and act. Against Kant, Thomists deny that the argument depends on the ontological proof at all: it argues from features of the world to their required cause, not from a concept to existence. Against Kant’s second charge, Thomists answer that the claim that causal concepts cannot reach beyond possible experience is itself a claim about what lies beyond experience. Whether these replies fully succeed is contested, and honesty requires saying so; but neither Hume nor Kant shows that a potential can actualize itself, and that is the premise the First Way needs.

The room: Aquinas (the Summa) builds on Aristotle’s act/potency and unmoved mover. Edward Feser, a leading recent defender, replies that inertia describes but does not explain; Anthony Kenny presses it, concluding the proofs fail; Hume and Kant press the causal principle itself. There is no scholarly consensus that the arguments succeed, nor that they fail.
Defending Premise 2

An essentially ordered (here-and-now) series of sustaining causes cannot regress to infinity — it needs a first sustainer.

The whole case turns on the per se / per accidens distinction in P2. Get that right and nearly every objection answers itself; the sharpest ones are in the bank below. A per accidens series could, for all philosophy shows, regress forever — which is precisely why Aquinas conceded the universe might be eternal.

The two kinds of series The key distinction

A series ordered per accidens (“accidentally ordered”) runs through time: a father begets a son, who begets a grandson. Each member, once it acts, no longer needs its predecessor. Abraham can be long dead while Isaac fathers Jacob.

A series ordered per se (“essentially ordered”) operates simultaneously, here and now. The stone’s motion depends, at this very instant, on the stick, which depends on the hand. The later members have no causal power of their own; they move only insofar as something is moving them right now. Take away the first member and the entire series ceases instantly.

P2 — a per se series needs a first sustainer The load-bearing premise

In a per se series the later members are mere instruments with only borrowed power — like a paintbrush moving a paintbrush moving a paintbrush, with no hand. As Edward Feser, a leading recent defender, puts it, in such a series the intermediate causes are mere instruments; their causal power is “borrowed.” A series of borrowers with no one who owns the power outright explains nothing. Infinitely many powerless instruments still lift nothing, so the power must originate in a first, underived member acting now. (A per accidens series, by contrast, can be infinite — which is exactly why Aquinas allowed that the universe might be eternal.)

Honest narrowing: this is the premise everything turns on. Whether a per se regress must terminate in a single first member — rather than, say, a necessary initial state of the universe — is exactly what the sharpest critics contest (the Gold gate below takes it up). And note the scope: because the per accidens series may run forever, the argument neither needs nor claims a beginning in time. It is not the Kalam.

No beginning in time is needed Scope · not the Kalam

The causal series Aquinas has in mind does not stretch backward in time to a first event. Aquinas, remarkably, did not think reason could prove the universe began at all. In his treatise De aeternitate mundi (c. 1271) and in the Summa itself, he held that the world’s beginning is “an object of faith, but not of demonstration or science” (ST I, q. 46, a. 2). This put him in open disagreement with his Franciscan contemporary Bonaventure, who insisted the world’s temporal beginning could be philosophically proven. So the Five Ways cannot depend on a beginning: they point to a sustainer at the foundation of present reality, not a starter long ago.

What the first member is — and what it is not Scope · not yet the triune God

Trace the present chain of actualizers, and you reach something that actualizes without itself being actualized — something with no unrealized potential, pure actuality (actus purus). That, Aquinas argues across the following questions, must be one, immaterial, unchanging, and the source of all being.

Honest limit: the arguments, if sound, establish a purely actual, necessary, sustaining first cause of present reality. They do not, by themselves, establish that this cause is good, personal, triune, or the God of Abraham; that is the work of the subsequent questions of the Summa and of revelation. That is not yet the whole Christian faith, which rests on revelation and on the historical evidence for the resurrection of Jesus. Nor do they prove the universe began in time — Aquinas insisted they don’t, and didn’t need to.

Objection — “Why cannot the series be infinite?”

Maybe there is just an infinite regress of sustaining causes, with no first member at all.

Reply

That confuses the two series. An accidentally ordered series could be infinite — Aquinas grants it. But an essentially ordered, here-and-now series cannot, because every later member is a mere instrument with no power of its own. Infinitely many powerless instruments still lift nothing — the power must originate in a first, underived source.

The room: Aquinas (the Summa) builds on Aristotle’s act/potency and unmoved mover. A genuine revival — Edward Feser, David Oderberg — has put rigorous versions back into peer-reviewed debate, met by equally rigorous critics, above all Graham Oppy and (on the inertia objection) Anthony Kenny. There is no scholarly consensus that the arguments succeed, nor that they fail. The First and Second Ways carry the weight; the Third needs repair for a quantifier-shift worry; the Fourth is widely regarded, even by sympathetic readers, as the weakest.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Almost every objection here comes from missing the per se / per accidens distinction. Strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged below.

The one that matters most: Newtonian inertia (Kenny) — does sustained motion really need a sustaining mover? The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"Who caused God? Why stop there?" Common

Strongest form

If everything needs a cause, God does too. Stopping at God is arbitrary.

Reply

The argument never says everything has a cause. It says whatever has potential being actualized, or is dependent, needs a sustainer. God is Pure Act — no potential, nothing to actualize, not dependent — so the question does not apply. He is not a first link needing a prior link; he is the ground the whole chain hangs on.

"Why cannot the series be infinite?" Hard

Strongest form

Maybe there is just an infinite regress of sustaining causes, with no first member at all.

Reply

That confuses the two series. An accidentally ordered series could be infinite — Aquinas grants it. But an essentially ordered, here-and-now series cannot, because every later member is a mere instrument with no power of its own. Infinitely many powerless instruments still lift nothing — the power must originate in a first, underived source.

"Act and potency is obsolete physics" Trickier

Strongest form

The metaphysics of actuality and potentiality is medieval physics, long superseded by modern science.

Reply

Act and potency are not empirical physics; they are an analysis of what change and dependence are. The principle that a potential cannot actualize itself is not an empirical generalization but a metaphysical claim about potency and act. Whatever the underlying mechanics, change still means something potential becoming actual. The argument operates one level below physics, on what it is for anything to change or depend at all. Whether this reply fully succeeds is contested; but no physics shows that a potential can actualize itself, and that is the premise the First Way needs.

"Why is the First Cause the God of theism?" Trickier

Strongest form

Even granting Pure Act, that is an abstraction. Getting to a personal God is a further leap.

Reply

From Pure Actuality, Aquinas derives — with further argument — unity, immateriality, eternity, omnipotence, omniscience, and goodness, because a purely actual being lacks the limitations that come from potency. The divine attributes are not slapped on; they follow from what Pure Act is. But the Ways themselves, if sound, establish a purely actual, necessary, sustaining first cause of present reality; they do not, by themselves, establish that this cause is good, personal, triune, or the God of Abraham. That is the work of the subsequent questions of the Summa and of revelation — and the whole Christian faith rests on revelation and on the historical evidence for the resurrection of Jesus.

"This is the fallacy of composition" Hard

Strongest form

Inferring from each part being sustained to the whole needing a sustainer is a composition fallacy.

Reply

It is not a composition inference. The claim is that each dependent thing, here and now, requires a simultaneous sustainer, and that the essentially ordered chain of sustainers must terminate. It is about the structure of dependence, not about summing parts into a whole that inherits a property.

"Why must there be only one?" Trickier

Strongest form

Maybe there are many ultimate sustainers, not one God.

Reply

A being of Pure Actuality cannot be multiplied. To have two, they would have to differ — and difference requires one to have some feature, some potency or limitation, the other lacks, which contradicts pure actuality. So there can be only one purely actual being. Unity follows from the nature of Pure Act. This is the oneness of the divine nature, not a denial of the Trinity: classical orthodoxy holds one simple divine essence subsisting as Father, Son, and Spirit — the persons neither collapsed into one nor parts of God.

The honest state of the scholarship

Where the argument really stands

The Five Ways are not a museum piece. A genuine revival of Aristotelian-Thomistic philosophy — Feser, Oderberg, and others — has put rigorous versions back into peer-reviewed debate, met by equally rigorous critics like Oppy and Kenny. There is no scholarly consensus that the arguments succeed; neither is there consensus that they fail. What no longer survives careful reading is the quick dismissal — the Russell “who caused God?” one-liner, the bumper-sticker “Newton settled it.” Those are misreadings. To his credit, Russell engaged these arguments more seriously elsewhere; it is the endlessly-repeated one-liner, not the man, that misses the qualifier in the premise. This is not to wave away the inertia objection itself: the underlying worry about whether sustained motion needs a sustaining mover is serious and must be answered with real work, not a slogan. What collapses is only the cheap version.

The real fork: whether an essentially ordered series must terminate in something purely actual (God) or whether reality might simply bottom out in a necessary initial physical state. That exchange — Oppy’s — not a slogan, is where the live debate now sits.

The bounded verdict: if sound, the argument establishes a purely actual, necessary, sustaining first cause of present reality — not by itself the good, personal, triune God, and not a beginning in time. We judge that it succeeds where it matters most: rightly read, the First and Second Ways survive their famous objections and give strong reason to believe a purely actual cause holds the world in being now. That is a serious, still-living case — not a knockdown proof — held with confidence rather than certainty, one strand in a cumulative case resting above all on the historical evidence for the resurrection.

    GO
    Graham Oppy
    "I'll grant you the per se / per accidens distinction — more than most atheists will. But why must an essentially ordered series terminate in God? Why not let it bottom out in a necessary initial state of the universe itself — no God required? On the naturalistic options, a necessary physical ground is at least as defensible as a divine one."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    This is the most serious contemporary form of the objection, and it grants the hardest-won distinction — so it deserves a real answer, not a slogan. The reply: a brute necessary physical state is not enough to be the terminus. Anything composed of parts, or carrying unrealized potential, still requires actualization — so it cannot be the first, underived member; it would just be one more dependent link. Only something purely actual and absolutely simple — not built up out of parts — can stop the series. Could the cosmos itself be that: purely actual, with no potency? That is the real crux — but a universe is changeable and composite, so it fails the very test that forced us up the series in the first place. (Note what “simple” means here: not built from components — it is not a claim about the Trinity. Classical orthodoxy holds one simple divine essence subsisting as Father, Son, and Spirit — neither collapsed into one nor parts of God.) That exchange — not a one-liner — is where the live debate now sits.
