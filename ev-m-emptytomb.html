The Empty Tomb

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and stay clear and calm in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

The empty tomb argument holds that Jesus' grave was found vacant on the first day of the week, and that the resurrection best explains why. The case is anchored in early testimony: the resurrection was proclaimed in Jerusalem on the early Christian account, within weeks of the crucifixion, and the creed Paul records in 1 Corinthians 15:3–7 — which most scholars date to within two to five years of the cross — affirms that Jesus "was buried" and "was raised." A movement preaching a risen Lord minutes' walk from an occupied tomb could have been refuted by producing the body; instead, the earliest counter-claim was that the disciples stole it, a charge most naturally read as conceding that the tomb was empty.

A telling detail is that women are named as the first witnesses. In that world a woman’s word carried little public weight, and whether women were formally barred from legal testimony is disputed; but a fabricator free to choose would have put respected men at the tomb, so that the tradition puts women there instead is the kind of counter-productive detail the criterion of embarrassment marks as probably historical.

The honest scope: an empty tomb does not by itself prove a resurrection — it rules out explanations that leave a body in place. Combined with the appearances, it points toward the resurrection as the best account; alone, it is one strand of a larger cumulative case.

Understand it

The argument, formally

Three lines from a burial to a vacated tomb.

P1

Jesus was buried in a tomb — that of Joseph of Arimathea, a named member of the council that condemned him (the bare fact of burial is held by most critical scholars; whether the tomb was known and locatable is the genuinely debated point).

P2

That tomb was, more probably than not, found empty by women followers on the first day of the week — and no source records the body ever being produced.

∴ C

Therefore the vacancy needs explaining — and the question becomes what best explains it.

The empty tomb does not prove the resurrection by itself — it eliminates every explanation that leaves a body in place, and combines with the appearances to point to one conclusion.

Then the key feature — this claim was checkable

Resurrection was proclaimed first in Jerusalem, minutes from the tomb. A movement built on an occupied grave dies in an afternoon: the authorities had motive, means, and location to refute it — and instead spread a theft story that concedes the vacancy. A body weeks dead would have been hard to identify — but the striking thing is that they are never recorded even trying.

Defending Premise 1

Jesus was buried in a tomb — that of Joseph of Arimathea, a named member of the council that condemned him

Precision check: the empty-tomb tradition is anchored to a named, identifiable burial. The bare fact of burial is held by most critical scholars; whether the tomb was known and locatable is the genuinely debated point, and it is the hinge on which the strongest version of the sceptical case turns. None of the lines of evidence below is decisive alone; their force is cumulative.

An embarrassing burier Strong · cumulative

According to all four Gospels, Jesus was interred not in a common grave but in the rock tomb of Joseph of Arimathea, a member of the Sanhedrin — the very council that had handed Jesus over. This detail carries weight precisely because it is awkward. Early Christian writing is, on the whole, sharply hostile to the Jewish leadership that secured Jesus’ death; for a pious member of that body to step forward and do right by Jesus cuts against the grain. The Catholic scholar Raymond Brown, who wrote as a critical historian rather than as an apologist, judged the burial by Joseph “very probable,” reasoning that a Christian fabrication of a Sanhedrist who honours Jesus is “almost inexplicable” given that hostility.

A named tomb in a named owner’s possession also means the claim was, in the language of historians, falsifiable: there was a specific place to point to.

Early and unembellished Cumulative

The burial sits inside the pre-Pauline creed of 1 Corinthians 15 (he was buried) and the simple Markan account — early, multiply attested, free of legendary flourish.

The kind of tomb it was Consistent · not proof

The tomb was a real and identifiable kind of place, not a narrative convenience. Jodi Magness, a leading archaeologist of Roman-period Judaea, judges the Gospel accounts of Jesus’ burial largely consistent with how Jerusalem Jews of the period buried their dead — the body wrapped in a shroud and laid in a rock-cut tomb, the entrance closed by a blocking stone.

Honest limit: This is consistent with the Gospel account rather than proof of an empty grave; the full archaeology is treated in the companion essay on the burial of Jesus.

Objection — Crossan: there was no known tomb at all Hardest

The most formidable challenge does not quibble about who first reached the tomb; it denies that there was a known tomb to reach. John Dominic Crossan argues that the burial narratives are theological fiction. Rome crucified to terrify, and routinely left the dead on the cross or in shallow graves as carrion for scavenging dogs and birds; a condemned provincial like Jesus, Crossan contends, would most likely have met the same fate, his remains never recovered. On this reading Mark created the Joseph-of-Arimathea burial tradition, and the empty-tomb story is, in his words, “a Markan creation.” If Crossan is right, the entire structure collapses: there is no tomb to be found empty. Bart Ehrman, on independent grounds, has likewise argued that the burial tradition is doubtful and that Romans typically left crucifixion victims unburied — so the sceptical case here is not Crossan’s alone.

Reply

It must be conceded what is true in the objection. Roman crucifixion was indeed a horror designed to deny the victim honourable burial, and non-burial was common. Yet the case has real countervailing weight. First, Jewish law required the burial of the executed before nightfall (Deuteronomy 21:22–23), and Josephus states that the Jews were so scrupulous on this point that they took down and buried even the crucified before sunset; Judaea was not a place where Roman non-burial customs went unchallenged. Second, the crucified man Yehohanan — whose heel-bone, still pierced by the nail, was recovered from a first-century Jerusalem tomb — is direct material proof that at least some victims of crucifixion in Jesus’ city were given proper burial. Third, the very embarrassment of crediting the burial to Joseph of Arimathea, a member of the council that condemned Jesus, and the discovery to the women, tells against pure invention. Crossan’s thesis requires treating as fiction precisely the details — a Sanhedrist benefactor, the women witnesses — that bear the marks of awkward memory rather than invention. The evidence answers this objection on balance even if it cannot rule it out with certainty.

The room: Raymond Brown judged the burial by Joseph “very probable”; Jodi Magness finds the Gospel burial accounts largely consistent with Jewish burial practice of the period. John Dominic Crossan presses the doubt hardest, and on independent grounds Bart Ehrman likewise.
Defending Premise 2

That tomb was, more probably than not, found empty by women followers on the first day of the week — and no source records the body ever being produced

Precision check: the verdict defended is the cautious “more probable than not,” not that the evidence compels the conclusion. None of this proves a resurrection. An empty tomb, by itself, is consistent with theft, relocation, confusion, or causes we cannot now recover; the leap from “the grave was empty” to “God raised him” depends on the appearances and on the failure of natural alternatives, argued separately. The empty tomb is a necessary feature of the bodily-resurrection claim and a serious historical datum in its own right — not, on its own, a demonstration of the miracle.

Early, multiply attested, and presupposed by the creed Cumulative · source debated

The empty tomb is reported in Mark and, independently, in the special material of Matthew, Luke, and John, and the burial is presupposed in the pre-Pauline creed of 1 Corinthians 15 (“he was buried, … he was raised”). A number of scholars judge that Mark drew his passion-and-burial account from an earlier connected source, pushing the tradition back well before the Gospel’s composition. On this view the empty-tomb report belongs to the pre-Markan passion source and is therefore very early — and its earliest form is conspicuously plain: an open grave and frightened women, with none of the guards, angelic choreography, or spectacle the second-century apocryphal gospels would later heap on. A tradition that reports a puzzle rather than a triumph reads far more like early memory than like developed legend.

The creed’s own words — Christ “was buried” and “was raised on the third day” (1 Corinthians 15:3–4) — already entail an emptied grave in a first-century Jewish frame. As N. T. Wright argues at length, no first-century Jew could have described a man as “raised” while his corpse still lay in the tomb; “resurrection” meant precisely the reversal of that state, so the creed presupposes the empty tomb rather than omitting it.

Honest limit: The existence and extent of the pre-Markan source is debated. The creed shows that an emptied grave belonged to the belief from the start, though not by itself that the discovery story was told that early; for the story, the weight rests on the pre-Markan source and on the embarrassment marks of the women’s witness.

Women as the first witnesses Probable · embarrassment

All four Gospels credit the discovery first to women — in the lead, Mary Magdalene. In first-century Judaea this was not a flattering choice. Josephus, reflecting the conventional attitude, writes: “Let not the testimony of women be admitted, on account of the levity and boldness of their sex.” A movement free to compose its founding story for maximum credibility would naturally have put male disciples at the tomb. That the tradition instead makes women the first and primary witnesses — and shows the male apostles disbelieving their report as “an idle tale” (Luke 24:11) — is the kind of counter-productive detail the criterion of embarrassment marks as probably historical.

The New Testament historian Richard Bauckham presses the point further: the women are not a vague literary flourish but identifiable individuals — Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James, Joanna, Salome — whose report could still be checked against living memory when the Gospels were written, and a movement inventing a story for maximum credibility would not have rested it on witnesses whose public word carried so little weight. N. T. Wright makes the same point from the other side: had the empty-tomb narratives been late apologetic fiction, casting women as the principal discoverers is the very last thing their authors would have done.

Honest limit: Whether women were formally barred from giving legal testimony is itself disputed, and this rests only on Josephus’s frank aside about how their word was regarded — but even that weaker claim is enough to make female first-witnesses a counter-productive choice for an inventor.

Enemy attestation: the counter-story Strong · not contemporaneous

One of the strongest strands is that Jesus’ opponents did not deny the tomb was empty — they offered a rival explanation for it. Matthew reports that the chief priests bribed the guard to say, “His disciples came by night and stole him away while we were asleep,” and adds that “this story has been spread among the Jews to this day.” Whatever one makes of the guard narrative itself, the telling datum is the polemic: the earliest recoverable Jewish response to the resurrection preaching was not “the body is still there” but “the body was taken.” To bother explaining why a tomb is empty is most naturally read as conceding that it was empty.

This counter-story has an unusually long paper trail. Around 150, Justin Martyr records that Jewish authorities were sending emissaries to say the disciples “stole him by night from the tomb”; around 200, Tertullian in North Africa knows the same charge. A polemic that the opponents kept alive for over a century is hard to reconcile with the idea that there was simply no empty tomb to argue about.

Honest limit: The guard narrative is unique to Matthew and critics question it. It should be granted, in fairness, that this counter-story is securely attested only from Matthew’s period onward, so the datum is strong but not contemporaneous with the events themselves.

The Jerusalem factor Suggestive

The resurrection was first proclaimed in Jerusalem, not in distant Galilee a generation later. This matters because the tomb was local and accessible, and the authorities had every motive and every means to end the movement by producing the corpse. The philosopher William Lane Craig has developed the point into a sustained argument: a movement proclaiming a bodily resurrection could not have taken root in the very city of the public execution and burial unless that grave was empty. The counter-move the sources record is not a corpse but a story about a stolen one, which concedes the tomb rather than refuting it.

Honest limit: The argument is not airtight — a body weeks dead might be hard to identify, and we cannot prove the authorities tried and failed — but the silence about any such refutation, in a context where one was so obviously wanted, is suggestive.

Objection — Paul never mentions an empty tomb

Neither the pre-Pauline creed of 1 Corinthians 15 nor Paul anywhere mentions a tomb found empty; the discovery narrative first surfaces in Mark, a decade or more later, which is why critics read it as a Markan device rather than early tradition.

Reply

The observation is accurate and should be granted: Paul nowhere narrates the discovery of an empty grave. Nor does his “spiritual body” (1 Corinthians 15:42–44) point the other way: on the most widely held reading it is not a ghost but a transformed physicality: “what is sown is perishable; what is raised is imperishable.” The precise force of pneumatikon is itself debated — some take it to mean a body animated and governed by the Spirit, others a body of a refined heavenly substance — but on either reading Paul is describing a real, embodied risen Jesus, not a disembodied apparition. But the inference does not follow, for three reasons. First, as noted above, the creed’s own words already entail an emptied grave in a first-century Jewish frame. Second, a compressed four-line kerygmatic formula, listing who died and to whom he appeared, would not pause to narrate a tomb-discovery regardless of what its author believed about the grave; silence in a summary is not denial. Third, the tradition Mark preserves — women as the first witnesses — bears the embarrassment marks noted above, which tell against a late apologetic invention. Paul’s silence, then, is the silence of a creed that assumes the empty tomb, not of a movement that had never heard of one.

Objection — Mark’s silent women: “they said nothing to anyone”

Mark’s undisputed text breaks off at the empty tomb, on a startling line: the women “went out and fled from the tomb, for trembling and astonishment had seized them, and they said nothing to anyone, for they were afraid” (Mark 16:8). Some critics read that last clause as a confession: the empty-tomb story was a late legend the earliest church had never heard, and the evangelist or his source closed it with the women telling no one precisely in order to explain why no one had heard it before. Paul never narrates the discovery; Mark, the first writer to narrate it, has its only witnesses keep it to themselves; and Matthew and Luke, writing later, both have the women report at once, which a critic can read as later evangelists smoothing an awkward original. If that is right, the empty tomb is not early memory but a story that had to apologise for its own lateness.

Reply

The observation is accurate, and whether Mark meant to end at 16:8 or his ending was lost is a question left open. But the inference fails. First, the silence is a reaction, not a policy: the women flee because “trembling and astonishment had seized them,” and, as Craig observes, fear and awe in the presence of the divine is a typical Markan reaction (Mark 4:41; 9:6). Second, the narrative itself tells us the silence did not last: one verse earlier the young man at the tomb commissions them, “go, tell his disciples and Peter that he is going before you to Galilee” (Mark 16:7), fulfilling Jesus’ own promise (Mark 14:28). On the story’s own logic the report had to get out, or there would be nothing for Mark’s readers to read. Third, the other Gospels read the silence as temporary (Matthew 28:8; Luke 24:9; John 20:2). A critic may call that correction; but it means the earliest known readers of Mark did not take 16:8 to mean the women never spoke. Fourth, the excuse theory needs the tomb story to be late, and the evidence places it early. A number of scholars judge that Mark drew his passion-and-burial account from an earlier connected source, and on that view the empty-tomb report belongs to it. A story already circulating before Mark cannot have been composed to explain why it had never circulated. The existence and extent of that source is debated, but the creed shows that an emptied grave belonged to the belief from before any Gospel was written. Fifth, a writer inventing an excuse would not have built it this way, fastening it to identifiable women whose report could still be checked against living memory and whose public word carried so little weight.

So the objection does not hold. Mark 16:8 records the women’s terror on the road from the tomb, not a silence kept until Mark wrote. This answer does not settle how Mark’s Gospel originally ended, and it does not by itself prove the tomb empty; that rests on the lines of evidence set out above. But the particular claim that the women’s silence betrays a late invention fails on the evidence.

The room: The empty tomb is affirmed by a wide range of mainstream scholars — among them the Catholic Raymond Brown, the Anglican N. T. Wright, the classicist Michael Grant (who judged the evidence “firm and plausible enough to necessitate the conclusion that the tomb was indeed found empty”), and the Lutheran ancient historian Paul Maier. Grant’s “necessitate” claims more than we do: the verdict defended here is the more cautious “more probable than not.” The oft-cited “about 75 percent” comes from Gary Habermas’s ongoing bibliographic survey; it is a count of published positions assembled by one researcher, not a randomised poll, and critics have questioned how the sample was drawn. What can be said responsibly is a substantial majority of those who have worked closely on the question — how large a majority cannot be precisely quantified — and not a unanimous one. It is also genuinely disputed, with Crossan, Ehrman, and others on the other side: a strong, well-defended position rather than a settled fact, and noticeably less secure than the near-consensus that the disciples had experiences they took to be appearances.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: Crossan and Ehrman’s — that there was no known tomb to find empty. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"Crucifixion victims were not buried" Hard

Strongest form

Crossan argues Roman practice left victims on crosses or in shallow graves for dogs. The honoured-tomb story is apologetic fiction.

Reply

Roman practice varied by province, and Judea was not a place where Roman non-burial customs went unchallenged: Josephus states Jews buried even crucified men before sunset by law, and the heel-bone of Yehohanan — a crucified man found in a family tomb — is archaeological proof that at least some victims were properly buried. Crossan’s thesis requires treating as fiction precisely the details that bear the marks of awkward memory.

"The women went to the wrong tomb" Common

Strongest form

Grief, darkness, an unfamiliar garden — the women simply mistook the tomb, found one empty, and the story grew from there.

Reply

The text says they watched where he was laid two days earlier; the Gospels place it in a named man’s tomb, not a field of holes. And the error would be the easiest in history to correct — Joseph, the authorities, anyone could walk to the right tomb and end the movement. No source records anyone doing so.

"The disciples stole the body" Common

Strongest form

The oldest explanation: the followers took the body at night and proclaimed a resurrection they knew was false.

Reply

Then the disciples who would have to be the thieves went on to suffer and, in several cases, die for proclaiming a resurrection they would have known to be staged. Sincere people die for false beliefs all the time; what is psychologically strained is a coordinated group dying for what they personally know to be a fraud. The theft story also leaves the appearances, Paul, and James completely unexplained.

"Mark has no appearances — the story grew" Trickier

Strongest form

The earliest Gospel ends at an empty tomb with frightened women and no risen Jesus. Each later Gospel adds more — classic legendary growth.

Reply

Mark ends abruptly but his Jesus has predicted both resurrection and Galilee appearances (Mark 14:28; 16:7) — the appearances are presupposed, not absent. And the appearance tradition predates Mark by decades in the 1 Corinthians 15 creed. The growth runs the wrong way for legend: the earliest source, the creed, already has the full claim.

"Jerusalem preaching proves nothing — who checks a tomb?" Trickier

Strongest form

Weeks had passed before public preaching; a decayed body would be unidentifiable, so producing one would settle nothing.

Reply

The argument is not airtight — a body weeks dead might be hard to identify, and we cannot prove the authorities tried and failed — but the silence about any such refutation, in a context where one was so obviously wanted, is suggestive.

"Paul never mentions an empty tomb" Hard

Strongest form

Our earliest source, Paul, speaks of appearances but never the tomb. The empty tomb is a later Gospel invention.

Reply

The observation is accurate and should be granted: Paul nowhere narrates the discovery of an empty grave. But the creed Paul cites runs died — buried — raised — appeared: for a first-century Jew, raised after buried means the grave is vacated; a resurrection that leaves a body is a contradiction in terms. The sequence implies the empty tomb so plainly that mentioning it would be redundant. Nor does his “spiritual body” (1 Corinthians 15:42–44) point the other way: on the most widely held reading it is not a ghost but a transformed physicality: “what is sown is perishable; what is raised is imperishable.”
