Jesus as God in the New Testament

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and stay clear and calm in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

Was the deity of Christ a doctrine invented centuries later at the Council of Nicaea (325 AD)? The New Testament documents themselves — written within the first century — the earliest within two decades of the crucifixion — by and for monotheistic Jews — already present Jesus as fully God. Nicaea defended this teaching; it did not create it.

The evidence falls into a three-part pattern: names, works, and worship. The names of God are applied to Jesus: Paul quotes Joel's "everyone who calls on the name of the LORD [YHWH] will be saved" and applies it to Jesus (Romans 10:13); John calls the Word "God" (John 1:1); Thomas addresses him as "My Lord and my God" (John 20:28); and the Father says to the Son, "Your throne, O God, is forever" (Hebrews 1:8, on the majority reading). The works of God are his: all things were made and are held together through him (John 1:3; Colossians 1:16-17), and he forgives sins and judges the world. And the worship reserved for God alone is given to him — worship that angels in Revelation refuse, yet the Lamb receives (Revelation 5).

Honest scope: individual proof-texts can be disputed, and Jesus's genuine humanity (he hungers, prays, and dies) is fully affirmed alongside his deity — the historic doctrine is that he is both. The strength of the case is the convergence: in a fiercely monotheistic culture, this whole pattern is either the gravest blasphemy or the deepest truth.

Before you start · key terms

Eight words this argument leans on

Learn these first — the objections below turn on them, and most weak versions of the argument come from blurring one of them.

Divine identityRichard Bauckham’s phrase for what the New Testament does repeatedly and across independent authors: including Jesus within the unique identity of the one God of Israel — placing him on the Creator’s side of the line that Jewish monotheism drew between God and everything else. The framework is influential but not uncontested: James D. G. Dunn and, from a different angle, James F. McGrath question how sharply that boundary can be drawn.
YHWH texts applied to JesusOld Testament passages about the LORD himself, applied without apology to Jesus: Isaiah 45’s every-knee-shall-bow in the Philippians hymn, Joel’s “everyone who calls on the name of the Lord” in Romans 10 (on the most natural reading of Paul’s argument), Psalm 102’s unchanging Creator in Hebrews 1, Isaiah’s “the first and the last” in Revelation.
Direct predicationA text that does not merely place Jesus on the Creator’s side of the line but calls him theos, “God,” directly. Two depend on neither the Granville Sharp rule nor a disputed comma — Thomas’s confession, which no grammatical alternative touches, and Hebrews’ “O God,” which rests on the reading its own chapter’s argument favours — and they are the ones worth resting weight on.
Granville Sharp constructionThe grammatical rule on which Titus 2:13 (“our great God and Savior Jesus Christ”) and 2 Peter 1:1 turn; Daniel Wallace argues it holds without exception in native Koine when its conditions are properly met. Translators have not always agreed, and the grammar is genuinely contested — the weakest leg of the argument, not its foundation.
Binitarian devotionLarry Hurtado’s term for the pattern of devotional practice that emerged “amazingly early,” among Jewish believers who would have regarded worshiping any creature as blasphemy: they sang to Christ, baptized into his name, called on his name, broke bread in his presence, and prayed to him — while insisting, with the Shema, that God is one.
Marana thaAn Aramaic prayer Paul preserves untranslated in his Greek-speaking churches: “Our Lord, come!” That it survives in Aramaic is the point — it carries us back behind Paul’s Greek to the Aramaic-speaking Judean church. It is not the homage due a vice-regent; it is an invocation, that is, prayer.
The agency frameworkThe reading of Jehovah’s Witnesses and biblical Unitarians, drawn from the Hebrew Bible: an emissary can bear the sender’s name and authority — the angel of the LORD speaks as God, Moses is made “like God to Pharaoh” — without being the LORD. The motif is genuinely present in the Hebrew Bible, but it cannot bear the weight put on it: it breaks at one point — cultic worship.
Developmental thesisBart Ehrman’s case that the earliest followers held an “exaltation” Christology and that the elevation to the co-equal God confessed at Nicaea was the later step. He also finds a pre-existent Christ in material older than Paul, so what he says developed was the divine figure’s rank, not the date of pre-existence itself.
The argument, formally

Names, works, and worship — the three tests of deity.

P1

The New Testament gives Jesus the names of God: Kyrios in Old Testament YHWH quotations applied to him (Romans 10:13), the I AM, the First and the Last, called theos outright (John 1:1; 20:28; Hebrews 1:8, on the majority reading).

P2

It gives him the works and worship of God: creating and sustaining all things, forgiving sins, judging the world — and receiving the worship that angels refuse and the Shema reserves.

∴ C

Therefore the New Testament presents Jesus as fully God — included within the identity of the one God of Israel, distinct from the Father, not a second god; not a later council’s invention but the source documents’ claim.

Structure your case on the triad — names, works, worship — and no single proof-text dispute can sink it: the pattern is the argument.

Then the context that gives it force — Jewish monotheism

These claims were written by and for monotheist Jews who recited the Shema daily and died rather than worship Caesar. In that world, applying YHWH texts to a crucified man is not poetic license; it is either the gravest blasphemy or the deepest truth.

What follows — and what doesn’t

Don’t read the conclusion — reason to it

Working only from what the argument has established: what does each piece of evidence rule out, and how far does the inference reach? Reason each line out, then reveal.

Some twenty-seven documents, perhaps nine or ten authors, roughly half a century, two languages, scattered communities that never coordinated their theology — and they converge on the same inclusion. So the convergence is…?
hard to explain as coincidence or gradual driftPick up any individual verse and a determined reader can propose another reading. But the New Testament is not a single verse. The convergence of independent authors on the same astonishing inclusion is hard to explain as coincidence or gradual drift.
A text in which the LORD swears that to him alone every knee shall bow is applied to Jesus by a monotheist Jew. So Jesus is placed…?
on the Creator’s side of the lineThat last phrase of the Philippians hymn is lifted from Isaiah 45, where the LORD swears that to him alone every knee shall bow — and Paul applies it to Jesus. In the same way Hebrews reads a psalm about Yahweh’s eternity and creative work, without apology, as words to Christ.
Grant every grammatical concession on the three contested texts. What is left?
a floor — two direct predications still standJohn’s confession, which no grammatical alternative touches, and Hebrews’ “O God,” which rests on the reading its own chapter’s argument favours. The “called God” case does not live or die on a disputed comma; it has a floor — John 20:28 at the least — and the floor is firm.
An angel twice refuses worship and redirects it to God; Jesus receives it without a word of protest; the first Aramaic-speaking church prays to him. So he has…?
outrun the category of “supreme agent”What Jewish monotheism guarded jealously for God was not the posture of homage but the substance of worship — sacrifice, invocation, prayer. A figure you worship and pray to, while confessing in the same breath that God is one, has already outrun the category of “supreme agent.”
The pattern of worship is in place within a decade or two of the crucifixion, not a century after. So what developed?
the clarity, not the substanceThe earliest Christians did not slowly climb a ladder from a low Christology to a high one; on Bauckham’s reading they expressed the highest possible claim from the start, using the very resources of Jewish monotheism. John is not the invention of a new doctrine but the most explicit articulation of an old one.
Grant all of it. Does this, by itself, establish the developed creed?
no — it is one strand of a cumulative caseIt does not, by itself, establish the developed creed. It does not settle the disputed grammar of Romans 9:5 or prove the Trinity from a proof-text. It interlocks with the historical case for the resurrection, the witness of the earliest post-apostolic church, and the philosophical coherence of trinitarian monotheism. What it offers is the foundation those other lines build on.
Defending Premise 1

The New Testament gives Jesus the names of God: Kyrios in Old Testament YHWH quotations applied to him (Romans 10:13), the I AM, the First and the Last, called theos outright (John 1:1; 20:28; Hebrews 1:8, on the majority reading).

Precision check: the claim is not that the New Testament hands us the later vocabulary of “one essence in three persons” or “two natures in one person.” Those are fourth- and fifth-century formulations — the church’s attempt to systematize, against rival readings, what it found already in these texts. It is the more modest and more defensible claim that the raw data the creeds later organized is already here, distributed across the whole canon, and that Jesus is placed on the Creator’s side of the line that Jewish monotheism drew between God and everything else. It does not settle the disputed grammar of Romans 9:5 or prove the Trinity from a proof-text.

Isaiah’s every-knee-shall-bow, given to Jesus Strong

In Philippians Paul quotes what most scholars regard as a pre-Pauline hymn — material older than the letter itself — describing one who was “in the form of God” and did not count “equality with God” a thing to be grasped, and who is finally given “the name that is above every name,” at which every knee shall bow. That last phrase is lifted from Isaiah 45, where the LORD swears that to him alone every knee shall bow — and Paul applies it to Jesus.

Honest limit: the pre-Pauline dating is itself debated — Gordon Fee argues Paul composed the hymn — but the worship-of-Jesus datum stands either way.

Kyrios with the freight loaded — Romans 10 and Joel Interpretive · the reading most adopt

In Romans 10, having just written that “if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord… you will be saved,” Paul clinches the point by quoting Joel — a passage in which the Lord one calls upon is Yahweh himself. On the most natural reading of Paul’s argument, the Old Testament’s own language for calling on God is applied, without apology, to Jesus.

Honest limit: the identification of the Lord of verse 13 with Christ rather than the Father rests on the flow of Paul’s argument here rather than on an explicit statement in the verse itself; it is the reading most interpreters adopt.

Hebrews 1 — a text about Yahweh, read as words to the Son Strong

Hebrews’ first chapter strings together Old Testament passages and aims them at the Son. The author quotes Psalm 102 — a psalm addressed to the LORD, the unchanging Creator — and applies it to the Son: “You, Lord, laid the foundation of the earth in the beginning, and the heavens are the work of your hands… but you are the same, and your years will have no end.” A text about Yahweh’s eternity and creative work is, without apology, read as words to Christ.

The First and the Last Strong

The risen Christ tells John, “Fear not, I am the first and the last,” and at the book’s end, “I am the Alpha and the Omega, the first and the last, the beginning and the end.” “The first and the last” is precisely how the LORD identifies himself in Isaiah — “I am the first and I am the last; besides me there is no god.” The title God reserves for himself, the exalted Jesus claims.

The Word was God; the I AM Strong

The Gospel opens by reaching behind Genesis: “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God.” The same Gospel has Jesus take the divine self-designation of Exodus and Isaiah onto his own lips — “before Abraham was, I am.”

Honest limit: Jehovah’s Witnesses render that last clause “a god” — a reading that, on the mainstream grammatical analysis, misreads the grammar; the focused treatment of why the standard translation holds lives in the companion essay on John 1:1.

“My Lord and my God” — the floor Strong · no grammatical alternative

Seeing the risen Jesus, Thomas “said to him” — the narrator’s own words — “My Lord and my God.” The narrator frames the words as spoken to Jesus; the phrase is an articular nominative that functions as a vocative of direct address, the ordinary Greek way of calling someone “Lord” and “God”; and Jesus, far from correcting the confession, accepts it and blesses those who will believe without seeing, while John lets it stand as the climax of the book. The “called God” case does not live or die on a disputed comma; it has a floor — John 20:28 at the least — and the floor is firm.

“Your throne, O God” — the Father addressing the Son Strong · majority reading

Hebrews 1:8 — the Father addressing the Son: “Your throne, O God, is forever and ever.” Most translations and commentators read “O God” (ho theos) as a vocative of address — the Father addressing the Son as God.

Honest limit: a minority render it “God is your throne,” taking the same words as a statement about the Son rather than an address to him, and they find their foothold in the next verse, where “God, your God, has anointed you” plainly means the Father. That alternative is grammatically possible; it simply sits awkwardly in a chapter whose entire argument is the Son’s superiority to the angels — a chapter that enthrones and anoints him — which is why the vocative is the majority reading. Concede the alternative and the point is softened, not lost.

The other direct predications Contested · the weakest leg

A few verses appear to call Jesus “God” directly: Titus 2:13 (“our great God and Savior Jesus Christ”) and 2 Peter 1:1 turn on the Granville Sharp rule, which Daniel Wallace argues holds without exception in native Koine when its conditions are properly met; Romans 9:5 turns on where one places a comma the original lacked.

Honest limit: translators have not always agreed, and the grammar is genuinely contested. This is the weakest leg of the argument, not its foundation. The case does not rest on it.

Objection — “Thomas was only crying ‘oh my God’”

A sceptic answers that this was an astonished cry to heaven, an “oh my God” rather than a confession of Jesus.

Reply

The reply is cumulative and rests on no single word. The narrator frames the words as spoken to Jesus (“said to him”); the phrase is an articular nominative that functions as a vocative of direct address; and Jesus, far from correcting the confession, accepts it and blesses those who will believe without seeing (John 20:29), while John lets it stand as the climax of the book, immediately before he states why he wrote (John 20:30–31). It is no accident that the same Gospel opens with “the Word was God” and closes here: the deity of Christ is the frame around the whole work, not a stray remark inside it.

Objection — “The ‘God’ texts depend on disputed grammar”

Titus 2:13, 2 Peter 1:1 and Romans 9:5 turn on the Granville Sharp rule and on a comma the original lacked. Translators have not always agreed, and the grammar is genuinely contested.

Reply

Candor is required — and it costs the case nothing. Grant, for the sake of argument, every grammatical concession on the contested three, and two direct predications still stand: John’s confession, which no grammatical alternative touches, and Hebrews’ “O God,” which rests on the reading its own chapter’s argument favours. The weight of the case falls on worship and divine identity, to which the objectors’ strongest reading must now answer.

The room: Richard Bauckham calls it including Jesus within the unique identity of the one God of Israel; Larry Hurtado documents the devotional practice; Daniel Wallace defends the Granville Sharp rule as holding without exception in native Koine, when its conditions are properly met; Murray Harris’s Jesus as God is the book-length study weighing text by text every place the New Testament calls Jesus theos. Bauckham’s “divine identity” framework is influential but not uncontested: James D. G. Dunn and, from a different angle, James F. McGrath have questioned how sharply the boundary of divine identity can be drawn, and Hurtado — broadly an ally — preferred to frame the data as devotional practice rather than in Bauckham’s identity categories.
Defending Premise 2

It gives him the works and worship of God: creating and sustaining all things, forgiving sins, judging the world — and receiving the worship that angels refuse and the Shema reserves.

Precision check: a gesture of homage was not itself the line — Israelites could bow before a king as well as before the LORD (1 Chronicles 29:20 uses a single verb of prostration for both). What Jewish monotheism guarded jealously for God was not the posture of homage but the substance of worship — sacrifice, invocation, prayer. Angels, prophets, and kings could be honored; but the intermediary figures of Second Temple Judaism characteristically refuse worship and redirect it to God.

Worship accepted Strong

Angels refuse worship (Revelation 22); Jesus accepts it — from Thomas, from the disciples, from heaven’s throng singing to the Lamb the doxologies of God (Revelation 5). In the very books that record it, an angel twice refuses the worship John offers, redirecting it to God (Revelation 19:10; 22:9); Jesus receives it without a word of protest.

The earliest devotion — Hurtado Strong

In his major study Lord Jesus Christ, Hurtado documents a pattern of devotional practice — what he calls a “binitarian” shape of worship — that emerged “amazingly early,” within the first years of the movement, among Jewish believers who would have regarded worshiping any creature as blasphemy. They sang to Christ, baptized into his name, called on his name, broke bread in his presence, and prayed to him. And they did this while insisting, with the Shema, that God is one.

Marana tha — prayer, in Aramaic Strong · early date not seriously contested

Paul preserves an Aramaic prayer untranslated into his Greek-speaking churches: Marana tha, “Our Lord, come!” That the invocation survives in Aramaic is the point. It carries us back behind Paul’s Greek to the Aramaic-speaking Judean church — to the very first community, calling on the risen Jesus as the Lord who will come. The highest devotion is not a late Hellenistic overlay. It is bedrock. Marana tha is not the homage due a vice-regent — it is an invocation, calling on the risen Lord to come; that is, prayer, offered by the first Aramaic-speaking church.

Doing what only God does — creation Supporting · Colossians authorship disputed

All things made through him and held together in him (John 1:3; Colossians 1:16–17). Paul calls Christ “the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation,” the one “by him all things were created,” and then says flatly that “in him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily.” The one by whom every created thing was made cannot himself be one of the created things — firstborn over creation, not first item within it.

Honest limit: the pre-Pauline status of the Colossians hymn (Col. 1:15–20) is more debated than that of Philippians 2, and the authorship of Colossians is itself disputed.

Doing what only God does — forgiving sins One strand of the cumulative case

Jesus forgives sins, prompting the objection, “Who can forgive sins but God alone?” — an objection the narrative does not deny but answers by demonstration.

Doing what only God does — judging One strand of the cumulative case

Jesus makes a person’s eternal standing turn on their response to him: “Everyone who acknowledges me before men, I also will acknowledge before my Father who is in heaven, but whoever denies me before men, I also will deny before my Father who is in heaven” (Matthew 10:32–33). A prophet points away from himself to God. Jesus places himself at the hinge of the final judgment.

Objection — the agency reading Hardest

Jehovah’s Witnesses and biblical Unitarians read this same evidence within an “agency” framework drawn from the Hebrew Bible. There, an emissary can bear the sender’s name and authority — the angel of the LORD speaks as God, Moses is made “like God to Pharaoh” (Exodus 7:1) — without being the LORD. On this reading, Jesus is God’s supreme agent, given the divine name and even a kind of worship as the one who perfectly represents God, while remaining a distinct and subordinate being. The worship Jesus receives is the relative homage due an exalted vice-regent. In its academic form the reading is subtler still: the New Testament historian James F. McGrath, contesting Bauckham’s and Wright’s “divine-identity” category, grants that John’s language about Jesus is genuinely exalted yet argues that Second Temple Judaism could let God share his name and functions with a supreme agent without a second God arising — and points to John 17:3, where Jesus addresses the Father as “the only true God” and himself as the one “whom you have sent” (James F. McGrath, The Only True God: Early Christian Monotheism in Its Jewish Context [Urbana: University of Illinois Press, 2009], esp. ch. 4). The agency motif these readings appeal to is genuinely present in the Hebrew Bible; the question is whether it can bear the weight they put on it.

Reply

It is true that in the Hebrew Bible an emissary can bear the sender’s name and authority. But there is one thing no agent — not the angel of the LORD, not Moses made “like God to Pharaoh” (Exodus 7:1), not the most exalted king, not even the great angels who were given God’s own name, such as Yahoel in the Apocalypse of Abraham — ever receives: the cultic devotion reserved for God alone. This is where even McGrath’s version meets its limit — grant that God’s name and functions could be entrusted to a supreme agent, and the worship itself still could not be. And that is exactly the line the earliest Christians crossed with Jesus. A figure you worship and pray to, while confessing in the same breath that God is one, has already outrun the category of “supreme agent.” John 17:3 excludes the idols, not the Son; two verses later the same Jesus asks for the glory he had “before the world existed” (John 17:5) — glory that Isaiah says YHWH shares with no other (Isaiah 42:8). Grant the framework the titles for the sake of argument; it still breaks at the altar. So the agency reading does not hold.

Objection — Ehrman: deity was a later development

Bart Ehrman’s developmental thesis in How Jesus Became God argues that the historical Jesus did not claim divinity, that his earliest followers held an “exaltation” Christology — God raised the man Jesus and adopted him to divine status — yet he also finds a pre-existent Christ already in material older than Paul, a figure he takes to have been conceived at first as a subordinate divine being — God’s chief angel, or a divinity of a lesser order — whose elevation to the co-equal God confessed at Nicaea in 325 is the later step, reaching its New Testament height in the latest Gospel, John. On this view what developed was the divine figure’s rank, not the date of pre-existence itself.

Reply

The disagreement is not really about dates — Ehrman himself finds a pre-existent Christ in material older than Paul’s letters — but about what kind of being the earliest Christians took him to be. And the evidence answers that question with the one thing his developmental thesis cannot easily accommodate: the earliest worship. Marana tha is Aramaic, pre-Pauline, rooted in the Jerusalem church of the 30s, and the pattern of worship Hurtado documents is in place within a decade or two of the crucifixion, not a century after. Bauckham sharpens the point: the earliest Christians did not slowly climb a ladder from a low Christology to a high one; they expressed the highest possible claim — inclusion in the divine identity — from the start, using the very resources of Jewish monotheism. The development is in the clarity, not in the substance. Ehrman is owed one concession, and it is narrower than it first appears: the technical creedal vocabulary and systematic formulation (Nicaea’s “one essence,” Chalcedon’s “two natures”) came later, as the church’s articulation of what was already in the texts — not that the tri-une reality, or the deity of Christ, was a later human invention. The data — Jesus sharing God’s throne, name, and worship — is present from the earliest layers. What the development thesis gets wrong is not that the vocabulary developed — of course it did — but its leap to the conclusion that the deity of Christ was a late arrival, when the deity is precisely the thing that developing vocabulary was straining to state.

The room: Hurtado (devotion “amazingly early”), Bauckham (inclusion in the divine identity from the start), McGrath (the academic form of the agency reading, contesting Bauckham’s and Wright’s “divine-identity” category while granting John’s language is genuinely exalted), Ehrman (the most formidable academic challenge; he too finds a pre-existent Christ in material older than Paul), Gordon Fee (argues Paul composed the Philippians hymn).
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: the Jewish agency framework — an emissary bears the sender’s name and authority. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"Son of God just meant king — Israel and her kings bore the title" Common

Strongest form

Psalm 2 calls the king God’s son; Israel is God’s son in Hosea. Son of God is royal language, not a deity claim.

Reply

Sometimes, yes — which is why this argument never rests on that title alone. Kings were called sons; none was given the divine name in YHWH quotations, none was prayed to, none made the agent of creation, none worshipped by monotheists. The triad — names, works, worship — is what no merely royal figure ever received.

"John 1:1 lacks the article — the Word was a god" Trickier

Strongest form

The Greek has no article before theos, so the New World Translation renders it a god — divine-ish, not Almighty God.

Reply

The construction is an anarthrous predicate nominative standing before the verb. Be careful with Colwell here: his 1933 rule was about definiteness, and apologists routinely overclaim it — the careful analysis (Harner, Wallace, Harris) is that the force is qualitative, the Word is everything God is without being the Father. Adding the article would have collapsed the Word into the Father, the one error John is avoiding. It is also true that the NWT renders article-less theos as ‘God’ at 1:6, 1:12 and 1:13 — but state that carefully. Do not call those the identical construction: they are genitives, not preverbal predicate nominatives. And do not say ‘throughout the chapter’ — at 1:18 the NWT prints ‘the only-begotten god,’ lowercase, and a well-read Witness will have that verse ready. And verse 3 settles it independently of any grammar dispute: all created things came through him, so he stands on the Creator’s side of the line.

"Jesus said the Father is greater than I — and prayed to God" Common

Strongest form

A God who prays to God, grows, hungers, and calls the Father greater is not Almighty God. The texts themselves block the claim.

Reply

The incarnation predicts exactly this two-register data: truly human, so he hungers and prays; truly God, so he creates and forgives. Greater is positional — the sent one honouring the sender (the same Jesus says I and the Father are one, and the Jews reach for stones, you make yourself God). Reading the human register against the divine one ignores that the documents assert both on purpose.

"High Christology evolved — Mark’s Jesus is humble, John’s is God" Hard

Strongest form

Trace the timeline: Mark has a human prophet, John — decades later — has the cosmic I AM. Deity is the end of a legend curve, not the beginning. In its academic form (Bart Ehrman, How Jesus Became God) the claim is about rank, not date: Ehrman finds a pre-existent Christ in material older than Paul, but a subordinate divine being — God’s chief angel, or a divinity of a lesser order — whose elevation to the co-equal God is the later step.

Reply

The curve fails at both ends. Earlier than Mark: Paul’s letters and the pre-Pauline material — the Philippians 2 hymn, the marana tha prayer, 1 Corinthians 8:6 dividing the Shema around Christ (the dominant reading, though James McGrath contests it) — already maximal, within the first two decades. And Mark is not low: his Jesus forgives sins as God alone can, commands the sea (a YHWH act in the Psalms), and walks the water intending to pass by them — Job’s language for God. The deity claim is at the root, not the branch. Against that form the dispute is not about dates but about what kind of being the first Christians took him to be — and the earliest worship answers it.

"Worship just meant homage — proskynesis to kings" Trickier

Strongest form

The Greek proskyneo covers bowing to any superior — people bow to kings and prophets throughout scripture. Bowing to Jesus proves respect, not deity.

Reply

Context assigns the meaning — and the New Testament polices it: Peter lifts Cornelius (I too am a man), the angel twice refuses John (worship God!). Jesus, in the same corpus, accepts it — in contexts of doxology, prayer, and the divine name. The early Christians knew the homage/worship line precisely; they died for refusing Caesar what they gave the Lamb.

"Council of Nicaea invented the deity in 325" Common

Strongest form

Constantine’s bishops voted Jesus divine for imperial unity — before Nicaea, Christians knew a human teacher.

Reply

Nicaea answered Arius; it could not invent what its sources already said. Ignatius writes our God, Jesus Christ around 107; Pliny in 112 reports Christians singing to Christ as to a god; the second-century critics mock Christians precisely for worshipping a crucified man. The council defined vocabulary for a faith already approaching three centuries old — the deity is in the first-century documents this whole argument is built from.

“He is God’s supreme agent, not God” — the Jewish agency reading Hard

Strongest form

This is not proof-texting, and it is rooted in the Hebrew Bible. There an emissary bears the sender’s name and authority — the angel of the LORD speaks as God, Moses is made “like God to Pharaoh” (Exodus 7:1) — without being the LORD. Jewish law put it as the shaliach principle: a man’s agent is as the man himself (Mishnah Berakhot 5:5). So Jesus is God’s supreme agent, given the divine name, the divine works, and a relative honour owed to the one who perfectly represents God — while remaining a distinct and subordinate being. And notice how much of your own evidence fits: the New Testament constantly says he was sent, given, appointed. That is agent language. In its academic form the reading is subtler still: the New Testament historian James F. McGrath, contesting Bauckham’s and Wright’s “divine-identity” category, grants that John’s language about Jesus is genuinely exalted yet argues that Second Temple Judaism could let God share his name and functions with a supreme agent without a second God arising — and points to John 17:3, where Jesus addresses the Father as “the only true God” and himself as the one “whom you have sent”.

Reply

This is the hardest objection on the board. The agency motif is genuinely present in the Hebrew Bible, and the New Testament does say he was sent, given and appointed; the question is whether that can bear the weight put on it. It cannot. A gesture of homage was never the line: Israelites bowed before a king and before the LORD with the same verb (1 Chronicles 29:20). What Jewish monotheism guarded for God alone was the substance of worship — sacrifice, invocation, prayer. Angels, prophets and kings could be honoured; none of them could be prayed to. And in 1 Corinthians 16:22 Paul, writing Greek to a Greek city, leaves one Aramaic word untranslated: Marana tha — “Our Lord, come!” That is prayer, addressed to Jesus, originating among the earliest Aramaic-speaking believers in Judea. A figure you worship and pray to, while confessing in the same breath that God is one, has already outrun the category of supreme agent. John 17:3 excludes the idols, not the Son; two verses later the same Jesus asks for the glory he had “before the world existed” (John 17:5) — glory Isaiah says YHWH shares with no other (Isaiah 42:8). Grant the framework the titles for the sake of argument; it still breaks at the altar. So the agency reading does not hold.

“Mark 1:11, Acts 2:36 and Romans 1:4 show he was adopted as Son” Trickier

Strongest form

Ehrman’s exaltation reading leans on a handful of verses that can be read as Jesus becoming Son or Lord at a particular moment: the voice at his baptism, “You are My beloved Son” (Mark 1:11); Paul’s sermon applying Psalm 2, “today I have become Your Father,” to the resurrection (Acts 13:33); Peter’s “God has made this Jesus, whom you crucified, both Lord and Christ” (Acts 2:36); and the Son “declared with power to be the Son of God by His resurrection” (Romans 1:4). His claim is not that Luke or Paul meant these verses as adoption, but that they preserve older formulas that did, and at Luke 3:22 he argues that Luke himself wrote the same line of Psalm 2 (“today I have begotten you”).

Reply

That is a reconstruction of a layer behind the texts, and it is genuinely debated; the texts as we have them do not require it. Two things count against it. It asks us to believe that Luke and Paul kept formulas whose sense they did not share, and Ehrman himself thinks Paul adjusted the Romans formula to fit his own view, so the words as Paul wrote them carry Paul’s sense, not an adoption. And the earliest material we have does not show exaltation coming first: the hymn behind Philippians 2, which Ehrman also dates before Paul’s letters, already has a pre-existent one “in the form of God,” so the two kinds of language stand side by side from the start. The voice at the baptism echoes Psalm 2 but says only “You are My beloved Son,” without the psalm’s “today I have become Your Father” (though one early family of manuscripts of Luke 3:22 does read the psalm). Luke applies the same psalm to Jesus before Easter, to the rulers gathered “against Your holy servant Jesus, whom You anointed” (Acts 4:25–27), so for him Psalm 2 does not fix the resurrection as the moment Jesus became Messiah; and he has the angel call the newborn Jesus “Christ the Lord” (Luke 2:11). The apostles did read the resurrection as God’s confirmation, but a confirmation needs something to confirm: the one “declared with power to be the Son of God by His resurrection” is already “His Son, who was a descendant of David according to the flesh” (Romans 1:3–4). The contrast is between the Son in the weakness of the flesh and the Son in power, not between a mere man and an adopted Son. The exaltation brings him, as the risen man, into the open exercise of a lordship that was always his; it gives him no new nature, no new sonship and no higher divine rank. The deep dive takes up Ehrman’s case in full.

The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

The “called God” case has a floor — John 20:28 at the least — and the floor is firm; Hebrews 1:8’s “O God” rests on the majority, vocative reading. Titus 2:13, 2 Peter 1:1 and Romans 9:5 turn on Greek grammar and punctuation, and the grammar is genuinely contested: this is the weakest leg of the argument, not its foundation. The weight falls on worship and divine identity — the pattern of devotion Hurtado documents is in place within a decade or two of the crucifixion, and the early date of the pre-Pauline Marana tha is not seriously contested.

A fairness most defenders skip: Bauckham’s “divine identity” framework is influential but not uncontested: James D. G. Dunn and, from a different angle, James F. McGrath have questioned how sharply the boundary of divine identity can be drawn, and Hurtado — broadly an ally — preferred to frame the data as devotional practice rather than in Bauckham’s identity categories. The pre-Pauline dating of the Philippians hymn is itself debated (Gordon Fee argues Paul composed it), the Colossians hymn’s status and the letter’s authorship more so; and Gathercole’s case for Synoptic preexistence is a serious minority-to-moderate position, not a scholarly consensus. The most formidable academic challenge is Bart Ehrman’s developmental thesis — and Ehrman himself finds a pre-existent Christ in material older than Paul.

The bounded verdict: This establishes one strand of a cumulative argument: that the New Testament, read across its full breadth, presents Jesus as sharing the identity of the one God of Israel and as the rightful recipient of God’s worship — and that this presentation is early, widespread, and woven into the church’s earliest practice, not bolted on at the end. The New Testament does not hand us the later vocabulary of “one essence in three persons” or “two natures in one person”; the raw data the creeds later organized is already here, and the developed doctrine is the faithful articulation of it rather than a later imposition upon it. When Thomas said “My Lord and my God,” he was not inventing a category; he was naming what the whole New Testament had been saying all along.

    Seeker
    "On the cross Jesus cries, “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” Someone who cries out to God, abandoned by God, can’t himself be God."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    The Gospels place on the lips of the dying Jesus the opening cry of Psalm 22, and the case does not hide it: the one who shares the divine identity enters, on the cross, the depths of human god-forsakenness. It is the incarnate Son, in his full humanity, who suffers, dies, and cries out — so that God himself, in the person of the Son, has entered our suffering and death for us. That is not to say the divine nature suffered or changed, nor that the Father was crucified or forsaken (the ancient error of patripassianism); the Father is not the Son, and the impassible God does not perish — the cross reveals who God is, it does not make God mortal. Nor was the Son torn from the Father or the Trinity divided: the Father never ceased to love the Son, and the union of the persons was never broken. The forsakenness is real, borne in the Son’s human experience, not a severing within the one God. And the cross does not stand outside the divine identity: Isaiah’s despised and crushed Servant shall be “high and lifted up,” the very words of the LORD’s throne-vision, and John says Isaiah “saw his glory and spoke of him.” On the divine-identity reading the cry is not a lapse into something less than God but the fullest disclosure of who God is: the God who gives himself, to the uttermost, for his people.
