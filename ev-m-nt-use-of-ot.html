How the New Testament Uses the Old

The deep dive explains the method. This track makes you able to wield it — show that the New Testament sorts its citations, keep typology in its lane, and answer the “you just rip verses out of context” charge with gentleness.

The argument in brief

“Christians just read Jesus back into the Old Testament after the fact.” The charge is about method, so the answer must be about the method, not merely this or that disputed verse. And on a close look at what the New Testament actually does, the charge fails: its use of the Old is graded, self-aware, and open to inspection — and the forward-leaning reading it performs was not imposed from outside, because the Hebrew Scriptures already read themselves that way.

“Quoting the Old Testament” is not one move but several, and Matthew keeps them distinct. His second chapter — where the charge bites hardest — reaches for the Hebrew Scriptures in four different ways: direct prediction (Micah 5:2 on Bethlehem, Matthew 2:5–6), typology (Hosea 11:1, “Out of Egypt I called my son,” 2:15), applied analogy (Jeremiah 31:15, “Rachel weeping,” 2:17–18, flagged with the looser “then was fulfilled”), and summary (“a Nazarene,” 2:23, credited to “the prophets” and matching no single verse). The word “fulfill” (Greek plēroō) is itself wider than “predict” — Jesus came to “fulfill” the Law (Matthew 5:17), and commandments are not predictions.

The method is contextual, not atomistic: David Instone-Brewer’s study of the earliest datable Jewish scribal exegesis finds its interpreters, before AD 70, assumed Scripture coherent and read it with its context, not atomised into slogans; Klyne Snodgrass reaches a kindred verdict about the New Testament’s own practice. And the forward-leaning reading was not fitted over these texts after the fact: the Hebrew Scriptures already read themselves forward (inner-biblical exegesis, documented by Michael Fishbane). The bounded verdict: this shows the New Testament reads in context — it does not by itself prove that Jesus is the one in whom Israel’s story reaches its goal. That case rests on the resurrection.

The argument, formally

From the charge about method to a bounded verdict.

P1

The New Testament’s use of the Old is not one undifferentiated move but several distinct, self-aware kinds — direct prediction, typology, applied analogy, and summary — sorted and flagged, sometimes with a looser verb for the looser links (“then was fulfilled”).

P2

Its method is contextual, not atomistic — the earliest datable Jewish scribal exegesis assumed Scripture coherent and read with its context — and the forward-leaning reading at its heart was not imposed from outside, because the Hebrew Scriptures already read themselves forward (inner-biblical exegesis).

∴ C

Therefore the method charge fails: the New Testament’s reading of the Old is describable, graded, and self-aware — not a conjuror’s trick.

Mind the scope. This shows the New Testament reads in context; it does not show that every citation is a strict prediction fulfilled to the letter, nor prove that Jesus is the one in whom Israel’s story reaches its goal. That case rests on the resurrection — and the forward-looking predictions are the business of the companion essay on messianic prophecy.

The key move — “fulfill” is wider than “predict.”

The whole caricature assumes that whenever the New Testament says “fulfill,” it is claiming a prediction came true — so a verse about Israel’s past (Hosea 11:1) looks like history mistaken for a forecast. But plēroō is wider than that: it often means “filled full,” an earlier pattern reaching completion in Christ. Jesus came to “fulfill” the Law (Matthew 5:17), and commandments are not predictions. Once you see the four kinds, the charge that it is all proof-texting cannot survive a close look.

Defending Premise 1

The New Testament’s use of the Old is not one undifferentiated move but several distinct, self-aware kinds — sorted and flagged, sometimes with a looser verb for the looser links.

Precision check: one of the most useful replies to the charge that the New Testament simply quote-mines is to notice that “quoting the Old Testament” is not one move but several, and Matthew keeps them distinct. His second chapter — the very stretch where the charge is pressed hardest — is the clearest classroom, because within a few verses it reaches for the Hebrew Scriptures in four different ways, and only one of them is typology.

The four ways, in one chapter Open to inspection

Direct prediction. Asked where the Messiah is to be born, the chief priests answer from Micah 5:2: “in Bethlehem of Judea” (Matthew 2:5–6) — a forward-looking forecast checked against an event, and the business of the companion essay on messianic prophecy, not this one. Typology. “Out of Egypt I called my son” (Hosea 11:1, at 2:15): not a prediction but a pattern — Israel’s story relived and brought to its goal in the one faithful Son. Application. Over the murder of the infants Matthew sets Jeremiah’s lament, “Rachel weeping for her children” (31:15, at 2:17–18) — and here he does not even use his usual “so that it might be fulfilled”; he writes “then was fulfilled,” a shift many read as marking the looser bond of an analogy applied, in a chapter whose very next breath is a promise of return and hope. Summary. That Jesus “would be called a Nazarene” (2:23) is credited not to one prophet but to “the prophets,” plural, and matches no single verse.

Sorted, graded, and self-aware

The payoff weakens the objection rather than merely parrying it. A writer out only to raid the Scriptures for proof-texts would not trouble to vary his method, still less to flag what many take to be the looser links with a looser verb. Matthew’s citations are sorted, graded, and self-aware — which is the opposite of arbitrariness. Typology, on this map, is one clearly bounded category among several, with its own warrant and its own honest limits.

Objection — “Matthew took Hosea out of context”

Start where the charge bites hardest. Matthew says the flight to Egypt was “to fulfill” Hosea’s “Out of Egypt I called my son” (Matthew 2:15). In Hosea the verse is plainly about the past — Israel’s exodus — and contains no future-tense verb; its “son” is the nation, not an individual; and Matthew attaches it to the flight into Egypt, while the return comes only later. Past history read as prediction, a nation turned into one man, a verse placed at the wrong moment — and it has been made in print: in 1961 S. Vernon McCasland titled his article, bluntly, “Matthew Twists the Scriptures.”

Reply

Matthew is not mistaking Hosea for a forecast, because his “fulfill” is wider than prediction. Plēroō often means “filled full”: Jesus came to “fulfill” the Law (Matthew 5:17), and commandments are not predictions. The “son” in Hosea is Israel — “Israel is my firstborn son” (Exodus 4:22) — and that is exactly why Matthew chose the verse: he tells Jesus’ early life as Israel’s story walked again, out of Egypt, through the waters, forty days in the wilderness answered from Deuteronomy, faithful where Israel faltered. Hosea 11 itself closes with a promised return from Egypt (11:10–11), and Israel’s own prophets read the first exodus as the pattern of a greater one. Honest limit: this shows Matthew reads in context and coherently; it does not, by itself, prove to a skeptic that Jesus is the one in whom Israel’s story reaches its goal. That case begins, as it always does here, with the resurrection.

The room: David Instone-Brewer’s study of the earliest datable Jewish scribal exegesis argues its interpreters, before AD 70, assumed Scripture coherent and read it with its context; Klyne Snodgrass, surveying the New Testament’s own practice, reaches a kindred verdict. The point should not be overstated — some New Testament citations are highly compressed, and the question is a live one among specialists — but the fourfold way of sorting Matthew’s citations (direct, typical, applicational, summary) is a standard one, following discussions in R. T. France and Craig Blomberg.
Defending Premise 2

Its method is contextual, not atomistic — and the forward-leaning reading at its heart was not imposed from outside, because the Hebrew Scriptures already read themselves forward.

Precision check: the charge imagines the apostles lifting verses out of the settings that gave them meaning. But Matthew’s care has a historical setting, and the forward-leaning reading he performs was native to the texts themselves. Two things carry this premise: the earliest datable Jewish exegesis, and the Hebrew Bible’s own habit of reading its promises as still reaching ahead.

The earliest datable Jewish exegesis A contextual habit

David Instone-Brewer’s study of the earliest datable Jewish scribal exegesis argues that its interpreters, before AD 70, characteristically assumed Scripture was coherent and was to be read with its context, not atomised into isolated slogans; the free, proof-texting manner often imagined for this period is in large part a later development.

The New Testament’s own practice Attends to context

Klyne Snodgrass, surveying the New Testament’s own practice, reaches a kindred verdict: its authors attend to the context of what they cite far more often than the caricature allows.

Honest limit: the point should not be overstated — some New Testament citations are highly compressed, and the question is a live one among specialists. The headline holds: Matthew’s method is describable and open to inspection, not a conjuror’s trick.

The Old Testament already reads itself forward Native to the texts

When Jacob blesses Judah — “the scepter shall not depart from Judah … until tribute comes to him” (Genesis 49:10) — Ezekiel is widely read as taking up the same unfulfilled expectation against a toppled crown (Ezekiel 21:27). And the Pentateuch can be read as closing with a glance past its own greatest prophet: Moses had promised “a prophet like me” (Deuteronomy 18:15), yet the book’s final lines record that “there has not arisen a prophet since in Israel like Moses” (Deuteronomy 34:10) — the promise left open, its fulfillment still outstanding. This is what scholars call inner-biblical exegesis (Michael Fishbane): later writers within the Hebrew Bible reading earlier promises as still reaching forward. One need not settle here what each text finally refers to; the narrower point is a recognized feature of the texts themselves.

Objection — “The forward reading was read back in after the fact”

Even grant the four kinds, the critic may press: the forward-looking reading itself was imposed on these texts only after Christians already believed in Jesus. The Hebrew Scriptures meant what they meant to the people who wrote and treasured them; reading them as “still reaching ahead” is a Christian overlay.

Reply

But the Hebrew Scriptures already read themselves this way, long before any Christian. Ezekiel takes up Jacob’s unfulfilled scepter-oracle; the Pentateuch closes with Moses’s promised prophet still outstanding. This forward-leaning, “this is not yet fulfilled” habit of reading is native to the Hebrew Scriptures themselves, not a lens Christians fitted over them from outside — which is exactly the point inner-biblical exegesis documents. One need not settle what each text finally refers to; the narrower point is a recognized feature of the texts themselves.

Objection — “You can find patterns anywhere” (parallelomania) Hardest

The strongest form of the worry has a name: parallelomania, put into circulation by the Jewish scholar Samuel Sandmel in 1961 (a term he said he had not coined but found in an earlier work). Give a clever reader any two texts and a devotional motive, the critic says, and patterns will appear — so the whole enterprise of finding Jesus in the Old Testament is pattern-matching read back in after the fact.

Reply

We grant that patterns can be over-read, and deny the inference. A method that can find Jesus everywhere has found him nowhere in particular. That is exactly why responsible reading uses controls: the strongest types are ones the New Testament authors themselves draw (Adam, the Passover lamb, Melchizedek, the tabernacle); a genuine type shows correspondence plus escalation, not mere resemblance; and it belongs to the one unfolding story of redemption. We flag which readings are apostolic and which are later devotion, and lean chiefly on the former. (The full case for typology is the business of the companion essay; here the point is that the method is disciplined, not arbitrary.)

The room: What is not seriously disputed is that the New Testament authors themselves read this way, deliberately and pervasively. The question is how far to follow them, not whether they did it. Instone-Brewer and Snodgrass carry the contextual-method point; Fishbane documents the inner-biblical reading; Sandmel named the parallelomania danger, and Christians should own the warning.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: “you can find patterns anywhere” — Sandmel’s parallelomania, that a method which can prove anything proves nothing. The deep dive answers it — take it with you, not just the reply below.

"You just rip verses out of context and call it prophecy" Hard

Strongest form

The New Testament writers comb the Hebrew Scriptures for anything that can be made to sound like Jesus, lift verses out of the settings that gave them meaning, override what they plainly meant, and dress the result up as “prophecy.” If that is what the apostles were doing, the whole structure of “fulfillment” is a conjuring trick.

Reply

It is a charge about method, so meet it on method. “Quoting the Old Testament” is not one move but at least four, and Matthew keeps them distinct — direct prediction (Micah 5:2), typology (Hosea 11:1), applied analogy (Jeremiah 31:15, flagged with the looser “then was fulfilled”), and summary (“a Nazarene”). The word “fulfill” (plēroō) is itself wider than “predict.” The method is contextual, not atomistic — the earliest datable Jewish exegesis read Scripture with its context (Instone-Brewer, Snodgrass) — and the forward-leaning reading was not imposed from outside, because Israel’s own Scriptures already read themselves forward. Its use of the Old is describable, graded, and self-aware — not a conjuror’s trick. Honest limit: this shows the New Testament reads in context; it does not by itself prove Jesus — that case rests on the resurrection.

"Matthew took Hosea 11:1 out of context" Common

Strongest form

Hosea 11:1 — “out of Egypt I called my son” — is about Israel’s past exodus, has no future-tense verb, means the nation not one man, and Matthew even attaches it to the flight into Egypt rather than the return. That is quote-mining (McCasland, “Matthew Twists the Scriptures,” 1961).

Reply

The objection is right about Hosea’s grammar; it is wrong about what Matthew is claiming. First, Matthew’s “fulfill” is wider than prediction: plēroō often means “filled full” — Jesus came to “fulfill” the Law (Matthew 5:17), and commandments are not predictions. Second, the “son” in Hosea is Israel (Exodus 4:22) — which is exactly why Matthew chose the verse: he tells Jesus’ early life as Israel’s story walked again, out of Egypt, through the waters, forty days in the wilderness answered from Deuteronomy, faithful where Israel faltered. Third, Hosea 11 itself closes with a promised return from Egypt (11:10–11); Israel’s own prophets read the first exodus as the pattern of a greater one. Honest limit: this shows Matthew reads in context and coherently; it does not by itself prove Jesus is the one in whom Israel’s story reaches its goal — that case begins with the resurrection.

The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

What is not seriously disputed is that the New Testament authors themselves read the Old this way — deliberately and pervasively. The question is how far to follow them, not whether they did it. Among specialists the fourfold sorting of Matthew’s citations (direct, typical, applicational, summary) is a standard one, and the contextual character of the earliest Jewish exegesis is well documented (Instone-Brewer, Snodgrass).

A fairness most defenders skip: the point should not be overstated. Some New Testament citations really are highly compressed, and how far the writers attend to context in a given case is a live question among specialists, not a settled triviality. The headline survives honest statement of that: Matthew’s method is describable and open to inspection.

The bounded verdict: the method charge fails — “Christians just rip verses out of context and call it prophecy” cannot survive a close look at what the New Testament actually does. But keep the verdict bounded. This does not show that every citation is a strict prediction fulfilled to the letter; and showing that Matthew reads in context is not yet showing that Jesus is the one in whom Israel’s story reaches its goal — that case rests on the resurrection. The forward-looking predictions are the business of the companion essay on messianic prophecy; this one establishes something narrower and still substantial: the New Testament’s reading of the Old is describable, graded, and self-aware.

    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    This is the application category, and Matthew signals it himself. Over the murder of the infants he does not even use his usual purpose-formula “so that it might be fulfilled” (hina plērōthē); he writes “then was fulfilled” (tote eplērōthē) — a shift many read as marking the looser bond of an analogy applied, not a claimed prediction. He is setting a fresh grief against an old one: Jeremiah’s lament for exiled Israel answers to Bethlehem’s mothers. And he chose a chapter whose very next breath is a promise of return and hope (Jeremiah 31:16–17). So this is not quote-mining a sad line; it is one of the four distinct, flagged ways Matthew uses the Old — and the looser verb is him telling you which kind it is. Honest limit: an applied analogy claims less than a prediction, and the case for Jesus never rested on it; it rests on the resurrection.
