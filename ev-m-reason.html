The Argument from Reason

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and keep its conditional shape clear when challenged.

The argument in brief

The argument from reason, developed by C.S. Lewis and sharpened by philosopher Alvin Plantinga, asks a probing question: if our minds are nothing but the product of blind physical forces, why should we trust them? On strict naturalism, every belief you hold is the end result of physics, chemistry, and natural selection — processes that have no interest in truth as such, only in survival and reproduction. But a process selecting for survival is not the same as a process aimed at producing true beliefs; a mind could be well-adapted yet systematically mistaken.

Here is the sting in the tail. If our reasoning is fully explained by non-rational causes, then the naturalist cannot, without strain, both explain reason as a byproduct of non-rational causes and rely on it to establish naturalism itself. The argument is a problem for naturalism, not for reason: it does not say reason is unreliable. The reasonable move is to reject naturalism, not reason. Our reliable rational faculties make better sense if they derive from a rational Mind than from mindless atoms.

Naturalists reply that true beliefs generally aid survival. Plantinga’s rejoinder: on naturalism a belief drives behaviour through its neurophysiology, not its meaning — and naturalism has not yet shown how content-blind selection yields truth-tracking minds. Critics such as Fitelson and Sober argue his probability claims rest on a flawed treatment of conditional probability, Plantinga replies, and the exchange continues without a knockout. Our verdict, chastened to its proper size: on this score theism explains the trustworthiness of reason better than naturalism does. Note the scope: this argument reaches a rational Ground for our reasoning — not yet the Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Identifying that being with the God revealed in Christ is the work of the other arguments, of revelation and, above all, of the historical evidence for the resurrection (see The Minimal Facts Argument).

Before you start · key terms

Eight terms this argument turns on

Learn these first — above all ground vs cause and the defeater, and the Anscombe distinction that keeps the argument honest.

The Argument from ReasonNot a proof of God but a problem for naturalism: can the naturalist’s story of where the mind came from survive being applied to the very reasoning that produced it?
Ground vs causeLewis’s key distinction. A belief can be caused by prior events; reasoning needs it to be held because of its logical ground — because the premises actually support it. Atoms pushing atoms know nothing of logic.
NaturalismReality is exhaustively physical; the mind is the product of causes that are not themselves rational — physics plus unguided evolution.
Survival, not truthNatural selection rewards adaptive behaviour; the content of a belief is, evolutionarily, along for the ride. A mind can be well-adapted yet systematically mistaken.
EAANPlantinga’s Evolutionary Argument Against Naturalism — the rigorous, probabilistic cousin: the probability that our faculties are reliable, given naturalism + evolution, is low or inscrutable.
DefeaterThe upshot: a reason to doubt everything one’s faculties produce — naturalism included. So naturalism is self-defeating to hold, not self-contradictory.
Irrational vs non-rational (Anscombe)A merely non-rational causal history of a belief does not make it irrational. Reasons and causes can both be true at different levels — her 1948 reply forced Lewis to revise, and the revised version is the one in view.
Rational ground of mindWhat the argument points toward: minds are better explained as deriving from a Mind than from mindless atoms — not yet the triune God of worship.
The argument, formally

Three lines that turn naturalism on itself.

P1

If naturalism is true, our beliefs are wholly the products of causes that are not themselves rational — physics, chemistry, and selection for survival, not truth.

P2

Then the naturalist has a defeater for trusting his own faculties — and so for every belief they produce, naturalism included.

∴ C

So naturalism is self-defeating to hold; reason is better explained as deriving from a rational Mind than from mindless causes.

The argument is a problem for naturalism, not for reason: it does not say reason is unreliable, but that the naturalist cannot, without strain, both explain reason as a byproduct of non-rational causes and rely on it. So the conclusion is to reject naturalism, not reason. Keep that straight and the it-is-self-refuting objection dissolves.

From the arguer to a rational Ground

Don’t read the conclusion — reason to it

Turn the naturalist’s own act of arguing back on his account of the mind, then reveal — and note exactly how far it reaches, and how far it does not.

The naturalist argues “all is matter in motion” and expects you to follow the logic. In doing so, what is he trusting?
his own mind to track truth — a belief held because of its ground, not just pushed by atoms
On naturalism, what produced that mind?
non-rational causes — physics + evolution selecting for survival, not truth
Flee the tiger because it’s dangerous, or because you think it’s a friendly cat best petted by running away — which does selection reward?
the fleeing (behaviour), not the accuracy — the content is along for the ride
So what is the probability the faculties reliably track truth, given naturalism + evolution?
low or inscrutable — a defeater for every belief they produce, naturalism included
How far does the argument reach?
a problem for naturalism, pointing to a rational ground of mind — not a proof, and not yet the triune God

Honestly stated: even at its most successful this is not a direct proof of God — it is a problem for naturalism. What it points toward is that reason is more plausibly grounded in a rational source than a mindless one — a reason to prefer theism over naturalism on this score, not a demonstration of the God of Abraham; it says nothing of incarnation, resurrection, or covenant, and does not by itself rule out every non-naturalist alternative. It works best as a defeater-style consideration and one strand in a cumulative case, held with confidence rather than certainty, resting above all on the historical evidence for the resurrection.

Defending the premises

Why each premise is more plausible than its denial

The two premises run two ways: P1 is naturalism’s own account of the mind; P2 is the defeater that follows (Lewis’s self-defeat and Plantinga’s probabilistic EAAN). The sharpest objections to each are answered in the bank below.

P1 — on naturalism, the mind is a product of non-rational causes Naturalism’s own commitment

This is not something the theist imposes; it is what naturalism says. Reality is exhaustively physical, and the mind is the terminal link in chains of physics, chemistry and unguided selection — a process that rewards survival-conducive behaviour, not true belief. Even the atheist geneticist J. B. S. Haldane felt the force: “If my mental processes are determined wholly by the motions of atoms in my brain, I have no reason to suppose that my beliefs are true.”

P2 — then the naturalist has a defeater for his own faculties The load-bearing premise

Two forms. Lewis: reasoning requires a belief be held because of its logical ground, not merely pushed by prior causes that know nothing of logic; if naturalism reduces all believing to event-causation, that is never secured — so the very thought “naturalism is true” is just another caused event. Plantinga’s EAAN: selection sees behaviour, not content, and many false belief-contents yield the same survival behaviour (his Paul flees the tiger believing it a friendly cat best petted by fleeing) — so the probability our faculties are reliable, given naturalism + evolution, is low or inscrutable: a defeater for every belief, naturalism included.

Honest narrowing: this is genuinely contested. Elizabeth Anscombe’s 1948 reply — that a merely non-rational cause is not an irrational one — forced Lewis to substantially revise the chapter (1960); the revised cause/ground version is the one in view. And the EAAN is disputed: Fitelson and Sober press Plantinga’s conditional-probability treatment, and teleosemantics aims to make belief-content causally relevant after all. Plantinga replies; the exchange continues without a knockout.

The room: the lineage runs Balfour (1895) and the atheist J. B. S. Haldane (1927, who later retracted it) through C. S. Lewis (Miracles, the first sustained form) and Victor Reppert’s rigorous reconstruction, to Alvin Plantinga’s EAAN. The critics are serious: Elizabeth Anscombe (the famous 1948 reply), and Branden Fitelson and Elliott Sober on the probabilities (Pacific Philosophical Quarterly, 1998; the Beilby-edited Naturalism Defeated?, 2002, collects the other critics with Plantinga’s replies). There is no scholarly consensus either way; the exchange is live.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these in its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged below. Watch how often the conditional shape of the argument does the work.

The one that matters most: Anscombe’s 1948 reply, which made Lewis rewrite the chapter — and Fitelson and Sober on Plantinga’s probabilities. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"A full causal story doesn’t make a belief irrational" Hard

Strongest form

Elizabeth Anscombe’s 1948 reply to Lewis: there is a difference between an irrational cause of a belief and a merely non-rational one. Giving a full causal history of how a belief arose does not, by itself, impugn its validity. As Anscombe put it, if a man has reasons, and they are good reasons, and they are genuinely his reasons for thinking something, then his thinking is rational — whatever further causal statements we might truly make about the neurons underneath. Naturalistic causation and rational justification might operate at different levels of description, both true at once, the way a sentence can be both a sequence of ink-marks and a valid argument.

Reply

The critique had real teeth; Lewis substantially revised the chapter (1960) around the cause/ground distinction, a revision widely regarded as an improvement, Anscombe herself among those who thought so. The revised argument, carried forward by Reppert and Plantinga, asks how a content-blind process produced minds whose beliefs are governed by their grounds — and that debt has not been paid.

"Evolution selects for true beliefs" Common

Strongest form

Accurate perception and reasoning obviously aid survival — creatures with false beliefs get eaten — so selection favours reliable cognition.

Reply

Selection acts on behaviour, and on a naturalist picture a belief causes behaviour through its neurophysiological properties, not its meaning. Plantinga’s Paul thinks the tiger is a friendly cat he wants to pet and believes the way to pet it is to run away: adaptive behaviour, false beliefs. So the probability that the contents are largely true stays low or inscrutable. Fitelson and Sober contest those probabilities and the exchange continues — but naturalism has not yet shown how content-blind selection yields truth-tracking minds.

"This is self-refuting" Trickier

Strongest form

You used reasoning to argue that reasoning is unreliable — so the argument defeats itself.

Reply

It does not say reason is unreliable. It says the naturalist cannot, without strain, both explain reason as a byproduct of non-rational causes and rely on it. The conclusion is to reject naturalism, precisely so we can keep trusting the reason we cannot help relying on. It targets naturalism, not rationality.

"Reasons just are physical causes" Hard

Strongest form

Mental states are brain states; a reason can be a cause. Because-it-is-true and because-of-physics describe the same event at different levels — no conflict.

Reply

But if the physical level does all the causal work, the rational level is epiphenomenal — along for the ride, doing nothing. Then you hold a conclusion because of neural causes. Truth drops out of the explanation, which is exactly the problem.

"Computers reason without souls" Trickier

Strongest form

Machines perform valid inference physically, which shows reason needs no immaterial mind.

Reply

Computers manipulate symbols whose meaning and validity we assign — their reasoning is borrowed from rational designers. They shuffle tokens without grasping truth (Searle’s Chinese Room). Derived, observer-relative rationality does not explain original, truth-grasping rationality.

"Faculties can just be reliable" Hard

Strongest form

Our faculties could simply be reliable as a brute fact, warranting our beliefs without any theistic story.

Reply

The argument grants they might be reliable — but holds that naturalism plus evolution gives no reason to expect it and, Plantinga argues, makes the probability of reliability low or inscrutable: a defeater the naturalist must answer. Theism supplies the missing reason to expect reliability; bare naturalism has not yet shown how content-blind selection yields truth-tracking minds.

"This is god-of-the-gaps for the mind" Common

Strongest form

Appealing to God to ground reason is just filling an explanatory gap.

Reply

It is not a gap in data; it is a structural problem — it leaves naturalism owing an account of how a content-blind process produced minds that track truth, and that debt has not been paid. It is an inference to the best explanation of a feature naturalism struggles to account for in principle, not a placeholder for future science.

The honest state of the scholarship

Where the argument really stands

It would be a failure of gentleness and respect to pretend the matter is settled. It is not. The EAAN is contested, and many capable philosophers — theist and naturalist alike — reject it. Branden Fitelson and Elliott Sober argue that Plantinga’s probability claims rest on a flawed treatment of conditional probability; others contend that, on the right theory of mind, semantic content just is causally efficacious. Plantinga replies in turn, and the exchange continues without a knockout on either side.

The real fork: whether a fully naturalistic account of mental content can be given on which content is causally relevant and tied to the world (dissolving the defeater), or whether a content-blind physical process simply cannot produce truth-tracking minds. That is where the live debate sits. (A footnote worth knowing: the legend that Anscombe’s reply so crushed Lewis that he gave up apologetics for children’s fiction is largely debunked — resist a good story the facts do not bear.)

The bounded verdict: even at its most successful the argument is not a direct proof of God — it is a problem for naturalism, a defeater-style consideration and one strand in a cumulative case. What it points toward is that reason is more plausibly grounded in a rational source than a mindless one. Our verdict, chastened to its proper size: on this score theism explains the trustworthiness of reason better than naturalism does, and the argument retains its power to provoke — it asks the most committed skeptic to turn his skepticism on the instrument of skepticism itself. Held with confidence rather than certainty, resting above all on the historical evidence for the resurrection.

    Gold gate · the unseen objection

    One you weren’t coached on

    Everything above rehearsed objections the page showed you. Here is the real test: the deepest contemporary naturalist reply — a theory of meaning designed to close the gap the argument exploits. Reason it out cold, then reveal how the argument handles it. An honest “nailed it” is the first half of Gold.

    NP
    Naturalist philosopher
    "Your whole argument assumes meaning floats free of the physics — 'invisible to selection.' But give a naturalistic theory of content: teleosemantics says a belief's content just is what it was selected to track in the environment. Then content is causally relevant and world-tied, selection can 'see' it after all, and your defeater simply dissolves. No God required."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    This is the real frontier, and the honest theist takes it seriously: if a fully naturalistic account of content can be given on which content is causally relevant and tied to the world, then the inference from “selection sees only behaviour” to “content is probably false” fails, and the defeater dissolves. That is exactly what teleosemantics aims to make good. The theist’s reply is not to deny the project’s difficulty but to point at its cost: the more you insist that semantic content is robustly real and genuinely causal, the harder it becomes to see how a purely physical, content-blind process could have produced and sustained it — which is the original worry returning in a different key. The argument is not refuted; but naturalism still owes an account of how a content-blind process produced minds that track truth, and that debt has not been paid.
