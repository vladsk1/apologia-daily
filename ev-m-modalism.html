Answering Modalism

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and stay clear and calm in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

Modalism (also called Sabellianism or, in one modern form, Oneness theology) tries to protect the oneness of God by saying that Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are not three distinct persons but three modes or roles that the one divine person manifests: anciently pictured as successive, today (in Oneness theology) as manifestations of the one omnipresent God, even all at once. Its motive, jealousy for monotheism, is not a vice — it is half of the truth the Trinity protects.

The historic Christian reply is that modalism cannot make sense of the biblical data. The persons relate to one another as genuinely distinct: the Father sends the Son, the Son prays to the Father, the Father speaks from heaven while the Son is baptised and the Spirit descends (Matthew 3:16-17). If they were merely the same person in different modes, these would be God talking to himself or sending himself — and the Son's intercession for us would be a performance rather than a relationship.

The Trinity threads the needle modalism misses. God is genuinely one in being, so monotheism is safe; yet genuinely three in person, so the love, sending, and conversation among Father, Son, and Spirit are real. Orthodoxy affirms the truth modalism is reaching for — one God — without erasing the distinctions Scripture plainly shows. Modalism is a serious error, as the historic church has judged.

The argument, formally

One actor, three masks — until all three are on stage at once.

Pastoral edge: modalism's instinct — jealousy for monotheism — is not a vice; honour it before correcting it. The Trinity protects the same oneness while keeping the love between Father and Son that modalism spends.

The key question — in Gethsemane, who was listening?

Every prayer of Jesus is the theory's crisis: if Father and Son are one person, the praying is either theater (a performance with no listener) or pathology (a man addressing himself in agony). Keep the prayers real and you have two who commune — which is the doctrine.

Defending Premise 1

Modalism (today's Oneness Pentecostalism) teaches that Father, Son and Spirit are one person in three modes or roles — anciently pictured as successive (Father in creation, Son in redemption, Spirit in the church), and in Oneness theology today as simultaneous manifestations of the one omnipresent God.

Precision check: modalism is the teaching that God is a single divine person who is revealed in three modes, roles, or manifestations. On either view there are not three who are God; there is one who is God, appearing under three titles. The motive should be stated accurately: modalism is trying to guard the oneness of God. That instinct — jealousy for monotheism — is not a vice; it is half of the truth the doctrine of the Trinity is built to protect.

The ancient forms — Sabellius, Noetus, Praxeas Known mostly through opponents

In the early centuries it was called monarchianism (from the concern to protect God’s sole monarchia, his single rule), and more specifically modalistic monarchianism or Sabellianism, after Sabellius, a teacher active in Rome in the early third century. Noetus of Smyrna and a figure named Praxeas taught, according to the church fathers, that the Father himself was born of the virgin and suffered on the cross. Because it makes the Father the one who suffers on the cross — there being no distinct Son to do the suffering — it also earned the mocking label Patripassianism: “the Father suffers.”

Honest limit: we know the early modalists mostly through their opponents, which calls for some caution about details.

Modern Oneness Pentecostalism Documented in its own standard statement

Modalism is not merely a museum piece. Its largest modern form is Oneness Pentecostalism, which emerged out of the “New Issue” controversy among American Pentecostals around 1913–1916. Its most influential systematic theologian is David K. Bernard of the United Pentecostal Church International, whose book The Oneness of God (1983) is the standard scholarly statement of the position. Bernard affirms one God, the absolute deity of Jesus Christ, and rejects the charge that he denies the threeness of biblical language. His claim is that Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are real and meaningful — but as “manifestations, modes, offices, or relationships” of the one God, not as three distinct persons. Crucially, Bernard locates the deity of the Son in the indwelling Father: “the Son” refers to God-as-incarnate, while the prayers and submissions of Jesus express the relationship of his genuine humanity to the deity dwelling in him.

Objection — “You are attacking a caricature; Oneness affirms the full deity of Jesus”

Oneness theology is more nuanced than its caricature. Bernard affirms one God, the absolute deity of Jesus Christ, and the reality of Father, Son and Spirit in biblical language. It is held by many people who are sincere and devout.

Reply

It should be engaged, not sneered at (1 Peter 3:15) — engaged, that is, as the serious error the rest of this case shows it to be. Both sides confess one God and call Jesus God — but Oneness locates the deity of the Son in the indwelling Father, so that “the Son” refers to God-as-incarnate. The real question is whether the one God is a single person or three coequal, coeternal persons who share one undivided being. Both sides call Jesus God in different senses, and modalism is a serious error, as the historic church has judged.

The room: Bernard states the Oneness position at its scholarly best; Tertullian (Against Praxeas, c. AD 210–213) gave the decisive early reply, and Sabellius was excommunicated at Rome around AD 220; there is real and respectful scholarly engagement between trinitarian and Oneness Pentecostal theologians — the Oneness–Trinitarian Pentecostal Dialogue (2002–2007) and critical assessments by Gregory Boyd, a former Oneness believer.
Defending Premise 2

Scripture stages simultaneous, interpersonal relations the theory cannot survive: at the baptism the Son stands in the water while the Father speaks from heaven and the Spirit descends; the Son prays TO the Father (Gethsemane, John 17) — real address to one who is really other, the incarnate Son's human will yielded to the Father's ('not my will but yours'); the Father SENDS, the Son ASKS, and 'ANOTHER Advocate' comes; the Father and Son are TWO witnesses (John 8:17-18); and the Son shared glory WITH the Father before the world existed (John 17:5), loved before the foundation of the world (17:24).

Precision check: the real question is whether the one God is a single person or three coequal, coeternal persons who share one undivided being. The texts that resist modalism are not a verse or two but the structure of the New Testament itself. The claim is not that there are three gods — the persons share one undivided divine essence, one will, one power.

Three present at once — the baptism Strong

Matthew 3:16-17 is staged against modalism: the Son emerges from the water, the Spirit descends AS A DOVE upon him, the Father's voice addresses him — 'this is my beloved Son.' Three simultaneous actors; one of them speaking ABOUT another TO bystanders. A single actor can change masks between scenes; he cannot wear all three at once and converse across them. Successive modes cannot share one scene; persons can act together.

Oneness reply, answered: modern Oneness theology replies that the omnipresent God can manifest himself in heaven, in the dove, and in the man Jesus at once. But omnipresence explains how one person can be in three places, not how he can address himself: one person cannot, in the same moment, speak from heaven to another as “my beloved Son” and be the Son who is spoken of — three in relationship, not one in three places. And manifestation cannot absorb John 17: the glory the Son “had with you before the world existed” (John 17:5) was not a manifestation to anyone.

The Son prays to the Father Strong

The deepest evidence is the prayer life of Jesus, of which Gethsemane is the sharpest case. “Not as I will, but as you will” (Matthew 26:39) is a real will yielded to another real will — the incarnate Son, a person truly distinct from the Father, genuinely submits. This is not two separate divine wills, still less two gods: the one God has one undivided will; in Gethsemane it is the Son’s genuine human will freely yielding to the divine will he eternally shares with the Father. The decisive answer is the high-priestly prayer of John 17, because it reaches behind the incarnation altogether. There the petitioner asks for “the glory that I had with you before the world existed” (John 17:5), and says “you loved me before the foundation of the world” (John 17:24).

Honest limit: the phrase “before the foundation of the world” in 17:24 is used elsewhere of God’s foreknowing purpose — Ephesians 1:4; 1 Peter 1:20 — so the weight rests on 17:5. Jesus does not say he was foreknown; he says he had glory “with you” before the world existed — a shared possession, not a place in a plan.

The Father bears witness to the Son Strong

John 8:17-18 — Jesus invokes the law requiring TWO witnesses and counts: 'I am one who bears witness about myself, and the Father who sent me bears witness about me.' One person in two modes is, by Jesus' own cited law, ONE witness — the argument only works if they are really two. (If the reply is that the two are Jesus’ deity and his humanity, the law Jesus invokes counts the testimony of two people — and natures do not testify; persons do.) The Father also speaks to the Son and of the Son — at the transfiguration, and at “Father, glorify your name,” answered by a voice from heaven (John 12:28).

The Spirit sent by Father and Son Supporting · allos not decisive alone

Jesus promises “another Helper” (John 14:16). The Greek is allos, which often (though not always, in New Testament Greek) means another of the same kind — and the context bears that out: the Spirit teaches, reminds, and bears witness as Jesus did (John 14:26; 15:26). The Son will ask the Father, and the Father will give the Spirit; later the Son himself sends the Spirit “from the Father” (John 15:26). A king does not petition himself to send himself, and a mode is not “another” Helper at all. The grammar of sending presupposes a sender and a sent who are distinct.

Objection — baptism in Jesus’ name alone (Acts 2:38) Hardest

Jesus commands baptism “in the name” — singular — “of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit” (Matthew 28:19). Yet the apostles, who heard that command, baptized “in the name of Jesus Christ” (Acts 2:38; cf. 8:16; 10:48; 19:5). For Bernard the conclusion is irresistible: the single name behind the three titles is Jesus, because Father, Son, and Spirit are one God whose saving name is Jesus.

Reply

The Oneness case leans hardest on this text, so it needs a full answer. “In the name of Jesus Christ” in Acts is most naturally read not as a liturgical formula competing with Matthew 28:19 but as a description of Christian baptism — baptism into allegiance to Jesus as Lord, marking converts off from John’s baptism and from the synagogue. Luke records varied wording (“in the name of the Lord Jesus,” “in the name of Jesus Christ”), which tells against a fixed verbal formula. And the inference Oneness draws — that Father, Son, and Spirit are therefore one person — simply does not follow: that three share one name can equally express the unity of three persons in one God, which is how the church has always read Jesus’ striking singular “in the name” in Matthew 28:19.

Objection — Gethsemane is the human nature speaking to the deity within

The Oneness reply is that the genuine human nature of Jesus is here addressing the deity indwelling him — flesh speaking to the Spirit within a single person.

Reply

The one praying in John 17 speaks of a relationship that obtained before any human nature existed to do the addressing — so the petitioner cannot be the human nature speaking to the indwelling deity; he is the pre-existent divine Son, asking the Father to restore a shared glory the two of them knew before creation. Love between Father and Son before the world began is not a manifestation to anyone; it is eternal interpersonal fact — the very thing a mode can never be. Oneness teachers reply that this glory existed only in God’s plan. But Jesus does not say he was foreknown; he says he had glory “with you” before the world existed (John 17:5) — a shared possession, not a place in a plan.

The room: Bernard presses the Oneness reading from Colossians 2:9, Isaiah 9:6, John 10:30, John 14:9 and Acts 2:38; Tertullian and the historic church answer from the simultaneity and address in the text. Within orthodox theology there are vigorous debates about how to state the doctrine: Cornelius Plantinga and others defend social-trinitarian language of three “centers of consciousness,” while Brian Leftow and Karen Kilby argue that overemphasizing the distinctness of the persons concedes too much to the modalist’s complaint.
Defending the conclusion

Therefore the persons are really distinct from eternity — masks do not converse, submit, send, witness, or love one another — and modalism, in saving the oneness, loses the gospel's central relationship. Modalism is a serious error, as the historic church has judged.

Precision check: the verdict is the historic church’s, and it is bounded. Among historians and theologians across the confessional spectrum — Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant — there is no live dispute that modalism falls outside Nicene orthodoxy; that judgment was settled in the third and fourth centuries and has never been seriously reopened. Modalism holds fast to one true thing — that there is one God — and any answer that loses that truth, or the full deity of Jesus Christ, has answered badly.

Eternal love Strong

‘God is love’ (1 John 4:8), and the Father loved the Son before the foundation of the world (John 17:24), in the glory the Son had with him before the world existed (John 17:5): the Trinity lets those texts stand together, so that God’s love neither waits on creation nor divides the God who is one; modalism has no eternal Beloved to point to. On modalism, the relationship that salvation invites us INTO — the Son's communion with the Father — was never eternal.

Adoption into communion Strong

The salvation the gospel offers is precisely adoption into that communion — to be brought, by the Spirit, into the Son’s relationship with the Father (Romans 8:15–17; John 17:21). On the modalist account there is no eternal Father–Son communion to be brought into. In saving the oneness, modalism spends the relationship that the gospel exists to share.

The cross Supporting

Who was the Son forsaken BY? To whom did he commit his spirit? Who accepted the sacrifice he offered? Modalism makes Calvary a soliloquy — the Father-ward face of the atonement disappears.

Objection — “Three persons who love and send one another are three gods”

Be honest — three persons who love one another, send one another, and submit to one another are three gods by any working definition. Oneness took the Shema seriously; you embroidered it. Your “distinct persons” always slides, in practice, into tritheism. This is a serious objection, and it must not be waved away. Its ablest contemporary exponent, Dale Tuggy, presses a version of it not toward Oneness modalism but toward a strict unitarianism in which God is the Father alone — which is a reminder that whatever answers the “three gods” charge must not, in doing so, collapse the three back into one person.

Reply

The reply turns on what actually makes for many gods. Polytheism counts gods by separable essences — Zeus and Poseidon are two because they have two distinct natures, two wills, divisible turf, and can act against one another. The persons of the Trinity have one undivided divine essence, one will, one power; nothing is parceled out among them. This is why the church rejected tritheism wherever it appeared, and why the Athanasian Creed insists on “neither confounding the persons, nor dividing the substance.” The Son is not a second instance of deity; he is the one God, eternally the Son. To refute modalism by drifting into tritheism would simply trade one error for its opposite — and the same Scripture that forbids collapsing the persons forbids dividing the being. The reply here answers the tritheism charge, not the further philosophical puzzle of how one being is three persons.

The room: the Trinity does not dissolve the mystery. The doctrine states the data — one God, three distinct persons, full equality — and rules out the failed solutions (modalism on one side, tritheism and Arian subordinationism on the other); it does not pretend to comprehend how one being is three persons. The analogies all limp: water’s three states are actually modalism (the same water, usually one at a time); three men sharing humanity are actually tritheism. The orthodox confess a reality that exceeds every illustration, and they should say so plainly.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: baptism in Jesus’ name alone (Acts 2:38) — the Oneness reading’s strongest text. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"I and the Father are one — and whoever has seen me has seen the Father; Jesus IS the Father" Common

Strongest form

John 10:30 and 14:9 in plain words. Jesus did not say we are two in one essence; he said one, and seeing him is seeing the Father.

Reply

Read the grammar and the next clause: 'one' in 10:30 is NEUTER (hen) — one thing, one unity — not masculine 'one person'; and 14:9-10 explains itself: seeing the Son reveals the Father because 'I am IN the Father and the Father is IN me' — mutual indwelling of two, not identity of one. The same discourse has the Son praying to, sent by, and returning to the Father — readings must survive their own chapter.

"Isaiah 9:6 — the Son is called Everlasting FATHER; the Bible collapses the persons for you" Common

Strongest form

Your own Christmas verse names the child Everlasting Father. Modalism is on the page.

Reply

The title is 'Father of Eternity' — Semitic idiom for possessor/source: as Jabal is ‘the father of those who dwell in tents’ (Genesis 4:20) and Eliakim ‘a father to the inhabitants of Jerusalem’ (Isaiah 22:21), the Messiah is fatherly source and possessor of eternity, eternal protector of his people — a ROLE title in a list of role titles (Counselor, Prince of Peace). It names what the Son is to his people, not which person he is. Trinitarian theology has always distinguished the divine PERSONS while sharing every divine PERFECTION — eternity included.

"The prayers were his human nature addressing his divine nature — no second person needed" Trickier

Strongest form

Oneness theology answers Gethsemane: the man Jesus prayed to the divine Spirit dwelling in him. Flesh speaks to Spirit — one person throughout.

Reply

Natures do not talk — PERSONS do; 'nature' is a what, and whats neither love nor listen. And the texts refuse the split: the Son asks for the glory HE HAD with the Father before the world existed (17:5) — the divine pre-existent one is the petitioner; the Father loved HIM before creation (17:24) — before any human nature existed to do the praying. Oneness teachers reply that the glory of 17:5 existed only in God’s plan. But he does not say he was foreknown; he says he had glory “with you” (17:5) — a shared possession, not a place in a plan.

"Sent and another are functional — a king sends himself incognito; roles converse in every drama" Trickier

Strongest form

Sending language is mission vocabulary, and another Advocate just means the same God in a new operation. You are literalising stage directions.

Reply

Then the stage directions are strange: the Sender REMAINS while the Sent goes ('I am not alone; the Father is with me'); the Son returns TO the sender and sits at his RIGHT HAND — Stephen sees TWO, Jesus standing beside the Glory; the 'new operation' is called ALLOS — which often (though not always) means another of the same kind — whom the Son must ASK the Father to give. Incognito kings do not petition themselves for their own arrival, and right hands require someone whose right it is.

"Progressive revelation of one God — OT Father, Gospels Son, Acts Spirit; the modes are dispensational, not simultaneous" Hard

Strongest form

The successive-modes version dodges your baptism scene: God revealed himself BY ERAS. Simultaneous appearances are accommodations, like the burning bush — manifestations, not persons.

Reply

The eras themselves refuse the schedule: the Spirit broods over creation and fills prophets in the 'Father's' era; the Son creates all things (John 1:3; Colossians 1:16); the Father is on full audible duty in the Gospels' 'Son era' — baptism, transfiguration, John 12:28 — and the Son still intercedes in the Spirit's era (Hebrews 7:25, NOW). And accommodation cannot absorb John 17:5 — glory the Son had WITH the Father before the world existed is not a manifestation to anyone; it is eternal interpersonal fact, the one thing modes can never be.

"Trinitarians worship three Gods in practice — Oneness preserves what the Shema demands; your distinction always slides to tritheism" Hard

Strongest form

Be honest: three centers who love, send, and submit ARE three gods by any working definition. We took monotheism seriously; you embroidered it.

Reply

Working definitions matter: gods are counted by separable essences — Zeus and Poseidon, rival wills, divisible turf. The persons have ONE undivided essence, one will, one power; nothing is parceled — which is why the church rejected tritheism wherever it appeared. This answers the tritheism charge, not the further puzzle of how one being is three persons — which the doctrine does not claim to dissolve. Oneness 'preserves' the Shema by deleting the New Testament's data — the witnesses, the sending, the pre-creation love — the same data-deletion method as Arianism, opposite direction. And mark the cost once more: in saving the arithmetic you spend the gospel. The Trinity lets ‘God is love’ and the Father’s love for the Son before the world stand together; modalism has no eternal Beloved to point to — and a salvation that brings us into the Son’s communion with the Father has, on your theory, nowhere to bring us.
