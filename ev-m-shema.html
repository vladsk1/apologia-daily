The Shema and the Trinity

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and stay clear and calm in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

The Shema — "Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4) — is Judaism's central confession of the one God, prayed daily by observant Jews. It is often raised as an objection to the Trinity: if God is emphatically one, how can Christians speak of three persons? The Christian answer is that the Shema and the Trinity are not rivals. Trinitarian faith affirms the Shema wholeheartedly; there is exactly one God.

Two points carry the response. First, a word about what the Shema does not settle. A popular apologetic argues that the Hebrew word for "one" here (echad) means a composite unity, because the same word describes a husband and wife becoming "one flesh" (Genesis 2:24). Honesty requires setting that argument aside: echad is the ordinary Hebrew numeral, and whatever plurality is in "one flesh" is supplied by the noun, not by the number. What can be said is narrower — the Shema asserts YHWH's exclusive uniqueness against the gods of the nations, and does not legislate on the inner life of the God it names. Second, the earliest Christians, Jewish monotheists almost to a man, do something remarkable: in 1 Corinthians 8:6 Paul takes the Shema itself and places Jesus inside it — "one God, the Father... and one Lord, Jesus Christ" — including Jesus within the identity of the one LORD of Israel rather than adding a second god.

It is only fair to grant that traditional Jewish reading takes the Shema as strict, unitarian monotheism, and that this is a serious interpretive difference. The Christian claim is not that the Trinity is stated in the Shema, but that confessing one God leaves room for the fuller revelation that this one God is Father, Son, and Spirit. So the Shema does not rule out the Trinity. And this is what we hold to be true: the one God exists eternally as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

Before you start · key terms

Eight words this argument leans on

Learn these first — the objections below turn on them, and most weak versions of the argument come from blurring one of them.

The ShemaIsrael’s central confession, prayed twice a day by the observant Jew: “Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one” (Deuteronomy 6:4). It is the first prayer a Jewish child learns and traditionally the last words of the dying; the closest thing Judaism has to a creed. Its theme is unmistakable — there is one God, and Israel belongs to him alone.
EchadThe Hebrew word translated “one” in the Shema. Is saying: the ordinary Hebrew cardinal number, which in context can describe a unity made of parts (“one flesh”), because the plurality is supplied by the noun it modifies, not by the number. Not saying: it does NOT by itself prove plurality in God or the Trinity — the “compound unity” argument is a discredited overreach, and the echad-versus-yachid contrast cannot bear the doctrinal weight some place on it. The case for the triune God rests on divine identity and 1 Corinthians 8:6, never on this one word.
Divine identityRichard Bauckham’s phrase for how ancient Jews defined God — less by abstract metaphysics than by identity: who God is and what only God does (God alone is sole Creator of all things, God alone is sovereign Ruler of all things). To include Jesus in that identity is not to make him a second god but to confess that the one God of Israel is somehow Father and Son together. The framework is widely influential, though the divine-identity category is itself debated: Larry Hurtado reframes the same data as a pattern of devotion, and James F. McGrath disputes the category.
The split Shema (1 Corinthians 8:6)Writing to Corinth around AD 53–54 against idolatry, Paul takes the creed’s two titles for the one God — theos (“God”) and Kyrios (“Lord”) — and distributes them: “one God, the Father… and one Lord, Jesus Christ.” He has not added a second deity alongside the Shema; he has reached inside it and placed Jesus within it. This reading (Bauckham, N. T. Wright) is influential, arguably dominant among New Testament scholars, but genuinely contested, not unanimous — James F. McGrath reads “one Lord” as set alongside the creed, not inside it.
KyriosIn the Greek Shema Paul knew (the Septuagint), Kyrios is not a generic word for a master; it is the standard reverent rendering of the unspeakable divine name YHWH. That is why Paul’s pairing of the creed’s “one God” with “one Lord, Jesus Christ” is so weighty — he gives Jesus the creed’s own divine title.
The agency readingThe Jewish and Unitarian reading, drawn from the Hebrew Bible and Second Temple Judaism: an exalted intermediary — personified Wisdom, the great angels, exalted patriarchs like Enoch — can bear God’s name and do God’s work without being God, and the risen Jesus is slotted into that role of supreme agent. The motif is genuinely present, but it breaks at one point: cultic worship.
The “there is no other” formulaThe Shema’s exclusivity is stated outright elsewhere in the recurring declaration that there is no god besides YHWH — the “there is no other” (’ên ’ôd) formula of Deuteronomy 4:35, 39; 1 Kings 8:60; and Isaiah 45:5–6, 21–22 (with the parallel “there is no god beside me” of Deuteronomy 32:39) — which Bauckham treats as a defining mark of ancient Jewish monotheism.
The worship boundaryWhat Jewish monotheism guarded jealously for God was not the posture of homage — Israelites could bow before a king as well as before the LORD (1 Chronicles 29:20) — but the substance of worship: sacrifice, invocation, prayer. The angels of Second Temple Judaism refuse worship and redirect it to God (Revelation 19:10); yet the earliest Christians prayed to Jesus, were baptized in his name, sang to him, and called on him — a devotional pattern Judaism reserved for God alone (Hurtado).
The argument, formally

Israel's creed, and what a Pharisee did inside it.

P1

The Shema — 'Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one' (Deut 6:4) — asserts YHWH's exclusive unique deity over against the gods of the nations; it does not address how many persons that one God is. (The popular 'echad means compound unity' argument does not work, and is not used here.)

P2

In 1 Corinthians 8:6, Paul — a trained Pharisee — restates the Shema with Jesus inside it: 'one God, the Father, from whom are all things... and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things' — distributing the creed's own titles (theos, kyrios) between Father and Son.

∴ C

Therefore the Shema does not rule out the Trinity: on the reading we defend (arguably dominant, though contested), the earliest Christology placed Jesus inside the unique divine identity of Israel's God — monotheism kept, not abandoned.

The argument's strength is who and where: not a fourth-century council but a first-generation Pharisee, handling the most protected sentence in his religion — in a passage about avoiding idolatry.

The key concept — splitting the Shema without breaking it.

In the Greek Shema Paul knew, LORD (kyrios) and God (theos) both name the one YHWH. Paul assigns theos to the Father and kyrios to Jesus — inside a sentence that still confesses ONE. If Jesus were a second, lesser deity, the verse would be the polytheism Paul is in that very chapter condemning.

What follows — and what doesn’t

Don’t read the conclusion — reason to it

Working only from what the argument has established: what does each piece of evidence rule out, and how far does the inference reach? Reason each line out, then reveal.

Defending Premise 1

The Shema — ‘Hear, O Israel: the LORD our God, the LORD is one’ (Deut 6:4) — asserts YHWH’s exclusive unique deity over against the gods of the nations; it does not address how many persons that one God is

Precision check: classical Christianity affirms the Shema without crossing its fingers. The Nicene faith confesses “one God” — not three gods, not a committee of deities, not one God plus two junior partners. No one should claim that the Trinity is “stated” in Deuteronomy 6:4, as if Moses had a doctrine of three persons in view; that would be reading the New Testament back into a text written centuries before it. The careful claim is that the Shema confesses that God is uniquely one, and that confession does not, by itself, settle the inner life of the one God it names.

The lexical point, honestly A concession we make

Echad is the ordinary Hebrew cardinal number. The plurality in “one flesh” (Gen 2:24) or “one cluster” (Num 13:23) does not come from the word echad at all; it comes from the noun it modifies. Critics from the Jewish and Unitarian traditions have pressed exactly this point, and on this lexical point the facts are with them: echad does not prove a composite unity, and the echad-versus-yachid contrast cannot bear the doctrinal weight some place on it. The popular “compound unity” argument is a discredited overreach, and is not used here.

Honest limit: what can be said — and only this — is the negative claim: echad does not forbid internal complexity either. The word leaves a door ajar. It does not, on its own, walk anyone through it.

The creed’s target Strong

The Shema asserts that YHWH is uniquely, exclusively Israel’s God; it does not legislate on the metaphysics of how many “persons” that one God is. Its polemic is against the gods of the nations — against having any rival to YHWH — not against later questions about the divine life that no Israelite was yet asking. That exclusivity is stated outright elsewhere in the recurring declaration that there is no god besides YHWH — the “there is no other” formula of Deuteronomy 4:35, 39; 1 Kings 8:60; and Isaiah 45:5–6, 21–22.

Objection — the traditional Jewish reading: the LORD is one, without a “within”

For more than two millennia, the mainstream of Jewish interpretation has understood Deuteronomy 6:4 as strict, indivisible monotheism: the LORD is one in the plainest sense, without parts, without persons, without a “within.” On this view, the Christian doctrine is not a development of Israel’s faith but a departure from it, and the burden lies entirely on the Christian to explain why the custodians of the creed have always read it the other way.

Reply

The traditional Jewish reading of the Shema is a serious and ancient interpretation, held in good faith by people who love this text as much as any Christian — and on what the verse itself asserts, Christians agree completely: there is one God, and no other. The Christian claim is not that Jews have failed at arithmetic; it is an intra-biblical claim that the one God of the Shema has disclosed more of his life than the verse alone reveals — a claim Jewish readers are entitled to find unpersuasive. The Shema does not rule out the Trinity, and the objection built on it does not hold; but grammar alone was never going to decide the question; the case against that reading rests on what the one God has since disclosed in Christ.

The room: the lexical point is conceded by careful trinitarian scholars and pressed by Jewish and Unitarian critics alike; Jeffrey Tigay (JPS Torah Commentary: Deuteronomy) treats echad as “one”/“alone” without any compound-unity claim, and Daniel Block (JETS 47.2, 2004) reads the Shema as a statement of YHWH’s exclusivity; Richard Bauckham treats the “there is no other” formula as a defining mark of ancient Jewish monotheism.
Defending Premise 2

In 1 Corinthians 8:6, Paul — a trained Pharisee — restates the Shema with Jesus inside it (the dominant though contested reading): ‘one God, the Father… and one Lord, Jesus Christ’

Precision check: on the reading we defend — influential, arguably dominant, but genuinely contested — Paul distributes the creed’s own titles (theos, kyrios) between Father and Son. He has not added a second deity alongside the Shema; he has reached inside the Shema and placed Jesus within it. It does not show that the Trinity is taught in Deuteronomy 6:4, and it does not refute the traditional Jewish reading by force of grammar alone.

The vocabulary trail Influential · contested reading

“Yet for us there is one God, the Father, from whom are all things and for whom we exist, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things and through whom we exist” (1 Corinthians 8:6). In the Greek Old Testament Paul used, the Septuagint, the Shema reads Kyrios ho theos h&emacron;m&omacron;n Kyrios heis estin — “the LORD (Kyrios) our God (theos) is one LORD.” Two divine titles, theos and Kyrios, both naming the one God of Israel; Kyrios in particular was the standard reverent substitute for the unspeakable name YHWH. Paul takes the creed’s two words for the one God and distributes them: “one God, the Father… and one Lord, Jesus Christ.”

Honest limit: this reading is associated above all with Richard Bauckham, who calls it the “Christology of divine identity,” and with N. T. Wright. Bauckham’s central thesis is widely influential, though the divine-identity category is itself debated. The Bauckham–Wright reading is supported in their different ways by Hurtado and, more cautiously, by James D. G. Dunn; but it is genuinely contested, not unanimous. These are real disagreements among serious scholars, and the trinitarian should name them as such rather than pretend the case is closed.

The who and the where Supporting · rests on the contested reading

Paul of Tarsus was, by his own description, “a Hebrew of Hebrews; as to the law, a Pharisee” (Philippians 3:5) — a man who had recited the Shema since boyhood. Writing to the Corinthians around AD 53–54, in a chapter explicitly about the danger of idolatry, he sets the gospel against the pagan world’s “many ‘gods’ and many ‘lords.’” This is not a Gentile convert centuries later but a trained Pharisee within roughly two decades of the crucifixion. If Paul thought he were smuggling a second, lesser divine being into the picture, he would be committing in verse 6 precisely the sin he condemns in verse 5. The natural reading is that he saw no contradiction at all: one God, confessed in the creed’s own words, now known to be Father and Son.

Creation marks the line Strong

In Israel’s Scriptures, creating is the act that marks the absolute line between God and everything else: “I am the LORD, who made all things… who alone stretched out the heavens” (Isaiah 44:24). The one through whom all things were made cannot himself be among the things that were made; he stands on the Creator’s side of that line. And the prepositions do not mark a difference of rank: Paul elsewhere piles all of them onto God without distinction — “from him and through him and to him are all things” (Romans 11:36). The Son is given a creation role that Jewish monotheism reserved for God alone.

Not a single sentence Corroborating

The verse reads less like a doctrine Paul invented than like a confession he quotes — compact, balanced, assumed as common ground. The same devotion surfaces in independent streams: the Philippians 2 hymn — widely regarded as pre-Pauline tradition — applies to Jesus the fiercely monotheistic oath of Isaiah 45:23, “to me every knee shall bow.” The Aramaic-speaking churches, older than any Greek theology, were already crying Marana tha, “Our Lord, come!”, a prayer addressed to Jesus (1 Corinthians 16:22). Baptism ran in the threefold name of Father, Son, and Spirit (Matthew 28:19). Paul is not the inventor of this devotion; he is our earliest written window onto a worship that was already there.

“Two powers” material Suggestive · debated

The influential thesis of Daniel Boyarin and others, that something like “binitarian” ideas circulated within ancient Judaism before being branded heresy, is itself debated among specialists and should be offered as a suggestive line of evidence, not a proof.

Honest limit: it must be kept sharply distinct from a stronger and quite different claim — associated with Margaret Barker and Bernhard Lang — that ancient Israel actually worshipped two gods. That thesis Bauckham judges the evidence does not support: the surviving literature of Second Temple Judaism is, as far as it reaches, uniformly monotheistic. The most the “two powers” material can carry is the modest point that Jewish monotheism already had room for God’s own Word, Wisdom, and Glory — never that there were two gods for the first Christians to choose between.

Objection — McGrath: “one Lord” is alongside the creed, not inside it Hardest

The leading scholarly critic here is James F. McGrath, who argues directly against Bauckham and Wright that 1 Corinthians 8:6 does not “split” the Shema at all. On McGrath’s reading, Paul confesses the “one God” of the Shema — the Father — and then adds “one Lord, Jesus Christ” alongside that confession, not inside it. On this reading, Jesus is the Messiah, the agent through whom God acts, exalted to a unique status — but a distinct figure subordinate to the one God, not a person of that one God. Second Temple Judaism was full of exalted intermediary figures who do God’s work, even bear God’s name, without being God. Reading Nicaea into Paul, on this view, is anachronism.

Reply

This is the objection at its best, and a fair-minded Christian should grant the facts it rests on, though not the inference drawn from them. The trouble is the word Kyrios: in the Shema Paul is unmistakably echoing, Kyrios is the Septuagint’s rendering of the divine name itself. If “one Lord” means a being who is not the one God — a separate, exalted creature — then Paul has confessed, in an anti-idolatry argument, one God plus one additional divine-but-not-God Lord through whom all things were made. That is not the strict monotheism the objection wants to protect; it is a second power. And if the reply is that an agent could bear God’s name without becoming a second god, grant it: God’s name and functions could be entrusted to a supreme agent, and the worship itself still could not be. The reading that actually preserves monotheism is the trinitarian one: the one divine identity, confessed under both of the creed’s titles, shared by Father and Son. The grammatical distinction in the verse — all things from the Father, all things through the Son — is genuinely there and must be reckoned with, not waved away.

Objection — the exalted agent

Some Second Temple texts do exalt intermediary figures to dizzying heights — personified Wisdom, the great angels, exalted patriarchs like Enoch — who do God’s work, even bear God’s name, without being God. Slotting the risen Jesus into that role of supreme agent is striking, but it is not the Nicene Trinity.

Reply

Two things distinguish Jesus from every intermediary figure in the literature. The first is the boundary of worship. As Larry Hurtado has documented in exhaustive detail, the angels of Second Temple Judaism consistently refuse worship and redirect it to God — the angel in Revelation cries “Worship God!” (Revelation 19:10). Yet the earliest Christians prayed to Jesus, were baptized in his name, sang to him, and called on him in worship — a devotional pattern Judaism reserved for God alone. The point needs care: a gesture of homage was not itself the line (Israelites could bow before a king as well as before the LORD, 1 Chronicles 29:20); what Jewish monotheism guarded jealously for God was the substance of worship — sacrifice, invocation, prayer. The second is that no intermediary figure we know of was ever placed inside the Shema. Wisdom is praised; Enoch is exalted; but no Jewish writer we know of took the creed of divine uniqueness and wrote a second figure into it (Philo’s “second god,” the Logos, is the nearest case, and even it is never placed inside the confession). Paul did.

The room: Richard Bauckham and N. T. Wright (Paul reworks the Shema), supported by Larry Hurtado and, more cautiously, James D. G. Dunn; James F. McGrath and others argue that “one Lord” is added to, not folded into, the creed, and a wider literature debates whether “splitting,” “supplementing,” or “brokering” best describes what Paul is doing. The Jewish reading of the Shema is a serious and ancient one, held in good faith.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each objection is stated as you will actually meet it, then answered — with the hardest one named above and answered at length in the deep dive.

The one that matters most: James McGrath’s — that Paul sets “one Lord” alongside the creed, not inside it. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"Echad just means one — composite unity is apologetic spin" Common

Strongest form

Echad is the ordinary numeral. One-flesh examples do not prove a compound God — that is reading Trinity into a number.

Reply

Agreed — and we do not run that argument. Echad is the ordinary numeral, the plurality in 'one flesh' belongs to the noun, and the echad-versus-yachid contrast cannot carry the weight some place on it. What survives is only the negative: the creed is aimed at rival gods, not at a person-count, so it does not forbid internal complexity. The Shema leaves the door ajar; 1 Corinthians 8:6 is what walks through it, and that is where the whole weight of this argument sits.

"One God AND one Lord — Paul distinguishes Jesus FROM God" Common

Strongest form

Paul confesses the one God of the Shema, the Father, and sets ‘one Lord’ alongside that confession, not inside it: Jesus is the exalted Messiah, God’s supreme agent bearing God’s name, as Second Temple Judaism exalted Wisdom, great angels and Enoch without making them God (James McGrath).

Reply

Then which 'Lord'? In the Shema Paul quotes, kyrios IS the divine name — the LXX rendering of YHWH. On that reading Paul confesses one God plus one additional non-God creator-Lord — inside an anti-idolatry argument. That is not unitarianism; it is a second god. The coherent reading: one divine identity, two persons sharing the creed's two titles. And if an agent could bear God’s name, grant it: the name could be entrusted to a supreme agent, but the worship could not.

"Through whom makes Jesus a mere instrument" Trickier

Strongest form

Prepositions matter: all things FROM the Father, only THROUGH Jesus — agency language fitting a created intermediary like personified Wisdom.

Reply

Creating is the act that separates YHWH from everything else — Isaiah 44:24: 'I am the LORD... who ALONE stretched out the heavens.' Romans 11:36 uses from-through-to of God without distinction, and Hebrews 1 and John 1 put the Son on the Creator side of the line precisely via 'through.' The one through whom are ALL things cannot be among the things.

"Standard Jewish agency — exalted figures bore the Name too" Trickier

Strongest form

Second-Temple texts exalt Enoch and angelic Name-bearers. Paul slotting Jesus into agency is Jewish and non-trinitarian.

Reply

The agency literature is real — and its boundary is what Jesus crosses: those figures refuse worship (Revelation's angel: 'worship God!'), receive no cultic devotion, and are never put inside the Shema. Homage alone was not the line (Israel bowed to God and king, 1 Chr 29:20); what Judaism guarded for God alone was the substance of worship: sacrifice, invocation, prayer. Prayers to Jesus, baptism in his name, YHWH-texts transferred to him — Hurtado documents a worship-pattern Judaism reserved for God alone.

"One verse from one creative theologian" Hard

Strongest form

Even granting your reading, you have one man's formula — a thin foundation for the Trinity.

Reply

1 Cor 8:6 reads as a formula Paul cites, not coins — compact, rhythmic, assumed as common ground. And it does not stand alone: the Philippians hymn, widely regarded as pre-Pauline, gives Jesus the Isaiah 45 kyrios-oath; Aramaic churches cried marana tha to Jesus before Greek theology existed; baptism ran in the threefold name. Paul is our earliest window onto the devotion, not its inventor.

"The creed’s custodians — Israel — say this breaks it" Hard

Strongest form

Two millennia of Jewish scholarship reads the Shema as excluding all this. The custodians outrank Paul.

Reply

Paul WAS a custodian — 'as to the law, a Pharisee' — as were the “great many of the priests” (Acts 6:7) and the believing Pharisees (Acts 15:5) in the earliest church; this is an intra-Jewish reading. The exclusion hardened in later polemic — understandable history, not neutral arbitration. And the older strata complicate the confidence: Word, Wisdom and Spirit acting as God, the two-powers debates the rabbis fought over Daniel 7’s “one like a son of man,” who comes with the clouds — a suggestive, contested line of evidence (Boyarin), not a proof. The argument asks the creed's question with the creed's own materials. Grammar alone was never going to decide it; the case rests on what the one God has since disclosed in Christ.

The honest state of the debate

The lexical leg is the one to give away, and honesty requires saying so plainly: echad is the ordinary Hebrew numeral, the echad-versus-yachid contrast cannot bear the doctrinal weight some place on it, and the popular “compound unity” argument is a discredited overreach the case does not use. That reading is influential, arguably dominant among New Testament scholars, but genuinely contested, not unanimous.

A fairness most defenders skip: The strongest scholarly critic is James F. McGrath, who argues directly against Bauckham and Wright that “one Lord” is set alongside the Shema, not folded into it; a wider literature debates whether “splitting,” “supplementing,” or “brokering” best describes what Paul does, and the Bauckham–Wright reading — supported by Hurtado and, more cautiously, by James D. G. Dunn — is not the settled view. Two further honesties are owed. The traditional Jewish reading of the Shema is a serious and ancient interpretation, held in good faith by people who love this text; the Christian claim is not that Jews have failed at arithmetic but an intra-biblical claim they are entitled to find unpersuasive. And Daniel Boyarin’s thesis that “binitarian” ideas once circulated within Judaism is suggestive, not decisive — and must be kept sharply distinct from the stronger Barker–Lang claim that ancient Israel actually worshipped two gods, which Bauckham judges the evidence does not support, the surviving literature of Second Temple Judaism being uniformly monotheistic.

The bounded verdict: The honest answer to the question is no: the Shema does not rule out the Trinity, and the objection built on it does not hold. But be exact about what the argument does. It has not shown that the Trinity is taught in Deuteronomy 6:4; it has not shown that echad “means” a compound unity; and it has not refuted the traditional Jewish reading by force of grammar alone. What it establishes is that the Shema confesses one God and does not, by itself, foreclose the question of that God’s inner life — and that the earliest Christians, Jewish monotheists led here by a Pharisee writing against idolatry, did not abandon the Shema when they confessed Jesus as Lord; they recited it, with Jesus inside it: monotheism kept, not monotheism lost. This argument does not prove the Trinity; it clears the charge against it. The positive case rests on revelation and on the historical case for who Jesus was — and this is what we hold to be true: the one God exists eternally as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, three coequal, coeternal persons, one God.

    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    That is a fair and exact reading of the verse, and the argument does not pretend otherwise: 1 Corinthians 8:6 is not offered as a proof-text for the whole Trinity. What it shows is the hard part — that the earliest Christians placed Jesus inside the Shema, within the unique identity of the one God, rather than beside it as a second deity. That this argument does not, by itself, prove the triune God is not a dodge; it is the honest scope. It clears the charge that confessing Jesus as Lord breaks the creed; it does not, on its own, establish the full doctrine. And the verse does not stand alone. The same confession of divine oneness is already shared out across Father, Son, and Spirit in independent streams older than any Greek theology: the threefold name into which the church baptized (Matthew 28:19), and the liturgical cadence of “one body… one Spirit… one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all” (Ephesians 4:4–6). So the Spirit is not an afterthought bolted on at Nicaea; the raw material the creeds later organized is already in the earliest practice. This argument does not prove the Trinity; the positive case rests on revelation and on the historical case for who Jesus was. But this is what we hold to be true, and the one verse is one true strand of it: the one God exists eternally as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — three coequal, coeternal persons, one God.
