The Kalam Cosmological Argument

The deep dive tells you what the argument is. This track makes you able to use it — reconstruct it from memory, answer its hardest objections, and hold the line in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

The Kalam cosmological argument runs on two short premises: whatever begins to exist has a cause, and the universe began to exist — so the universe has a cause. Notice the careful wording. The claim is not that "everything has a cause" (which would invite the question "then what caused God?"), but only that things which come into being require something to bring them about. The first premise rests on one of the most secure intuitions we have — out of nothing, nothing comes — and its deepest warrant is that metaphysical intuition, not the inductive track record alone.

The second premise — that the universe had a beginning — draws on both philosophy and science. Philosophers argue that an actually infinite past leads to absurdities, since you could never "finish" counting through an endless series of prior moments to arrive at today. These arguments are serious reasons, though contested, not knock-down demonstrations. Scientifically, the standard Big Bang model and the 2003 Borde–Guth–Vilenkin theorem strongly corroborate, without proving, a finite cosmic past. A cause of all space, time and matter would itself have to be spaceless, timeless (no part of the time that began with the universe), immensely powerful, and — as the best explanation of why a timeless cause produces a temporal effect — plausibly a personal agent.

Honestly stated: the argument concludes to a transcendent cause, not yet to the full God of Christianity. Kalam is one strand in a cumulative case, alongside fine-tuning, moral value, contingency, and ultimately the historical evidence for the resurrection. "The universe just exists, inexplicably" is not the only rational option — and, we judge, not the most reasonable one: taken together, the premises and the inference to the best explanation favor a personal Creator.

Before you start · key terms

Eight words this argument leans on

Learn these first — the objections below turn on them, and most weak versions of the Kalam come from blurring one of them.

“Begins to exist”The premise is restricted to things that come into being — never “everything has a cause.” The precision is built into the argument, not bolted on after the fact.
Ex nihilo nihil fit“Out of nothing, nothing comes” — being does not arise from non-being. The deepest warrant for Premise 1.
Actual infiniteAn infinite collection existing all at once in reality — as opposed to a potential infinite that only ever grows toward it.
Successive additionBuilding a collection one member after another. However long you go on, you can always add one more — you never complete it.
Material vs. efficient causeWhat a thing is made out of (material) versus what brings it about (efficient). Premise 1 asks only for the second.
BGV theoremBorde–Guth–Vilenkin, 2003: any universe that has on average been expanding has a past boundary — it cannot be extended infinitely into the past as a classical spacetime.
Agent causationAn agent bringing about a new state of affairs without any prior determining change — the signature of a person, not a mechanism.
Inference to the best explanationReasoning to the most reasonable account of the evidence — strong where it holds, but not a deductive proof.
The argument, formally

Three lines. The whole weight rests on the two premises.

P1

Whatever begins to exist has a cause of its existence.

P2

The universe began to exist.

∴ C

Therefore, the universe has a cause of its existence.

The logic is valid — if both premises are true, the conclusion follows. So objections come in two families: attacks on P1 or P2, and attacks on the further step from the conclusion to what it is taken to show. Knowing which premise an objection targets is half the battle.

From a cause to a Creator

Don’t read the conclusion — reason to it

Working purely from the premises, before importing any religious commitment: what must a cause of all space, time, and matter be like? Reason each line out, then reveal.

It caused all space — so it cannot itself be…?
spacelessThe cause brought all of physical reality into being — all space, all time, all matter and energy. Therefore the cause cannot itself be spatial, temporal, or material — timeless in at least this sense, that it is no part of the time that began with the universe.
It caused all time — so it is…?
timeless*…timeless in at least this sense, that it is no part of the time that began with the universe. (Whether God’s own life has any succession to it is a further question, on which orthodox Christians differ and which this argument does not settle.)
It caused all matter and energy — so it is…?
immaterial…it must be spaceless, timeless*, and immaterial, transcending the very framework it produced.
If it had begun, Premise 1 would demand its own cause — so it is…?
uncaused, beginninglessIt must be uncaused — for if it began to exist it would, by Premise 1, require a cause of its own, and we have simply moved the question back a step. The cause of the universe is itself beginningless.
It brought the entire cosmos into being — so it is…?
enormously powerfulIt must be unimaginably powerful, having brought the entire cosmos into existence.
Best explanation of a temporal effect from a timeless cause (agent causation)?
plausibly personalIf the cause were a changeless set of sufficient conditions — like water freezing the instant the temperature drops below zero — then wherever the cause existed, the effect would exist too, and a timeless cause would yield an equally timeless, beginningless universe. But the universe is not beginningless. An agent — who can refrain as well as act — is the most natural fit for a cause that is changeless without the universe and yet brings about an effect that begins. (Morriston doubts a timeless agent can coherently “timelessly will” a first moment.) So it adds weight to the case for a personal cause rather than settling it — inference to the best explanation, not proof. (Those who hold that God’s own life has succession will frame the step differently; the argument does not decide that.)

* Timeless here means only that the cause is no part of the time that began with the universe. Whether God’s own life has any succession to it is a further question on which orthodox Christians differ, and which this argument does not settle.

Honestly stated: this reaches a spaceless, timeless, immaterial, enormously powerful, and plausibly personal cause — a long way toward theism, but not yet the triune God of the Nicene Creed, nor even a morally good or worship-worthy being. That is not a flaw; it is honesty about scope. Kalam is one strand in a cumulative case, alongside fine-tuning, moral value, contingency, and ultimately the historical evidence for the resurrection.

Defending Premise 1

Whatever begins to exist has a cause

Precision check: not “everything has a cause” (which invites “then what caused the cause?”) — only things that begin to exist. The restriction is principled, built into the argument from its origins.

The metaphysical intuition The core warrant

Being does not arise from non-being — ex nihilo nihil fit. If things could pop into existence uncaused out of nothing, it becomes inexplicable why we do not see bicycles, symphonies, and herds of zebra flickering into being in midair. That reality is orderly rather than a lottery of spontaneous somethings is itself evidence that coming-to-be requires a cause.

The inductive track record Confirmatory · limited

Premise 1 is confirmed throughout ordinary experience — though that track record is entirely of causes acting on pre-existing material inside the universe, so extending it to the universe’s own beginning is a step a critic can resist. The deeper warrant is the intuition above, not the induction alone.

Even David Hume — usually cited against the principle — affirmed it: in a 1754 letter he denied ever asserting “so absurd a Proposition” as that anything might arise without a cause. What he disputed was only that we know it by intuition or demonstration — which is exactly where Morriston presses.

Objection — “Quantum particles come from nothing”

In the quantum vacuum, virtual particles appear with no deterministic cause — so Premise 1 is empirically false.

Reply

The quantum vacuum is emphatically not “nothing.” It is a real sea of fluctuating energy governed by the laws of quantum field theory; a particle arising from it is no more something-from-nothing than a wave from the ocean. The objection equivocates: the physicist’s “nothing” is empty space with its fields; Premise 1’s “nothing” is non-being — no space, no fields, no laws. Two further distinctions: an indeterministic event is not thereby uncaused (a nucleus decays randomly in time but, as the physicist Peter Bussey notes, “because certain nuclear forces are acting”); and “creation out of nothing” denies a material cause, not an efficient one — Premise 1 asks only for the latter.

Objection — Morriston: our causal sense comes only from inside time Hardest

Our confidence that things have causes is built from events within time and space. The absolute beginning of all space and time is utterly unlike that — so why assume the principle reaches that far? (Morriston also doubts a timeless agent can coherently “timelessly will” a first moment.)

Reply

The causal principle is grounded not merely in inductive habit but in a metaphysical insight about being and non-being that holds whether the “beginning” is in time or of time — a case the defender must make, not assume: nothing has no properties and no powers, so it can produce nothing. On timeless willing: an agent can bring about a new state without any prior determining change, since what is willed, changelessly, is a world with a first moment. This is the objection worth taking most seriously.

Objection — Oppy: the first state could be an exception

The principle may hold everywhere except at the very first state — singled out by its position, as the one state with nothing before it — so the naturalist may take the initial state as a brute fact needing no cause.

Reply

Being first is not a power or feature that could make uncaused origination possible; it is only the absence of anything prior — and the absence of anything is exactly the condition under which the principle says nothing comes (an answer going back to Jonathan Edwards, which the logician A. N. Prior, no apologist, judged “entirely cogent”). Oppy has replied that this still allows a first state of only contingent things; whether that holds at the absolute beginning is the same question Morriston presses, and it remains open.

The room: Craig defends it; Quentin Smith denied it of the universe in a 1993 exchange (“we came from nothing, by nothing, and for nothing”), later arguing from quantum cosmology instead; Morriston and Oppy press it hardest — both fair-minded critics, not cranks.
Defending Premise 2

The universe began to exist

The controversial premise, and the battleground. Claim: the past is finite — a first moment, not an endless ocean of prior events. Defended along two independent lines, so even if one failed entirely, the other would remain.

Line 1 — philosophical

A · An actual infinite can’t be instantiated Strong · contested

Hilbert’s Hotel: a “full” hotel of infinitely many rooms still takes new guests; empty the odd-numbered rooms and infinitely many leave, yet exactly as many remain. Signs, the argument runs, that an actually infinite number of real things cannot be instantiated in the concrete world — so the series of past events cannot be actually infinite.

Honest narrowing: Cantorian set theory shows the actual infinite is perfectly coherent mathematically — Hilbert’s Hotel is counterintuitive, not contradictory. So the claim must be narrowed to: an actual infinite cannot be concretely instantiated in the physical world. Whether that holds is the disputed hinge.

B · You can’t form an infinite by successive addition Strong · contested

The past is built up one event at a time; however long you add, you never complete an infinite — reaching today by traversing infinitely many prior moments would be completing the uncompletable. Since we have arrived at today, the series behind us is finite.

Honest limit: this assumes time really passes (a dynamic view). On the rival “block” view, nothing is ever added and the argument loses its grip — Craig grants it presupposes a dynamical view of time. One reason Premise 2 does not rest on it alone.

Objection — Morriston: “counting down from infinity” begs the question

A beginningless past has no first moment to set out from, so the image smuggles in a starting point; the defender owes a “symmetry breaker” to count a beginningless past impossible while allowing an endless future (Malpass & Morriston, 2020).

Reply

The worry was never about a starting point: it is that an actual infinite cannot be completed by successive addition, one moment at a time, whether or not the series had a first member — and a beginningless past is precisely such an uncompletable series offered to us as already complete.

C · The Grim Reaper paradox Strong · contested

Infinitely many “reapers,” each set to act at a fixed moment packed ever closer to noon: you cannot survive past noon, yet no reaper can be the one who strikes (there is always an earlier one). The scenario is flatly contradictory — so the actually infinite army it requires cannot exist. Pruss and Koons, from a 1964 Benardete paradox, spread the same structure across an infinite past.

Line 2 — scientific (corroboration, stated honestly)

D · Big Bang cosmology Corroboration

The standard model traces the expansion of the universe back toward a finite past boundary some 13.8 billion years ago.

E · The Borde–Guth–Vilenkin theorem (2003) Corroboration

Any universe that has, on average, been expanding cannot be extended infinitely into the past — its history has a past boundary — with no assumptions about energy content, so it reaches even most multiverse and eternal-inflation models.

Stated honestly: it is a result about classical spacetime; it does not by itself say what lies at the boundary. Vilenkin says the universe “probably” began and that “a deep mystery remains”; his own co-author Alan Guth is not persuaded it began (he is sympathetic to past-eternal models like Carroll–Chen). Powerful corroboration of a beginning of the universe as we know it — not an airtight proof.

F · Thermodynamics Suggestive

If the universe were infinitely old it would presumably have reached “heat death” equilibrium an infinite time ago; that it has not suggests a finite wind-up. Genuinely disputed — Carroll notes that in an expanding universe the maximum possible entropy rises, so equilibrium need never be reached — so best treated as suggestive; but the low-entropy past we actually observe still sits awkwardly with a literally infinite history.

The room: Vilenkin (“probably began”), Guth (unpersuaded), Carroll (rising entropy ceiling), Mithani & Vilenkin (no viable eternal model). The science converges on a beginning; it does not close the case — which is why the philosophical line matters independently.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

This is what separates someone who knows the Kalam from someone who can defend it. Each gets its best form first, then the reply, sorted by difficulty. Two of the sharpest — Morriston on the causal principle and Oppy on the first state — are answered inside Defending Premise 1 above, where they bite.

The one that matters most: Morriston’s — our causal intuition is built from events inside time and space. It is answered under Defending Premise 1 above; the deep dive presses it further — take it with you.

Common
"Then what caused God?" Common

Strongest form

If everything needs a cause, then God needs one too. Stopping at God is an arbitrary exception — special pleading.

Reply

P1 doesn't say everything needs a cause. It says everything that begins to exist does. God, by definition, never began — so He's not an exception to the rule, He simply falls outside its scope. It isn't arbitrary; the premise was principled from the start. The question is rather like asking what the bachelor's wife is doing tonight: it presupposes what the case has already ruled out.

Trickier
"Mathematicians use infinities fine" Trickier

Strongest form

Cantor showed actual infinite sets are perfectly coherent. So why deny an actual infinite of past events?

Reply

There's a difference between the infinite as a concept in mathematics (consistent within its axioms) and an infinite collection instantiated in the physical world. Granted: Cantorian set theory shows the actual infinite to be perfectly coherent mathematically — Hilbert's Hotel is counterintuitive, not contradictory — so the defender's claim must be narrowed to that second thing: an actual infinite cannot be concretely instantiated in the physical world. Whether that metaphysical claim holds is precisely the disputed hinge, so the arguments are offered not as knock-down demonstrations but as serious reasons to doubt that the past stretches back forever.

Hard
"Maybe the universe has no beginning" Hard

Strongest form

Some cosmological models — cyclic universes, quantum-gravity scenarios — avoid an absolute beginning. So P2 isn't established.

Reply

The Borde–Guth–Vilenkin theorem shows that any universe which has, on average, been expanding has a past boundary — its history can't be extended infinitely into the past as a classical spacetime. It's a powerful, general result, but worth being honest about its limits: it concerns classical geometry, and what lies at that boundary — a true beginning, a quantum-gravity regime, or a bounce — is left open. Vilenkin himself concludes the universe "probably" began; his co-author Alan Guth isn't persuaded it began at all. So we lean on it as strong corroboration, not a knock-down proof — which is exactly why the philosophical arguments against an infinite past matter independently.

"Why does the cause have to be personal?" Hard

Strongest form

Even granting a first cause, you've got a deist abstraction, not God. Why think it's a mind that chooses, rather than some impersonal force?

Reply

Two considerations point to a mind. First, a cause of all space, time, and matter seems to fall into one of only two categories we can conceive: an abstract object (a number, say) or an unembodied mind — and abstract objects don't stand in causal relations. Second, if a changeless cause were mechanically sufficient, its effect should be as eternal as the cause; the best explanation for why a temporal universe springs from a timeless cause is a free act of will — agent causation. Neither is a deductive proof, but together they make a personal cause the most reasonable inference — a mind, not a mechanism. Even so, that is not yet the triune God of the Nicene Creed, nor even a morally good or worship-worthy being: Kalam is one strand in a cumulative case, alongside arguments from fine-tuning, moral value, contingency, and ultimately the historical evidence for the resurrection.

"Then isn't God infinitely old?" Hard

Strongest form

Turn the argument's own philosophy back on it: if an actual infinite can't be concretely instantiated, and no infinite series can be completed by successive addition, what about God? Some theists hold God is everlasting in time — so His own past would be an unbounded series of moments, now complete: the very thing the two arguments were built to tell against.

Reply

Both arguments are pressed against a series of events in the physical world; the cause this argument reaches is spaceless, timeless, and immaterial. On the classical account of divine eternity, a timeless cause is not a series of moments at all, so there is nothing to "complete." Don't answer by calling God "infinitely old" — that concedes the very picture being denied, treating His eternity as a vast stretch of elapsed time. How God stands to time is genuinely disputed among orthodox Christians, and the Kalam settles none of it. On the classical account the objection dissolves; on the other account the defender owes a further answer about God's own past — but the conclusion does not fall with it either way, since neither account makes God a member of the physical past these arguments are about.

"Quantum cosmology dissolves the beginning" Hard

Strongest form

The Hartle–Hawking "no-boundary proposal" replaces ordinary time with a fourth dimension of space near the earliest era, so the universe is finite in the past but has no sharp first moment — "like the surface of the earth" with no edge. Hawking drew the moral himself: "What place, then, for a creator?"

Reply

The model is speculative and leans on the contested device of "imaginary time," which Vilenkin describes as introduced "only for computational convenience" and which Hawking himself read positivistically — as just a mathematical model — on which reading it cannot carry the theological moral. More importantly, even on the no-boundary model the universe still has a finite past; it still begins to exist in the relevant sense, even without a first instant as we ordinarily picture one. And smoothing the earliest geometry does not, by itself, supply a cause for why there is a finite cosmos at all rather than nothing.

"The cause needn't be the God of theism" Hard

Strongest form

Even granting everything, the argument delivers a spaceless, timeless, immaterial, enormously powerful, and plausibly personal cause — not yet the triune God of the Nicene Creed, nor even a morally good or worship-worthy being.

Reply

Simply true, and a careful defender should say so plainly. This is not a flaw; it is honesty about scope. No single argument of natural theology was ever meant to carry the whole weight. Kalam's job is more modest and more foundational: to show that the universe is not a self-explaining brute fact, and that the door to a transcendent cause stands open. It is one strand in a cumulative case — alongside fine-tuning, moral value, contingency, and ultimately the historical evidence for the resurrection.

Touch the originals

Where this comes from — primary sources, not our summary

Al-Ghazalic. 1095 · Incoherence of the Philosophers; Kitab al-Iqtisad
The Persian theologian who gave the argument its classic shape, attacking the philosophers' eternal world in the Incoherence and formulating the syllogism itself in his Kitab al-Iqtisad: "Every being which begins has a cause for its beginning."
John Philoponus6th c. · Against Aristotle, on the Eternity of the World
The Alexandrian Christian philosopher who turned Aristotle's own ban on a completed actual infinite against the eternal universe — arguing the past must be finite. This is the genuine seed of the Kalam's finite-past reasoning, later developed in Islamic kalam.
Borde, Guth & Vilenkin2003 · Phys. Rev. Lett. 90, 151301
"Inflationary Spacetimes Are Incomplete in Past Directions" (arXiv:gr-qc/0110012) — proved on-average-expanding spacetimes are past-incomplete. Read it with Mithani & Vilenkin, "Did the Universe Have a Beginning?" (arXiv:1204.4658) for Vilenkin's own careful framing of how far the result reaches.
William Lane Craigcontemporary
Revived and systematized the Kalam in analytic philosophy, including the conceptual analysis of what the cause must be. (Read his work directly — we paraphrase, but the originals reward the time.)
The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

Among philosophers it remains live and genuinely contested. Premise 1 is widely regarded as plausible, though critics like Morriston raise real questions about its scope. Premise 2 is the battleground: the philosophical arguments have able defenders and able critics, and the scientific evidence is corroborative rather than conclusive. The step from cause to personal Creator is the most contested of all, best held as a reasoned inference, not a proof. The Kalam has not ended the conversation; it has sharpened it, and forced naturalists to say exactly where they get off the train.

A fairness most defenders skip: the whole theistic tradition does not reason as the Kalam does. Thomas Aquinas held that the world's beginning in time cannot be proved by reason at all — "by faith alone do we hold… that the world did not always exist" — while still judging God's existence demonstrable by arguments that don't turn on a temporal beginning. Maimonides reasoned much the same way. None of this refutes the Kalam, but a case is not strengthened by conscripting authorities who expressly declined to make it.

The bounded verdict: stated without exaggeration, the Kalam makes a serious, defensible case that the universe began and therefore has a transcendent cause — spaceless, timeless, immaterial, beginningless, enormously powerful, and most plausibly a personal mind. It does not by itself prove the God of any particular religion, and the best critics remain in the field. What it accomplishes is to show that "the universe just exists, inexplicably" is not the only rational option — and, we judge, not the most reasonable one.

    PH
    Philosophy student
    "Fine — but why think the first cause is a mind at all? Why not an impersonal state that just happened to have some chance of producing a universe? You don't need a will for that — physics with a dice-roll would do."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    An impersonal first state that produced the universe by chance meets a dilemma. A cause that necessitated its effect would have an effect as old as itself — but the universe is not beginningless. A mindless state with merely some chance of producing a universe would be poised to change; and it is hard to see how a mindless state poised to change could remain changeless without beginning, rather than changing after some finite stretch. An agent — who can refrain as well as act — is the most natural fit for a cause that is changeless without the universe and yet brings about an effect that begins. Critics reply that without time “some finite stretch” has no clear meaning; so it adds weight to the case for a personal cause rather than settling it — inference to the best explanation, not proof. (Those who hold that God's own life has succession will frame the step differently; the argument does not decide that.)
