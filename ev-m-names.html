The Argument from Palestinian Names

The deep dive tells you what the argument is. This track makes you able to use it — reconstruct it from memory, answer its hardest objections, and hold the line in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

The argument from Palestinian names draws on onomastics — the study of name frequencies. How common a given name is varies sharply by region and era, and that distribution is extremely hard for an outsider or a much later writer to reproduce by guesswork. Richard Bauckham, building on a database compiled by Tal Ilan, showed that the personal names in the Gospels and Acts closely match the statistical profile of Jewish Palestine in the early first century: the same most-common names (Simon and Joseph for men, Mary and Salome for women), roughly the same proportions, and the same habit of adding nicknames or qualifiers to distinguish people who shared a common name.

The inference is that this incidental fingerprint is best explained by traditions rooted in early Palestine, among people who actually lived in that world, rather than by authors writing much later or far away — who would more likely have reflected the different name profiles of the diaspora.

The honest scope: the underlying premise that name frequencies are local and time-bound is uncontroversial, and the data match is genuinely striking. The live questions are how precise the match is and how far it licenses the conclusion. The argument supports an early Palestinian provenance; the maximal eyewitness claim built on it is contested. It addresses provenance, not the resurrection — the inference this Library defends elsewhere, that Jesus rose bodily from the dead — and it does not by itself establish that every reported event is true.

Before you start · key terms

Eight words this argument leans on

Learn these first — the objections below turn on them, and most weak versions of the argument come from blurring one of them.

OnomasticsThe study of personal names and their distribution. Its founding observation is simple and uncontroversial: the popularity of a given name is sharply local and time-bound — which names are common, and in what proportions, varies by region, by religious community, and by generation.
Ilan’s Lexicon — the baselineTal Ilan’s Lexicon of Jewish Names in Late Antiquity, Part I: Palestine 330 BCE–200 CE (2002) catalogues the attested Jewish persons of that region and era — roughly three thousand individuals, drawn from literary texts, Josephus, ossuary inscriptions, and documentary papyri — with the frequencies of their names. Ilan was not doing apologetics; she was building a reference work, an independent baseline against which any text claiming to depict that world can be tested.
Relative frequenciesIt is one thing to include a few common names and quite another to reproduce their relative frequencies. What no ancient author had the tools to do was ensure that the second-commonest name appeared about as often as it actually did, and that the rare names stayed rare in roughly the right proportion. There were no frequency tables in antiquity.
DisambiguationBecause a handful of names were so over-common, first-century Palestinian Jews tagged people — by a patronymic (Simon bar-Jonah), a hometown (Joseph of Arimathea, Mary Magdalene), a nickname (Simon the Zealot, Simon the Leper), or a trade. The Gospels apply this disambiguation selectively, to exactly the over-common names, while rare names are usually left bare.
The diaspora profileAmong Egyptian Jews the most common names included forms like Eleazar, Sabbataeus, Joseph, Dositheus, and Pappus — a noticeably different mix, with far more Greek names, than that of Palestine. The Gospels match Palestine specifically; they do not match the diaspora profile.
Goodness-of-fit testThe chi-squared test Luuk Van de Weghe and the statistician Jason Wilson (2024) used to ask whether the Gospel names behave like a sample drawn from the population or from an author’s imagination. They found the fit at least as good as that of the names in Josephus, and markedly better than names drawn from ancient fiction or modern historical novels. Gregor and Blais have in turn replied, and the exchange is ongoing.
Anonymous community transmissionThe rival model Kamil Gregor and Brian Blais (2023) propose: the names accrued through a Palestinian Christian community over time, with no need for named eyewitnesses behind them. Even granted in full, it grants that the names entered the tradition in Palestine — the core of the provenance claim.
Provenance, not truthThe argument addresses provenance and dating — when and where these traditions were really formed — not inerrancy or the resurrection. It does not, by itself, establish that any reported event happened; accurate names alone cannot rule out a story that later grew, which is why the other lines of evidence matter.
The argument, formally

Three lines. The whole weight rests on the two premises.

P1

The frequency of personal names in a population is local and time-bound, and is very hard for an outsider or a much later writer to reproduce by guesswork.

P2

The names in the Gospels and Acts match the statistical profile of Jewish Palestine in roughly 330 BCE–200 CE — in their specific names, their relative frequencies, and their disambiguation habits, attested in independent sources.

∴ C

Therefore, the Gospel traditions are best explained as rooted in early Palestinian sources close to the events, not composed by distant, later authors.

It is an inference to the best explanation resting on a statistical fact. P1's first half is uncontroversial — onomastics genuinely varies by region and era; its second half is where Tuckett presses, since Palestinian naming knowledge travelled with people like Josephus. The action is on P2 (does the data really match?) and on how far the match licenses the conclusion.

Then the conceptual step — why is name-frequency so hard to fake?

You can copy a famous name, but you would be very unlikely to intuit that "Simon" and "Joseph" were the two commonest male names, or that bearers of common names get nicknamed to tell them apart. That texture is an unconscious statistical fingerprint left by people who actually lived in that world.

Defending Premise 1

The frequency of personal names in a population is local and time-bound, and is very hard for an outsider or a much later writer to reproduce by guesswork

Precision check: the founding observation — that the popularity of a given name is sharply local and time-bound — is not a contested theological premise; it is an everyday fact any historian of antiquity will grant. Which names are common, and in what proportions, varies by region, by religious community, and by generation. The first half of Premise 1 is essentially conceded by everyone; its second half is where Tuckett’s objection presses. The argument as a whole is an inference to the best explanation built on a statistical fact, not a deductive proof.

Names cluster by community and era The core warrant

The fashion that makes “Simon” ubiquitous in one generation and rare in another is an emergent social fact, not a rule anyone consciously follows. You cannot deduce it; you can only absorb it by living inside the population. Tal Ilan’s Lexicon of Jewish Names in Late Antiquity catalogues the attested Jewish persons of Palestine, 330 BCE–200 CE—roughly three thousand individuals—with the frequencies of their names, giving a hard baseline to test against.

The diaspora pattern differed sharply Corroboration

Among Egyptian Jews, the most common names included forms like Eleazar, Sabbataeus, Joseph, Dositheus, and Pappus—a noticeably different mix, with far more Greek names, than that of Palestine. So a writer working in the diaspora would not merely fail to copy the Palestinian list; he would tend to default to the names of his own world. The watermark is not just hard to forge—the forger’s own background actively pulls him away from it.

Including a few common names is easy; reproducing their relative frequencies is not Corroboration

An author could easily salt a story with “Simon” and “Mary.” What no ancient author had the tools to do was ensure that the second-commonest name appeared about as often as the second-commonest name actually did, and that the rare names stayed rare in roughly the right proportion. There were no frequency tables in antiquity. Reconstructing them took Ilan a modern scholar’s lifetime of cataloguing inscriptions. The pattern can leak out of genuine memory; it is very hard to engineer from the outside.

Honest limit: used honestly, it is a piece of corroboration, not a proof. But it is a surprisingly stubborn piece.

Objection — “A careful later author could just research the right names”

An educated writer with access to Jewish sources could have studied which names were common and salted his story accordingly to sound authentic.

Reply

That demands a feat no ancient author is known to have attempted: there were no name-frequency tables to consult, and matching the relative ratios — not just including common names but getting their proportions and the nicknaming patterns right across dozens of incidental characters — is statistical research no first- or second-century writer had the tools to perform.

Objection — Tuckett: the knowledge travelled Hardest

Christopher Tuckett does not deny that the names match Palestine; he calls the book a “tour de force.” His point is sharper. Bauckham wants to conclude that the names could not have been invented from outside Palestine. But, Tuckett notes, what that conclusion really requires is evidence that people outside Palestine were unaware of Palestinian names—and that is exactly the evidence we lack. Jews were mobile; Palestinian Jews lived in Rome and Alexandria, pilgrims streamed to Jerusalem, and Josephus himself wrote his Palestinian narratives in Rome for a Greco-Roman audience. So a sufficiently well-informed insider—a diaspora Jew steeped in Palestinian tradition—might in principle have reproduced the right names without being a Galilean eyewitness. The onomastic data therefore underdetermines the eyewitness conclusion.

Reply

Grant the logic and narrow the claim: Tuckett is right that the names cannot, by themselves, prove that a Galilean fisherman wrote them down. But the “skilled diaspora mimic” hypothesis requires an author who not only knew which Palestinian names were common but reproduced their relative frequencies across dozens of incidental characters, and who deployed disambiguating nicknames on exactly the over-common names—all while having no frequency tables to work from and, so far as we know, no awareness that anyone would ever check. Josephus could write accurate Palestinian narratives in Rome because he was a Palestinian writing from memory; he is evidence that the knowledge travels with such a person, not that it could be reconstructed by a Gentile or a third-generation diaspora author from scratch. Tuckett shows the door is not bolted; he does not show that anyone of the relevant profile is known to have walked through it. The most economical explanation of an unconscious statistical fingerprint remains that it was left by people who actually lived in that world—whether the named eyewitnesses Bauckham argues for, or, at minimum, the early Palestinian tradition the names entered before the books were written far away.

The room: Ilan was not doing apologetics; she was building a reference work. Richard Bauckham ran the test on the Gospels in Jesus and the Eyewitnesses (2006); Peter J. Williams has popularized it in Can We Trust the Gospels? (2018). The underlying facts are not seriously disputed — no one denies that name frequency is local and time-bound. Tuckett grants the match but denies it isolates eyewitnesses specifically.
Defending Premise 2

The names in the Gospels and Acts match the statistical profile of Jewish Palestine in roughly 330 BCE–200 CE — in their specific names, their relative frequencies, and their disambiguation habits, attested in independent sources

Precision check: the claim is a match to Jewish Palestine specifically, and it is a claim about provenance — of when and where these traditions were really formed. It does not prove that the named individuals are eyewitness authors, does not by itself establish that any reported event happened, and does not establish the miraculous. The real work — and the real debate — lies here: does the data genuinely match, and how tightly?

The ratios line up Corroboration · strength debated

Working from Ilan’s data (which itself includes Josephus), Bauckham found that the most popular Jewish male name in Palestine was Simon, followed by Joseph—and these are exactly the top two in the Gospels and Acts. In Ilan’s catalogue of Palestinian men, about 15.6% bore one of those two names; in the Gospels and Acts, about 18.2% of named men do. Widen the lens to the nine most popular male names—Simon, Joseph, Lazarus (Eleazar), Judas (Judah), John (Yohanan), Jesus (Joshua), Ananias (Hananiah), Jonathan, and Matthew (Mattathias)—and the fit holds: roughly 41.5% of Palestinian men in the data carry one of these nine, against about 40.3% in the New Testament narratives.

Honest limit: the sample is modest, and Gregor and Blais are right that one should not oversell the precision of any single ratio drawn from a few dozen names; the women’s figures in particular wobble, and individual anomalies are real.

The women’s names Looser fit

The two most popular female names in the Palestinian record were Mary (Mariam) and Salome; in the Gospels and Acts these are again the two commonest. The fit is looser here—about 28.6% of women in the external data bear one of the two, against about 38.9% in the New Testament—but the rank order and the broad shape match. The smaller female sample makes these figures less stable than the male ones.

The disambiguation habits match The most elegant confirmation

Because a handful of names were so over-common, real first-century Palestinian Jews had a practical problem: in any crowd there were too many Simons and Josephs to keep straight. Their solution was to tag people—by a patronymic (Simon bar-Jonah), a hometown (Joseph of Arimathea, Mary Magdalene), a nickname (Simon the Zealot, Simon the Leper), or a trade. The Gospels apply this disambiguation selectively, and apply it to exactly the names that needed it—the over-common ones—while rare names are usually left bare. That is not the behavior of a writer sprinkling in local color. It is the unconscious behavior of a culture that genuinely had too many Simons.

Two further controls Contested exchange

When the Gospel and Acts names are tested against the same Palestinian baseline alongside the names in Josephus, a first-century Palestinian Jew, they fit it at least as well as Josephus does (Van de Weghe and Wilson, 2024)—so the Gospels behave like a genuine Palestinian sample, not an invented one. And the Gospels match Palestine specifically. They do not match the diaspora profile. If these were late Greek compositions written far from Judea, that is the last thing we should expect.

Honest limit: Gregor and Blais have in turn replied, and the statistical exchange is ongoing; the weight of the case therefore rests on the narrow provenance claim, not on the verdict that “the statisticians settled it.”

A second, independent line: “sea” or “lake” Suggestive

Matthew, Mark, and John, whose narratives are steeped in the Palestinian scene, call the modest inland body of water beside which so much of the action unfolds a sea (Greek thalassa), the grand, local term for it. Luke never does: he calls it, more precisely, a lake (limnē)—the word a man who knew the Mediterranean would naturally choose for so small a body of water, and, on the ancient tradition that Luke was connected with Antioch on the Orontes, exactly the usage one would expect from him. It is checkable in the Greek and owes nothing to the frequency of names.

Honest limit: it is a strand of the same cumulative rope, not a proof—the Antioch connection is itself a tradition, so the point is suggestive rather than decisive.

Objection — “The sample is too small for the statistics to mean anything”

Gregor and Blais (Journal for the Study of the Historical Jesus, 2023) argue, first, that the New Testament sample is simply too small for the law of large numbers to do reliable work—a few dozen named people cannot establish a tight statistical fit. Second, they identify what they regard as anomalies: certain names appear in the Gospels at frequencies that, on a closer look, sit awkwardly with the external data. Third, they contend that Bauckham’s thesis of eyewitness sourcing offers no statistical advantage over a rival model they call “anonymous community transmission”—a story in which the names accrued through a Palestinian Christian community over time, with no need for named eyewitnesses behind them. If a non-eyewitness model predicts the same data, the name fit cannot be evidence specifically for eyewitnesses.

Reply

The sample is modest but not tiny, and the match is multi-layered: not just “the common names appear,” but their rank order tracks the independent data, and the nicknaming falls on exactly the over-represented names. A chance fit on one of those is plausible; a simultaneous fit on all three is much harder to produce by chance. In 2024 Van de Weghe and Wilson—the latter a working statistician—replied that Gregor and Blais’s negative verdict followed from questionable testing choices and data selection; using a chi-squared goodness-of-fit test, they found that the name occurrences in the Gospels and Acts fit their Palestinian context at least as well as the names in Josephus do, and markedly better than names drawn from ancient fiction or modern historical novels. On the published evidence, it behaves like a sample drawn from the population, not like a sample drawn from an author’s imagination. Two honest concessions are owed: the sample is modest, and Gregor and Blais’s demand that the eyewitness hypothesis be tested against rival explanations is exactly the right demand. The exchange is ongoing, so rest on the narrow provenance claim. Note, too, that the rival model Gregor and Blais propose is itself a story of names accruing through a Palestinian Christian community; even granted in full, their third point grants that the names entered the tradition in Palestine—the core of the provenance claim defended here.

The room: Bauckham, Williams, and Van de Weghe with the statistician Jason Wilson hold that the fit is tight enough to point to early, locally grounded—and probably eyewitness—sources. Gregor and Blais argue the sample is too thin to license that inference and that alternative transmission models fit equally well; Tuckett grants the match but denies it isolates eyewitnesses specifically. The Jewishness and Palestinian flavor of the names is broadly accepted across the spectrum; this is a real, ongoing argument among specialists about strength and reach. The responsible verdict is that the onomastic data is genuine and significant corroborating evidence for an early Palestinian provenance, while the maximal claim—that it proves named eyewitnesses—remains contested and should be held with a lighter grip.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

This is what separates someone who knows the argument from someone who can defend it. Each gets its best form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: Tuckett’s — Josephus wrote Palestinian narratives in Rome, so the knowledge travelled. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"A careful later author could just research the right names" Common

Strongest form

An educated writer with access to Jewish sources could have studied which names were common and salted his story accordingly to sound authentic.

Reply

That demands a feat no ancient author is known to have attempted: there were no name-frequency tables to consult, and matching the relative ratios — not just including common names but getting their proportions and the nicknaming patterns right across dozens of incidental characters — is statistical research no first- or second-century writer had the tools to perform.

"The sample is too small for the statistics to mean anything" Trickier

Strongest form

The Gospels name only a few dozen people. With so small a sample, a rough match to the top Palestinian names could easily be coincidence — you can't run real statistics on it.

Reply

The sample is modest but not tiny, and the match is multi-layered: not just "the common names appear," but their rank order tracks the independent data, and the nicknaming falls on exactly the over-represented names. A chance fit on one of those is plausible; a simultaneous fit on all three is much harder to produce by chance. Bauckham also tested it against the wrong baseline — the diaspora — and the Gospels fit early Palestine specifically, which a coincidence wouldn't predict. Gregor and Blais (JSHJ 2023) press exactly this; Van de Weghe and Wilson (2024) reply that the fit is at least as good as Josephus's and far better than fiction's. The exchange is ongoing, so rest on the narrow provenance claim.

"Even genuine early names don't prove the miracles" Trickier

Strongest form

Grant that the names show early Palestinian roots. Eyewitness-based traditions can still grow legends; accurate names don't certify the resurrection or any supernatural claim.

Reply

Correct, and the argument's reach is deliberately limited. It targets the popular theory that the Gospels are late, geographically distant compositions cut off from the events. The onomastic fit makes that theory very hard to hold: the traditions trace back to people who lived in first-century Palestine. That doesn't settle the miracles by itself, but it relocates the debate onto early, locally-grounded testimony rather than far-removed legend. It addresses provenance, not the resurrection — the inference this Library defends elsewhere, that Jesus rose bodily from the dead.

"Ilan's data is itself patchy and skewed" Hard

Strongest form

The name database draws on inscriptions, ossuaries and texts that survive unevenly — over-representing the wealthy, the literate, or particular regions. If the baseline is biased, the "match" is built on sand.

Reply

The baseline is imperfect, but it is not a single source: Ilan's lexicon pools Josephus, ossuaries, the Judean Desert texts and rabbinic sources, each skewed differently. And when the Gospel names are tested against that baseline alongside the names in Josephus, himself a first-century Palestinian Jew, they fit it at least as well as his do (Van de Weghe and Wilson, 2024). A match that survives across differently-biased material is unlikely to be an artifact of any one source's skew — though how precise the fit is remains a live statistical debate.

"The names were edited in later to add a layer of realism" Hard

Strongest form

Names could have been inserted or adjusted during transmission — copyists and redactors retrofitting plausible local names onto an originally vaguer story.

Reply

Even granting late tinkering, the editors would face the same difficulty as the original author: there were no frequency tables in antiquity, and the pattern is very hard to engineer from the outside. The realism is too systematic to be a plausible cosmetic later layer.

The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

The underlying facts are not seriously disputed: no one denies that name frequency is local and time-bound, and few deny that the Gospel names sit far closer to the Palestinian profile than to the diaspora one. The live disagreement is about strength and reach. Bauckham, Williams, and Van de Weghe with the statistician Jason Wilson hold that the fit is tight enough to point to early, locally grounded — and probably eyewitness — sources. Gregor and Blais argue the sample is too thin to license that inference and that alternative transmission models fit equally well; Tuckett grants the match but denies it isolates eyewitnesses specifically. Gregor and Blais have in turn replied to Van de Weghe and Wilson, and the statistical exchange is ongoing.

A fairness most defenders skip: The sample is modest, and one should not oversell the precision of any single ratio drawn from a few dozen names; the women’s figures in particular wobble, and individual anomalies are real. And Tuckett’s logical point is accurate: the onomastic data underdetermines the eyewitness conclusion. But that narrows the claim rather than defeating it. The fit is real and is not plausibly the product of invention, and that alone is enough to carry the narrow conclusion, which is about provenance; Tuckett shows the door is not bolted, but he does not show that anyone of the relevant profile is known to have walked through it.

The bounded verdict: The onomastic data is genuine and significant corroborating evidence for an early Palestinian provenance. The personal names woven through the Gospels and Acts carry the unconscious watermark of early-first-century Jewish Palestine — the right names, in roughly the right proportions, disambiguated in the right way — and conspicuously not the watermark of the diaspora. That makes the popular picture of the Gospels as late Greek legends untethered from Judea very difficult to maintain. The maximal claim — that it proves named eyewitnesses — remains contested and should be held with a lighter grip; and the argument addresses provenance and dating, not inerrancy or the resurrection; the resurrection — that Jesus rose bodily from the dead — is the inference this Library defends elsewhere.

    "The Gospels are crowded with Marys — the mother of Jesus, Magdalene, the sister of Martha, the mother of James, the wife of Clopas. Stories that cannot even keep their characters’ names distinct look like a muddle of merged legends, not reliable tradition."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    The sheer profusion of Marys is not a narrative embarrassment to be explained away; it is precisely what a real first-century Palestinian sample looks like. Mary (Mariam) and Salome were the two most popular female names in the Palestinian record, and in the Gospels and Acts they are again the two commonest. The fit is looser than for the men — about 38.9% of named women in the New Testament bear those two names against about 28.6% in the external data — and the smaller female sample makes those figures less stable, but the rank order and the broad shape match. And a culture with too many of one name kept its bearers apart by tagging them — by patronymic, hometown, nickname, or trade — as the Gospels do with Mary Magdalene. So the profusion of Marys is no sign of muddle: Mary was the commonest woman’s name, and the Gospels tag their Marys as that world did.
