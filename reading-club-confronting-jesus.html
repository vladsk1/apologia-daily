Rebecca McLaughlin walks through nine “encounters” — nine titles the Gospels give Jesus: the Jew, the Son, the King, the Healer, the Teacher, the Lover, the Servant, the Sacrifice, and the Lord. Read in their first-century Jewish context, they build one cumulative portrait of a man who stands exactly where only God stands — vindicated, in the end, by an empty tomb. This club follows the book encounter by encounter, with discussion questions designed for a church small group, and gentle enough to work through with a friend who hasn’t yet decided what to make of Jesus.

Sessions & Discussion

About this club

Read the book at your group’s own pace. Each session gives you the encounter to read and discussion questions to work through together — that’s all you need.

If you’d like to go further, a few optional Apologia Daily deep-dives are linked at the bottom of each session. They’re our own material, clearly marked, and easy to skip.

It is built to be led by an ordinary small-group host, not an expert. Every session follows the same simple shape: a short summary in our own words, the section to read, open discussion questions, and one line to carry into the week.

About this study

The sessions follow the book’s own structure — the introduction and all nine encounters, in order. This study has been prepared for Rebecca McLaughlin’s review; the summaries, questions and judgements in it are ours, not hers.

The session titles are our own working headings, and the summaries and discussion questions are ours too. Nothing on this page reproduces the book’s text. Every summary was written in our own words from a complete, cover-to-cover reading.

A few places carry a short note marked clearly as ours — our house standard for this kind of material, not the book’s. We state the full deity and full humanity of Christ together (the one person Jesus Christ is fully God and fully man); when the cross is in view we hold to the historic guardrail that Christ suffers in his humanity and the divine nature does not die; and at the trial scene we rest the weight on what Jesus did with Daniel’s vision — “coming on the clouds,” the way the Scriptures picture God’s own arrival — rather than on any contested reading of the figure in Daniel.

Where the book addresses sexual ethics (in the Teacher and Lover encounters), this study presents Rebecca McLaughlin’s own convictions in her own voice, as the book sets them out, rather than softening them. That — like following the book’s wording on the cross — is a deliberate choice for this author’s reading club, and not a change to how Apologia Daily handles these questions elsewhere on the site.

The extra Apologia Daily links are clearly labelled as our own optional resources — not part of the book, and not the author’s endorsement of them. A group can complete the whole club without ever opening one.

Finish the study · Mastery check

Can you introduce him — from the Gospels?

One capstone for the whole book — eight prompts that walk back through the nine encounters, in two modes. Flashcards: read a prompt, answer it in your head (or out loud to the group), then tap to reveal a model answer — McLaughlin’s argument, put in our own words and our own house tone. Self-rate quiz: take the same eight prompts one at a time, rate how well you answered each, and get an honest score at the end. If you can walk through all eight, you’re ready to introduce the Jesus of the Gospels to someone else — warmly, and from the Gospels themselves.

01 · The argument
What is Confronting Jesus actually arguing?
Tap to reveal →
That the Jesus of the Gospels, read in his first-century Jewish context, is not a good teacher later mistaken for God but God come among us — shown across nine titles (Jew, Son, King, Healer, Teacher, Lover, Servant, Sacrifice, Lord) and vindicated by the resurrection. It’s a cumulative portrait, written for believers and honest skeptics alike, offered as the best explanation of the evidence rather than a proof that bypasses faith.
02 · The method
Why does she open with Hamilton?
Tap to reveal →
The hit musical gives us a gripping Alexander Hamilton without ever claiming to report his actual words. The Gospels claim the opposite: not a dramatization but eyewitness-rooted testimony to what Jesus truly said and did. So before asking who Jesus is, McLaughlin asks whether the Gospels are the kind of sources that can tell us — and argues they are: early, eyewitness-based (Bauckham), and far better attested than most people assume.
03 · The sources
Can we actually trust the Gospels?
Tap to reveal →
On several fronts, yes. Even skeptics like Bart Ehrman grant that Jesus existed (“virtually every expert on the planet”). The Gospels carry named eyewitness testimony, written as those witnesses were dying out. Nicaea didn’t “pick” them — the four were already in place, and rivals like the Gospel of Thomas are later. The thousands of variants are what let scholars triangulate back to the original wording, and no core claim hangs on a disputed reading. (Preservation shows the text is intact; whether it’s true is argued separately.)
04 · The Son
Is Jesus really God?
Tap to reveal →
The Gospels show him doing what only God can do — forgiving sins (“who can forgive sins but God alone?”, Mark 2:7), commanding wind and sea, taking the divine “I am” on his lips (John 8:58) — and a doubter ending on his knees: “My Lord and my God” (John 20:28). What the church confesses is not a man promoted but God stooping down: the one person Jesus Christ is fully God and fully man, the eternally-begotten Son — never a child by a consort.
05 · The King
What kind of King is he?
Tap to reveal →
The promised Messiah in David’s line — but one who turns power upside down. His throne is a cross; his glory is to lift the despised. He comes in two stages: grace now, judgment later (Luke 4). At his trial he answers with Daniel’s Son of Man “coming with the clouds” (Matt 26:63–64) — the way the Old Testament pictures God himself arriving. The force is in what he does with that scene, not in settling who the figure is in Daniel’s own vision.
06 · Morals
Where do our deepest morals come from?
Tap to reveal →
McLaughlin argues the convictions we treasure — equal human worth, care for the weak, love even of enemies — are a Christian inheritance, “the ethical raft Jesus built,” echoed by non-Christian historians like Tom Holland. (Our note: where a moral idea came from doesn’t by itself prove it true — origins and truth are different questions — so this is a striking historical witness, not a proof.) And his teaching searches everyone’s heart, the hearer included.
07 · The cross
Why did Jesus have to die?
Tap to reveal →
McLaughlin reads the cross through Israel’s whole story — Isaac on Moriah, the Passover lamb, the Day of Atonement, the temple — and shows Jesus as both the Lamb who is sacrificed and the temple where God and humanity meet. Her answer is substitution: Jesus takes the judgment we deserved, drinking the cup of God’s wrath in our place. (Our one guardrail: Christ suffers in his humanity — the Trinity is never divided and the divine nature does not die.)
08 · The 60-second version
Did it actually happen — and the whole book over coffee?
Tap to reveal →
The resurrection is early (in the first Christian texts, and the creed Paul “received” within a few years), unexpected (the disciples disbelieved at first), and attested by witnesses no one would invent (the women). The 60-second version: one real first-century Jew is shown, title by title, to stand where only God stands — and the empty tomb is God’s own vindication of the claim. If he is risen, he is Lord of everything, and the fitting response is Thomas’s: “My Lord and my God.”

Every answer puts Rebecca McLaughlin’s argument into our own words and our own house tone, except where a note is marked as ours — nothing reproduces the book’s text, and this capstone is optional. Switch between flashcards and the self-rated quiz above.
