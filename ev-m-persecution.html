What the Early Church Suffered — and Why It Held

The deep dive tells you what the argument is. This track makes you able to use it — reconstruct it from memory, answer its hardest objections, and hold the line in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

Around the year 112 a Roman governor executed people accused of a strange new religion — and the striking thing is that every one of them could have walked free that morning. A pinch of incense before the emperor’s image, a prayer to the gods, a curse on the name of Christ, and they were released. The ones who died, died because they would not. The early Christians were not victims of a manhunt they could not escape; they were people handed an exit and refusing to take it.

We are not depending on pious legend for this. Trajan’s policy made “the name itself” the crime; the libelli — signed certificates of sacrifice recovered from the Egyptian sand — prove the pressure was real and mortal; and a core of sober, early records names the people who died: Polycarp, the Scillitan martyrs, the letter of Lyons, Perpetua and Felicity. Above all, hostile pagan writers — Pliny, Tacitus, Lucian, Galen — recorded a courage they could not explain, which is worth more than any friendly testimonial.

But the scope must stay honest, and this track keeps it honest. Candida Moss is right that the “Age of Martyrs” was overstated: persecution was usually episodic and local, and some later legends are embroidered. That correction trims the frequency and the legend — it leaves the documented core standing, for the martyrs really did die rather than deny Christ. And dying proves sincerity, not truth. So this argument shows the first Christians believed with their eyes open and were no cynics; it does not by itself show the faith is true. That case rests on the resurrection, argued elsewhere in the Library.

Understand it

Not more prose — the load-bearing structure. See the skeleton, then learn to defend each bone.

The argument, formally

Three lines. The whole weight rests on the two premises.

P1

For roughly three centuries it was legally lethal simply to be a known Christian, yet a steady stream of believers — slaves, mothers, aging bishops, teenagers — chose death rather than deny Christ, attested by hostile witnesses and the earliest documentary records.

P2

Each of them was offered an easy exit — a pinch of incense and a curse on Christ bought instant freedom — so their choice of death is best explained not by cynical pretence but by sincere conviction that Jesus is risen and Lord.

∴ C

Therefore the courage of the early martyrs is documented history, not legend, and proves the sincerity of their faith — though sincerity is not truth; that Christianity is true rests on the resurrection, argued elsewhere.

This is an inference to the best explanation, and its conclusion is deliberately bounded: it establishes that the martyrs were real and sincere and rules out cynical pretence. It is not an argument that Christianity is true — dying for a belief never shows that.

Then the honest step — what courage does and does not show

Facing death for a conviction shows a person meant it; it never shows the conviction was correct. Keep the two apart.

Defending the premises

Why each premise is more plausible than its denial

Support for P1 — the danger and the courage are documented, not legendary

The name itself was the crime

Pliny’s letter to Trajan (c. 112) is the earliest Roman government document on Christians, and Trajan’s reply set the policy for over a century: no charge of arson or sedition was needed — the offence was being a Christian and admitting it out loud. To risk death, a believer had only to keep the name.

Hard evidence, not pious memory

In 250 the emperor Decius ordered every inhabitant to sacrifice and obtain a signed libellus proving it; roughly forty-six of these certificates have been recovered from the Egyptian sand and published. And a core of sober, early accounts names the martyrs: Polycarp of Smyrna (c. 155), the Scillitan martyrs (dated 17 July 180 — the earliest dated document of the Latin church), the letter of Lyons (177), and Perpetua and Felicity (203), whose prison diary is the earliest known writing by a Christian woman.

Support for P2 — the exit was real, so death was chosen, ruling out pretence

An exit was always on the table

Pliny released everyone who invoked the gods and cursed Christ; Decius’s certificate was there for the signing; the Scillitans were offered thirty days to reconsider; Perpetua’s own father begged her, again and again, to think of her baby and simply deny the name. The escape was cheap and instant — and they refused it.

Enemies could not explain it

The strongest witness is hostile. Lucian, mocking, records that the Christians “despise death and even willingly give themselves into custody”; Galen, the greatest doctor of the age, notes their “contempt of death” and a discipline “not inferior to that of genuine philosophers.” They do not deny the courage; they cannot account for it — and that incomprehension is worth more than a hundred friendly testimonials.

The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

This is what separates someone who knows the martyr story from someone who can defend it. Each gets its best form first, then the reply — and the objection that matters most is flagged above.

The one that matters most: the sceptic’s best move is not to deny the courage but to say it proves nothing — people die for beliefs that turn out false, so martyrdom is no truth-detector. That is correct, and this track concedes it in our own voice. What the courage does prove is narrower but real: sincerity, not cynical pretence. The case that the faith is true is a separate one, and rests on the resurrection.

“People die for false beliefs all the time — martyrdom proves nothing” Common

Strongest form

History is full of sincere people dying for causes that were mistaken — suicide bombers, cult members, doomed political movements. Courage is not a truth-detector, so the martyrs tell you nothing about whether Christianity is true.

Reply

Granted — and the track says so plainly: dying for a belief proves sincerity, not truth. But it does prove something, and something worth having: it rules out cynical pretence. You do not go to the beasts to keep up an act. So these Christians genuinely believed Jesus was risen and Lord, and held it with their eyes open. Whether the first witnesses could have died for something they knew to be false is a sharper question — and it belongs with the evidence for the resurrection, argued elsewhere in the Library.

“The persecution is basically a myth — Candida Moss showed the martyr story was invented” Trickier

Strongest form

In The Myth of Persecution, the historian Candida Moss argues that the cherished picture of a church under relentless three-century assault — the tidy “ten great persecutions,” an unbroken “Age of Martyrs” — is a later construction, and specialists broadly agree with her.

Reply

Concede it, honestly and in full: for most of these centuries most Christians in most places were not being hunted. Persecution was usually episodic and often local, with only Decius, Valerian and Diocletian mounting anything empire-wide, and some later legends are embroidered. But look at what the correction touches. It trims the frequency and the legend; it leaves the core exactly where it was. Moss herself singles out Polycarp, the Scillitans, the letter of Lyons, and Perpetua as standing closest to authentic records — and even she does not deny these people really died rather than renounce Christ. A danger that was intermittent was still a danger; it did not make the sword less sharp for Polycarp. As Wolfram Kinzig has urged in reply, the sources will not let us shrink the persecutions to nothing.

“These martyr stories are just later church legends” Trickier

Strongest form

Martyr acts were written by the church, for the church, to inspire. The narrative detail is pious embroidery — how would we ever separate what happened from what got added?

Reply

Some later accounts are embroidered, and a few are fiction — say so. But a core is singled out even by sceptical specialists as the earliest and best evidence we have: the Martyrdom of Polycarp, the Acts of the Scillitan Martyrs (a court record), the letter describing Lyons, and Perpetua’s prison diary. Critics dispute how much of the detail is later shaping — a fair point — but not that these people really died refusing to deny Christ, which is the bare fact the argument needs. And the picture is corroborated from outside: Pliny and Tacitus record the executions, Lucian and Galen the Christians’ startling readiness to face them.

“Nero didn’t even really target Christians — the whole thing is exaggerated from the start” Trickier

Strongest form

Some historians (following Brent Shaw) doubt that Nero’s scapegoating after the fire of 64 targeted Christians as Christians so early. If the founding episode is shaky, the rest is inflation.

Reply

The debate over Nero is real and worth knowing — Shaw questions the traditional reading, Christopher Jones defends it, and both accept the Tacitus passage is genuine. But do not rest the case there. The firmer ground is Pliny onward, from about 112, and there the pattern is not in dispute: an empire that made the name a capital offence, believers handed an exit and refusing it, and hostile observers baffled by the result. Concede the disputed edge; the datable core holds.

Touch the originals

Where this comes from — primary sources, not our summary

Pliny the Youngerc. AD 112 · Letters 10.96
A Roman governor’s report to Trajan — the earliest government document on Christians. Pliny interrogated the accused three times, released those who cursed Christ, and executed those who would not, and asks whether “the name itself” should be punished.
The Martyrdom of Polycarpc. AD 155 · from Smyrna
The earliest surviving Christian martyrdom narrative. The aged bishop, pressed to curse Christ, answers: “Eighty and six years have I served Him, and He never did me any injury: how then can I blaspheme my King and my Saviour?”
Perpetua & FelicityCarthage, AD 203
A well-born nursing mother and her pregnant slave, who went into the arena together. The central section is Perpetua’s own first-person prison diary — the earliest known writing by a Christian woman — recording her father’s pleas to deny the name.
Lucian & Galen2nd c. · hostile pagan witnesses
The satirist Lucian mocks the Christians who “despise death”; the physician Galen (surviving only in Arabic) notes their “contempt of death… patent to us every day” and philosophic discipline. Enemies who confirm the courage they could not explain.
Candida Moss2013 · The Myth of Persecution
The sceptical case, worth reading in full: that the “ten persecutions” and unbroken “Age of Martyrs” are a later construction. Read alongside Wolfram Kinzig’s Christian Persecution in Antiquity (2021).
