What the Early Church Actually Suffered

The deep dive tells you what the argument is. This track makes you able to use it — reconstruct it from memory, answer its hardest objections, and hold the line in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

The Hollywood picture — three centuries of relentless hunting, believers always in the catacombs, an empire straining every nerve to stamp them out — is badly out of proportion, and the honest thing is to say so at the front. A believer handed the inflated version will meet a sceptic who dismantles it, and be left wondering what else will not survive contact.

But the fashionable correction — that the whole thing is essentially a Christian myth — overshoots in turn. Candida Moss’s deflation is largely right and should be granted: the “ten great persecutions” scheme is a later construction, most Christians most of the time were not being hunted, and the martyr acts are largely a devotional genre. Grant all of it and a solid body of evidence remains, much of it from hostile Roman officials: Trajan’s rescript making Christianity a capital charge on accusation, the Decian sacrifice certificates that survive on papyrus, and the church’s own fifty-year fight over those who complied.

Two limits keep the argument sound, and this track holds both. Martyrdom proves sincerity, not truth — it rules out deliberate fraud and nothing more. And the record establishes cost, not distinctiveness: Rome suppressed other groups too, so “they were persecuted” does not by itself set Christianity apart. Why the movement grew under that cost is a separate question, taken up in the companion argument on the church’s growth.

Understand it

Not more prose — the load-bearing structure. See the skeleton, then learn to defend each bone.

The argument, formally

Three lines. The whole weight rests on the two premises.

P1

Being a Christian carried a real, recurring, legally-backed capital liability from Trajan’s rescript (c. AD 112) through Diocletian — attested largely by hostile Roman sources and by the surviving sacrifice certificates, and confirmed by the church’s own records of mass compliance.

P2

The willingness of many to accept that cost rather than recant establishes the sincerity of their belief — it rules out deliberate fraud — but sincerity is not the same thing as truth, and the pressure was survivable, which the mass of the lapsed proves.

∴ C

Therefore the persecution was genuine but proportionate to neither the pious nor the sceptical legend; it shows the cost was real and the believers sincere, not that Christianity is true, and not that Christians were uniquely targeted.

The two premises are deliberately modest, and the conclusion is bounded on both sides at once — it refuses the inflated legend and the deflationary myth, and it stops short of both “this proves the faith” and “Christians were singled out.” A sceptic who attacks the strong version is attacking a claim this track does not make.

Then the honest step — what suffering does and does not show

Dying for a belief proves the believer meant it; it never proves the belief. Keep sincerity and truth apart.

Defending the premises

Why each premise is more plausible than its denial

Support for P1 — the liability was real, recurring, and law-backed

The legal machinery, from hostile hands

Trajan’s rescript to Pliny (c. AD 112) set the shape for a century and a half: Christians were not to be sought out, but those accused and unrepentant were executed — a standing capital liability any neighbour could turn lethal. This is Roman administrative correspondence, not a martyr legend.

The certificates that survive

Decius’s order of 250 required all inhabitants to sacrifice and obtain a certificate (libellus) before a commission. Between forty and fifty of these papyrus slips survive from Egypt, naming the petitioner and attesting he sacrificed — hard physical proof that the requirement was issued and administered.

The church’s own embarrassment

For fifty years afterward the churches tore themselves apart over the lapsi — those who sacrificed, bribed for a certificate, or fled. A church does not build a fifty-year discipline for apostates unless the pressure and the apostates were real. It is the surest proof precisely because it is the church admitting failure, not advertising heroism.

Support for P2 — sincerity, not truth; survivable, not heroic

What the deaths do establish

Accepting death rather than a token sacrifice rules out deliberate fraud on the part of those who died — you do not knowingly die for what you know to be a lie. That is a real and bounded result: it speaks to sincerity, not to the truth of what was believed.

And what they do not

People die for false beliefs constantly, so martyrdom cannot prove a belief true. And the mass of the lapsed shows the pressure was survivable — the early church was not made of a stronger grade of person, but of people who mostly wanted to live and then had to work out how to forgive the ones who caved. Where believers did stand firm, the tradition credited grace (Philippians 1:29), not their own reserves.

The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

This is what separates someone who knows what the early church actually suffered from someone who can defend it. Each gets its best form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: Rome also suppressed the Bacchanalia, banned druidry, expelled Jews from the city, and legislated against the Manichaeans — so “a group was periodically illegal in Rome” is no evidence about that group. This track concedes it in our own voice: the record establishes cost, not distinctiveness. Why this movement grew under the cost is the companion argument’s job, not this one’s.

“The persecution was mostly a myth” Common

Strongest form

Modern scholarship (Candida Moss) has shown the “ten great persecutions” scheme is a later invention, the martyr stories are largely devotional fiction, and Christians were rarely being actively hunted. The whole picture is a pious exaggeration.

Reply

Concede almost all of it — and notice that what even Moss grants still refutes the flatter claim. Trajan’s rescript authorised execution for the name; the Decian sacrifice certificates survive on papyrus; Diocletian’s Great Persecution happened. Moss disputes Nero and disputes Rome’s intent; grant both and a recurring, state-backed capital liability remains, confirmed by the church’s own fifty-year fight over the lapsed.

“The martyrs’ deaths prove Christianity is true” Common

Strongest form

People do not die for what they know to be false. That so many Christians accepted death rather than recant is powerful evidence that their faith was true.

Reply

This one cuts against us and we grant it: martyrdom proves sincerity, not truth. People die for false beliefs constantly; what dying rules out is deliberate fraud, and only for those in a position to know whether they were lying — a narrower argument that concerns the apostles specifically, made in the companion essay on the disciples’ belief. The martyrs of 250 died sincerely; that alone settles nothing about the resurrection.

“It was prosecution under general law, not persecution of a belief” Trickier

Strongest form

Roman magistrates thought of themselves as keeping public order, not suppressing a religion — Christians were tried for disobedience, like anyone else. Calling it “persecution” imports a meaning the Romans did not have.

Reply

Grant the magistrate’s self-understanding — it does not dissolve the cost. A defendant executed for refusing to sacrifice, where sacrificing would have ended the matter on the spot, faces a religiously specific test; and Trajan’s rescript makes recantation the whole hinge of the procedure, which is precisely what a charge of ordinary criminality would not do. The liability was real whatever the magistrate believed he was doing.

“Christians weren’t special — Rome banned lots of groups” Trickier

Strongest form

Rome suppressed the Bacchanalia, the druids, the Manichaeans, and expelled Jews from the city. Christians were just one more group that ran afoul of Roman order; there is nothing distinctive to explain.

Reply

Correct, and this track concedes it rather than dodging: the record establishes cost, not distinctiveness. “A group was periodically illegal in Rome” is not by itself evidence about that group. But that is an argument for narrowing the claim, not abandoning it — the cost was real, and the separate, genuinely interesting question of why this movement grew under it is taken up in the companion argument on the church’s rise.

Touch the originals

Where this comes from — primary sources, not our summary

Pliny the Younger & Trajanc. AD 112 · Letters 10.96–97
The governor asks how to handle Christians; the emperor’s reply sets the policy for a century and a half — do not seek them out, disregard anonymous accusations, but execute the accused who will not recant. The legal shape of the whole period, in Rome’s own hand.
The Decian libelliAD 250 · papyri from Egypt
Between forty and fifty surviving sacrifice certificates, each naming the petitioner and attesting that he has sacrificed before the commission — physical proof that Decius’s order was issued, administered, and generating paperwork ordinary people had to obtain.
Cyprian of CarthageAD 251 · On the Lapsed
A bishop’s treatise wrestling with what to do about the mass of Christians who sacrificed or bought certificates under Decius — the church’s own record that the pressure was real and that most did not endure it.
Candida Moss2013 · The Myth of Persecution
The strongest modern case for deflation, steelmanned here rather than waved at — the “ten persecutions” scheme is late, the martyr acts are largely a genre, active hunting was brief. Most of it is granted; the residue is what this argument defends.

    One strong way to answer — in a 1 Peter 3:15 tone

    “You’re half right, and I’ll go further than you might expect — the ‘lions everywhere, three centuries of hiding in catacombs’ picture really is inflated, and Candida Moss is right that the tidy ‘ten persecutions’ scheme is a later invention. But grant all that and something solid is left. In 250 Decius makes everyone sacrifice and get a certificate, and we still have the actual certificates on papyrus. And here’s the tell: the churches then spent fifty years fighting bitterly about what to do with the people who caved. You don’t build that unless the danger was real and lots of people failed it. One honest thing, though — none of this proves Christianity is true. It proves the cost was real and the believers were sincere. Sincere people can be wrong; that’s a different argument.”
