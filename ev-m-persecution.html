What the Early Church Suffered — and Why It Held

The deep dive tells you what the argument is. This track makes you able to use it — reconstruct it from memory, answer its hardest objections, and hold the line in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

Around the year 112 a Roman governor executed people accused of a strange new religion — and the striking thing is that every one of them could have walked free that morning. A pinch of incense before the emperor’s image, a prayer to the gods, a curse on the name of Christ, and they were released. The ones who died, died because they would not. The early Christians were not victims of a manhunt they could not escape; they were people handed an exit and refusing to take it.

We are not depending on pious legend for this. Trajan’s policy made “the name itself” the crime; the libelli — signed certificates of sacrifice recovered from the Egyptian sand — prove the pressure was real and mortal; and a core of sober, early records names the people who died: Polycarp, the Scillitan martyrs, the letter of Lyons, Perpetua and Felicity. Above all, outsiders with no love for them — Pliny, Lucian, Galen — recorded a courage they could explain only as delusion or obstinacy, which is worth more than any friendly testimonial.

But the scope must stay honest, and this track keeps it honest. Candida Moss is right that the “Age of Martyrs” was overstated: persecution was usually episodic and local, and some later legends are embroidered. That correction trims the frequency and the legend — it leaves the documented core standing, for the martyrs really did die rather than deny Christ. And dying proves sincerity, not truth. So this argument shows the first Christians believed with their eyes open and were no cynics; it does not by itself show the faith is true. That case rests on the resurrection, argued elsewhere in the Library.

Not more prose — the load-bearing structure. See the skeleton, then learn to defend each bone.

The argument, formally

Three lines. The whole weight rests on the two premises.

P1

For roughly three centuries it was legally lethal simply to be a known Christian, yet a steady stream of believers — slaves, mothers, aging bishops, teenagers — chose death rather than deny Christ, attested by hostile witnesses and the earliest documentary records.

P2

Each of them was offered an easy exit — a pinch of incense and a curse on Christ bought instant freedom — so their choice of death is best explained not by cynical pretence but by sincere conviction that Jesus is risen and Lord.

∴ C

Therefore the courage of the early martyrs is documented history, not legend, and proves the sincerity of their faith — though sincerity is not truth; that Christianity is true rests on the resurrection, argued elsewhere.

This is an inference to the best explanation, and its conclusion is deliberately bounded: it establishes that the martyrs were real and sincere and rules out cynical pretence. It is not an argument that Christianity is true — dying for a belief never shows that.

Then the honest step — what courage does and does not show

Facing death for a conviction shows a person meant it; it never shows the conviction was correct. Keep the two apart.

Defending Premise 1

For roughly three centuries it was legally lethal simply to be a known Christian, yet a steady stream of believers — slaves, mothers, aging bishops, teenagers — chose death rather than deny Christ, attested by hostile witnesses and the earliest documentary records

Precision check: the claim is not that Christians were hunted for three centuries straight. For most of these centuries most Christians in most places were not being hunted; persecution was usually episodic and often local, and only under Decius (250), Valerian (257–258), and Diocletian (from 303) was there anything like an empire-wide, top-down campaign. The correction cuts the frequency, not the fact: to be a known Christian was legally to risk death for the whole period, and men, women and slaves paid that price rather than deny Christ.

The name itself was the crime The core evidence

Pliny’s letter to Trajan (c. AD 112) is the earliest surviving Roman government document on how to handle Christians. Trajan’s reply became the working policy of the empire for over a century: Christians were not to be hunted down (conquirendi non sunt); anonymous accusations were to be ignored; but anyone properly accused and convicted, who would not recant, was to be punished. There is no charge of arson, sedition, or any specific act. The offence is the name — being a Christian, and admitting it out loud. Pliny had wondered whether it was “the name itself” that should be punished, or only crimes attached to the name; Trajan’s answer, in effect, was: the name.

The certificates in the sand Strong · documentary

In the year 250 the emperor Decius issued an empire-wide order: every inhabitant was to sacrifice to the gods and obtain a signed, witnessed certificate — a libellus — proving he had done so. Roughly forty-six of these certificates have been recovered from the dry sands of Egypt, on papyrus, and published. They are mundane, bureaucratic, and utterly damning. The certificates prove that the pressure was real and mortal.

Honest limit: The pressure was real enough that complying was a genuine temptation, and some did comply — Cyprian’s writings show that many Christians obtained such a certificate anyway. But that is exactly what throws the others into relief: behind every one of them stands the unwritten record of those who would not sign, and faced the magistrate instead.

The earliest accounts, named Strong · with the detail contested

We can name them — not all of them, but enough, in accounts sober and specific enough that even the historians most sceptical of martyr literature acknowledge them as the earliest and best-attested we possess. Polycarp of Smyrna, around 155 or 156, pressed by the proconsul to swear by the emperor’s fortune and curse Christ: “Eighty and six years have I served Him, and He never did me any injury: how then can I blaspheme my King and my Saviour?” The Scillitan martyrs — seven men and five women, tried at Carthage on 17 July 180 — whose court record survives and is the earliest dated document of the Latin-speaking church. Perpetua, in 203, whose own prison diary is the earliest known writing by a Christian woman. And the letter from Lyons (177), whose hero is Blandina, a slave girl her fellow Christians feared would be too frail to hold out.

Honest limit: Candida Moss disputes how much of the narrative detail in these accounts is later shaping, and on some of that detail the evidence is with her. Moss holds that none of them is wholly accurate history, and argues that even these were edited by later hands. The core needs only the bare fact that Christians really died for refusing to renounce Christ.

An eyewitness from the Great Persecution Strong

From the last of the empire-wide campaigns, the Great Persecution that Diocletian began in 303, we even have an eyewitness: Phileas, bishop of Thmuis in Egypt, wrote from prison in Alexandria to his own church, describing the tortures he had seen fellow-believers endure, and how, when offered release if they would touch the sacrifice, they “did not hesitate, but went to death cheerfully” — and he was himself beheaded.

Corroborated from outside Hostile Roman witness · the Nero episode contested

Even the earliest and most brutal episode, Nero’s scapegoating of the Christians after the great fire of 64, is known to us mainly through the pagan historian Tacitus, who despised the sect yet recorded that “an immense multitude” was condemned, convicted less of arson than of “hatred of the human race,” and put to death with a cruelty that stirred even Roman pity.

Honest limit: Some historians, following Brent Shaw, now doubt whether that particular episode targeted Christians as Christians so early; others, like Christopher Jones, defend the traditional reading. The debate is real and worth knowing — but the firmer ground is Pliny onward, and there the pattern is not in dispute.

Objection — Moss: the ‘Age of Martyrs’ is a later construction

The historian Candida Moss, in The Myth of Persecution, argues that the cherished picture of a church under relentless, three-century assault — the tidy scheme of “ten great persecutions,” an unbroken “Age of Martyrs” — is a later construction. Specialists broadly agree with her on this. Some later martyr legends are embroidered, and a few are fiction. Moss presses two further points at full strength: that none of the early martyr stories, even the best, is wholly accurate history; and that Rome, by its own lights, was prosecuting obstinate and disloyal subjects rather than persecuting a religion.

Reply

It is worth conceding plainly: for most of these centuries most Christians in most places were not being hunted. But look closely at what this concession does and does not touch. It trims the frequency and the legend; it leaves the core exactly where it was. Moss herself acknowledges a small set of accounts that scholars treat — among them Polycarp, the Scillitan martyrs, the letter of Lyons, and Perpetua and Felicity — as the earliest and closest to authentic records. Moss does not deny that Christians really died for refusing to renounce Christ, and her quarrel with these accounts is over how the deaths were later told, not over whether they happened. And a danger that is intermittent is still a danger. As the historian Wolfram Kinzig has recently urged in reply to the deflationary mood, the sources will not let us shrink the persecutions to nothing; the martyrs were real people who really died, and the courage was not a myth.

Objection — “Nero didn’t even really target Christians”

Some historians (following Brent Shaw) doubt that Nero’s scapegoating after the fire of 64 targeted Christians as Christians so early. If the founding episode is shaky, the rest is inflation.

Reply

The debate is real and worth knowing, but the firmer ground is Pliny onward, and there the pattern is not in dispute. Concede the disputed edge; the datable core holds.

The room: Candida Moss presses the point hardest — frequency and legend — and specialists broadly agree with her on that; Wolfram Kinzig replies that the sources will not let us shrink the persecutions to nothing. Brent Shaw doubts the Neronian episode; Christopher Jones defends it. Tacitus, who despised the sect, and Pliny are the hostile Roman witnesses.
Defending Premise 2

Each of them was offered an easy exit — a pinch of incense and a curse on Christ bought instant freedom — so their choice of death is best explained not by cynical pretence but by sincere conviction that Jesus is risen and Lord

Precision check: an inference to the best explanation, deliberately bounded. It establishes that the martyrs were real and sincere and rules out cynical pretence. Dying for a belief proves sincerity, not truth — it is not an argument that Christianity is true.

The exit was always on the table Strong

The people in front of Pliny could have walked free at any moment. A pinch of incense before the emperor’s image, a prayer to the Roman gods, a curse on the name of Christ — Pliny offered all of it, and released everyone who took the offer. The ones who died, died because they would not. The early Christians were not victims of a manhunt they could not escape. They were people who were handed an exit and refused to take it. To be delivered from danger, a believer did not have to renounce Christ in his heart or betray anyone else; he had only to perform a small public gesture. Decius’s certificate was there for the signing; the Scillitans were offered thirty days to reconsider; Perpetua’s father begged her, again and again, to think of her baby and simply deny the name. She would not.

Enemies could explain it only as delusion or obstinacy The strongest part of the evidence

It comes not from Christians but from their cultured despisers. Lucian, mocking, wrote: “The poor wretches have convinced themselves, first and foremost, that they are going to be immortal and live for all time, in consequence of which they despise death and even willingly give themselves into custody.” Galen, the most famous doctor of the ancient world, in a passage that survives only in Arabic translation, remarks that Christians include people whose “contempt of death is patent to us every day,” along with a self-discipline in food and conduct “not inferior to that of genuine philosophers.” They do not deny the courage; they can explain it only as delusion or obstinacy. To a Roman, a willingness to die for a conviction, quietly and without a sword in your hand, was not heroism but a kind of madness — and that incomprehension is worth more than a hundred friendly testimonials.

Honest limit: Galen also complains that Christians draw their faith from parables rather than demonstration. Marcus Aurelius, in his private notebook, seems to gesture at the same stubbornness — though many editors regard the words “like the Christians” as a later reader’s note that crept into the text, so this witness must be handled with care.

“The blood of Christians is seed” A partisan’s rhetoric · a true report

Tertullian, around the year 197, did not write “the blood of the martyrs is the seed of the church.” What he wrote is sharper: “The oftener we are mown down by you, the more in number we grow; the blood of Christians is seed.” As a report of something Tertullian could see with his own eyes — a movement that executions were failing to extinguish, that kept producing more Speratuses and Blandinas than the courts could silence — it was simply true. A faith that its enemies could not kill out of existence, and that its own adherents would not buy their way out of, is a historical fact that wants explaining.

Honest limit: It is a partisan’s rhetoric, not a demographic law; persecution did not mechanically multiply believers, and the church grew for many reasons in seasons of peace as well as pressure.

What it rules out — and what it does not Sincerity, not truth

Dying for a belief proves sincerity, not truth. People have laid down their lives for causes that were mistaken, and courage is not a truth-detector. What martyrdom does rule out is cynical pretence. These were not people play-acting a faith for gain; you do not go to the beasts to keep up an act. Their deaths prove that these Christians sincerely believed what they had been taught — that Jesus is Lord and risen — and held it with their eyes open.

Honest limit: Whether the first witnesses could have died for something they knew to be false is a separate and even sharper question, and it belongs with the evidence for the resurrection, argued elsewhere in this Library. The case that Christianity is true does not rest here; it rests, above all, on the resurrection of Jesus.

Objection — “People die for false beliefs all the time” Hardest

History is full of sincere people dying for causes that were mistaken. Courage is not a truth-detector, so the martyrs tell you nothing about whether Christianity is true.

Reply

Granted — and the track says so plainly: dying for a belief proves sincerity, not truth. But it does prove something, and something worth having: it rules out cynical pretence. You do not go to the beasts to keep up an act. So these Christians genuinely believed Jesus was risen and Lord, and held it with their eyes open. Whether the first witnesses could have died for something they knew to be false is a sharper question — and it belongs with the evidence for the resurrection, argued elsewhere in the Library.

Objection — Moss: Rome was prosecuting disloyalty, not a religion

Rome, by its own lights, was prosecuting obstinate and disloyal subjects rather than persecuting a religion.

Reply

This is answered by the documents already quoted: Pliny asked whether “the name itself” was the crime, Trajan’s answer in effect made it so, and anyone who would sacrifice and curse Christ walked free. Whatever Rome called the proceedings, the one thing that sent these people to their deaths was refusing to deny Christ.

The room: Lucian and Galen are the hostile witnesses who noticed the readiness to die; Tertullian is the partisan who boasted of it. Candida Moss presses that Rome saw disobedience rather than a religion; Wolfram Kinzig, replying to the deflationary mood, urges that the courage was not a myth.

The objections you will actually meet

This is what separates someone who knows the martyr story from someone who can defend it. Each gets its best form first, then the reply — and the objection that matters most is flagged below.

The one that matters most: the sceptic’s best move is not to deny the courage but to say it proves nothing — people die for beliefs that turn out false, so martyrdom is no truth-detector. That is correct, and this track concedes it in our own voice. What the courage does prove is narrower but real: sincerity, not cynical pretence. “These martyr stories are just later church legends” Trickier

Strongest form

Martyr acts were written by the church, for the church, to inspire. The narrative detail is pious embroidery — how would we ever separate what happened from what got added?

Reply

Some later accounts are embroidered, and a few are fiction — say so. But a core of accounts is acknowledged even by sceptical specialists as the earliest and best evidence we have: the Martyrdom of Polycarp, the Acts of the Scillitan Martyrs (a court record), the letter describing Lyons, and Perpetua’s prison diary. Critics dispute how much of the detail is later shaping, and on some of that detail the evidence is with them — but not that Christians really died refusing to deny Christ; the quarrel is over how these deaths were later told, not whether they happened. That is the bare fact the argument needs. And the picture is corroborated from outside: Pliny and Tacitus record the executions, Lucian and Galen the Christians’ startling readiness to face them.

“Nero didn’t even really target Christians — the whole thing is exaggerated from the start” Trickier

Strongest form

Some historians (following Brent Shaw) doubt that Nero’s scapegoating after the fire of 64 targeted Christians as Christians so early. If the founding episode is shaky, the rest is inflation.

Reply

The debate over Nero is real and worth knowing — Shaw questions the traditional reading, Christopher Jones defends it, and both accept the Tacitus passage is genuine. But do not rest the case there. The firmer ground is Pliny onward, from about 112, and there the pattern is not in dispute: an empire that made the name a capital offence, believers handed an exit and refusing it, and hostile observers baffled by the result. Concede the disputed edge; the datable core holds.

Touch the originals

Where this comes from — primary sources, not our summary

Pliny the Youngerc. AD 112 · Letters 10.96
A Roman governor’s report to Trajan — the earliest government document on Christians. Pliny interrogated the accused three times, released those who cursed Christ, and executed those who would not, and asks whether “the name itself” should be punished.
The Martyrdom of Polycarpc. AD 155 · from Smyrna
The earliest surviving martyrdom narrative outside the New Testament. The aged bishop, pressed to curse Christ, answers: “Eighty and six years have I served Him, and He never did me any injury: how then can I blaspheme my King and my Saviour?”
Perpetua & FelicityCarthage, AD 203
A well-born nursing mother and a pregnant slave, Felicity, who went into the arena together. The central section is Perpetua’s own first-person prison diary — the earliest known writing by a Christian woman — recording her father’s pleas to deny the name.
Lucian & Galen2nd c. · hostile pagan witnesses
The satirist Lucian mocks the Christians who “despise death”; the physician Galen (surviving only in Arabic) notes their “contempt of death… patent to us every day” and philosophic discipline. Outsiders who confirm the courage they could explain only as delusion.
Candida Moss2013 · The Myth of Persecution
The sceptical case, worth reading in full: that the “ten persecutions” and unbroken “Age of Martyrs” are a later construction. Read alongside Wolfram Kinzig’s Christian Persecution in Antiquity (2021).
