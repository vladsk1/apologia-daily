The Manuscript Reliability Argument

The deep dive tells you what the argument is. This track makes you able to use it — reconstruct it from memory, answer its hardest objections, and hold the line in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

Are the New Testament manuscripts reliable? The evidence says we can reconstruct what the original authors wrote with a high degree of confidence. The text survives in over 5,800 Greek manuscripts, plus thousands more in Latin, Syriac, Coptic and other languages, and a vast number of quotations in the early church fathers — far more copies, reaching far closer to the originals, than for any other work of antiquity. The earliest fragment of John (P52) is commonly dated c. AD 125, within a few decades of composition — though paleographers dispute the range, with Orsini and Clarysse arguing for c. AD 125–175, and no case should lean on a single scrap. Because the copies branch into independent streams across regions and languages, scribal slips in one line are exposed by the others, letting scholars cross-check their way back toward the original wording.

Skeptics rightly note there are hundreds of thousands of textual variants — but that number is a by-product of having so many manuscripts, and the overwhelming majority are spelling differences, word order and obvious copying errors that change nothing in translation. Even the skeptical scholar Bart Ehrman concedes that no central Christian doctrine hangs on a disputed reading.

One honest caveat: this argument establishes that the text was accurately preserved, not that its contents are true. It removes the claim that we are reading a late legend rewritten over centuries, clearing the way for separate arguments about whether the eyewitnesses were honest and well-placed to know what they reported, and, above all, whether Jesus rose from the dead.

Before you start · key terms

Eight words this argument leans on

Learn these first — the objections below turn on them, and most weak versions of the argument come from blurring one of them.

Transmission, not truthThe question here is one of transmission, not truth: can we reconstruct, with confidence, what the original authors wrote? Establishing that we possess the authors’ words tells us nothing, by itself, about whether those words are true — the Iliad is splendidly preserved, and no one thinks that settles the existence of Zeus.
Textual criticismThe discipline of comparing the surviving witnesses to recover what an author wrote. It is far older than the apologetic use to which it is sometimes put, and it is the ordinary method applied to Homer, Plato, Tacitus, and every other ancient author — not special pleading invented for the Bible.
Branching transmissionManuscript transmission is not a single whispered chain, as in the game of telephone. Copies were carried to Egypt, Syria, Rome, and Asia Minor, translated into Latin, Coptic and Syriac, and quoted across the Mediterranean, so the streams cross-check one another; a vast, geographically scattered tradition cannot easily bury the original in every branch at once.
Textual variantAny point at which the manuscripts differ. Estimates of the total run somewhere between 200,000 and 400,000 (scholars’ estimates differ) — a by-product of abundance, not of corruption, since more manuscripts mechanically produce more recorded disagreements. The overwhelming majority are spelling differences, word-order slips, and obvious copying errors.
Meaningful and viableDaniel B. Wallace’s two independent tests for a variant: does it change the sense (meaningful), and does it have a serious claim to be what the author wrote (viable)? On his sorting the variants fall into four groups, and only the meaningful-and-viable group — which he estimates at less than one percent of all variants — is a live candidate for the original.
The “strict text”The most careful category in Kurt and Barbara Aland’s classification of manuscript quality, reserved for copies made with unusual discipline; P75 is its exemplar. Not every early papyrus was copied so strictly — the Alands label others, such as P66 and P45, “free.”
Initial textThe form from which the surviving tradition descends. Modern editors increasingly speak of reconstructing the “initial text” rather than the autograph itself, because for some passages — the ending of Mark is the classic example — the search for a single original wording may be genuinely indeterminate. A refinement at the margins, not a collapse at the center.
Independent comparandaCritical editions built on different foundations by different methods. Gregory Lanier’s collations found the Tyndale House Greek New Testament agreeing with the standard Nestle-Aland text in about 98.8 percent of its wording, and even the opposite-method Byzantine text agreeing with the older critical text in roughly 92 to 93 percent. Part of edition-to-edition agreement is editorial conservatism, which is why these independent comparisons carry the weight.
The argument, formally

Three lines. The whole weight rests on the two premises.

P1

A text whose manuscript tradition is early, abundant, and in broad agreement can be reconstructed to what its authors originally wrote.

P2

The New Testament has a manuscript tradition that is early, abundant, and in broad agreement.

∴ C

Therefore, the New Testament we read today faithfully transmits what its authors originally wrote.

The logic is valid — if both premises are true, the conclusion follows. So objections come in two families: attacks on P1 (the criteria for reconstruction) or P2 (whether the NT actually meets them), and attacks on the further step from the conclusion to what it is taken to show. Knowing which premise an objection targets is half the battle.

What follows — and what doesn’t

Don’t read the conclusion — reason to it

Working only from what the argument has established: what does each piece of evidence rule out, and how far does the inference reach? Reason each line out, then reveal.

easier: different scribes make different errorsEach independent line of copying introduces its own errors — but different scribes make different errors. When you lay many independent copies side by side, a slip in one is exposed by the dozens that do not share it. The original reading tends to survive somewhere in the stream, and the comparison of witnesses lets you triangulate back toward it.
The manuscripts carry hundreds of thousands of variants — so the size of that number reflects…?
the abundance of witnesses, not corruptionMore manuscripts mechanically produce more recorded disagreements; a tradition surviving in three copies would have almost no variants, while one surviving in thousands generates hundreds of thousands. a faithful line of transmission, not a lucky matchP75 is not merely old but demonstrably careful, so its closeness to Codex Vaticanus reflects a faithful line of transmission. We can name the longer ending of Mark and the Johannine Comma as later additions — so the deliberate changes show…?

no — truth is argued separately, on other groundsA defender who slides from “the text is well preserved” to “therefore Christianity is true” has committed a fallacy. What good transmission does is clear the ground, so the questions that bear on truth — how early the Gospels were written, whether they rest on eyewitness testimony, and above all whether Jesus rose from the dead — can be argued on first-century evidence.
Defending Premise 1

A text whose manuscript tradition is early, abundant, and in broad agreement can be reconstructed to what its authors originally wrote.

Precision check: the question is one of transmission, not truth. Establishing that we possess the authors’ words tells us nothing, by itself, about whether those words are true. The Iliad is splendidly preserved, and no one thinks that settles the existence of Zeus. What good textual transmission does is more modest and more foundational: it removes one specific objection—“you’re reading a late legend, rewritten beyond recognition”—so that the real arguments about the contents can proceed on first-century testimony rather than be dismissed in advance.

Abundance and antiquity make a text recoverable The core warrant

One might think that having more copies, made by more fallible scribes, would make the original harder to find. The opposite is true. Each independent line of copying introduces its own errors—but different scribes make different errors. When you lay many independent copies side by side, a slip in one is exposed by the dozens that do not share it. The original reading tends to survive somewhere in the stream, and the comparison of witnesses lets you triangulate back toward it.

Transmission branches — it is not a game of telephone Cross-checking

The right analogy is not the children’s game of telephone, where a single message is whispered down one chain and each player hears only the one prior whisper, so errors accumulate with nothing to check them. Manuscript transmission branches. Copies were carried to Egypt, Syria, Rome, and Asia Minor, translated into Latin and Coptic and Syriac, and quoted in sermons and commentaries across the Mediterranean. A corruption that entered the Egyptian line can be caught against the Latin and the Byzantine; the streams cross-check one another. A small tradition—three copies in one place—can hide a corruption in every surviving witness. A vast, geographically scattered one cannot easily bury the original in every branch at once.

The ordinary method, applied to every ancient author Same discipline

The discipline behind Premise 1 is textual criticism, and it is far older than the apologetic use to which it is sometimes put. It grew up in the Renaissance among scholars editing classical authors. None of this is special pleading invented for the Bible. It is the ordinary method applied to Homer, Plato, Tacitus, and every other ancient author. The point of Premise 1 is simply that the New Testament is subject to the same discipline, and that the discipline rewards abundance and antiquity. The science of comparing the witnesses is mature and public.

Objection — Ehrman: scribes deliberately changed the text

Ehrman argues that some changes were not innocent slips but deliberate alterations—scribes adjusting the text to sharpen its Christology, harmonize the Gospels, or settle theological disputes. In The Orthodox Corruption of Scripture (1993), he documents passages he believes were modified in the heat of second- and third-century controversy. The implication is that the transmission was not a neutral relay but an interested one: the text we have was shaped by the very debates it was later used to adjudicate.

Reply

Ehrman is right that some alterations were intentional; he has demonstrated that. Whether they were theologically motivated—as opposed to reverent smoothing or harmonizing—is his further and more contested claim, which the identifiable, catalogued nature of these changes does not by itself establish. But we can name these passages—the longer ending of Mark (16:9–20), the woman caught in adultery (John 7:53–8:11), the explicitly Trinitarian “Johannine Comma” of 1 John 5:7–8—precisely because the comparative method flagged them as later additions. The discipline is not defeated by the intentional changes; the discipline is how we know about them. Indeed, one of the commonest deliberate changes cuts against the “telephone” picture: scribes tended to harmonize—to smooth one Gospel toward the wording of its parallel—which means conforming a passage to a parallel the scribe received as already fixed rather than freely rewriting, and which is why textual critics can recover the earlier, non-harmonized reading. It is a change the method catches, telling against any notion of a text mutating unchecked; it does not, on its own, settle Ehrman’s subtler claim that some alterations were theologically interested—a scribe’s reverence for the text and a wish to adjust it could, after all, coincide. The famous cases are few, identifiable, and—crucially—none establishes a doctrine that the rest of the New Testament does not teach independently. The Johannine Comma is the most theologically loaded interpolation in the tradition, and the doctrine of the Trinity rests not an ounce on it.

Objection — Parker: the “living text” has no single original

David C. Parker, in The Living Text of the Gospels (1997), argues that for the earliest Christians the Gospel tradition was fluid and performed, not a fixed wording to be transcribed, so that the very idea of a single recoverable “original text” may be the wrong question to ask. If Parker is right, the apologist may be chasing a phantom: an “autograph” that the early church itself did not treat as a fixed target.

Reply

The honest answer is to grant the observation beneath it while refusing the inference drawn from it. It is true that for some passages—the ending of Mark is the classic example—the search for a single original wording may be genuinely indeterminate, and the contemporary discipline has grown more cautious about the very phrase “the original text.” Modern editors increasingly speak of reconstructing the “initial text” (the form from which the surviving tradition descends) rather than the autograph itself. But this is a refinement at the margins, not a collapse at the center. The fluidity Parker documents operates within narrow bounds: the substance of the narratives, the sayings, the letters of Paul, is stable across the tradition. Granting that a handful of passages resist a single resolution is a long way from granting that we cannot recover what the authors substantially wrote.

The room: Ehrman presses deliberate alteration; Parker presses the “living text.” The live debates are narrower and more technical: whether “original text,” “initial text,” or “living text” is the right editorial goal, and how much intentional theological alteration occurred and how early. These are real disagreements among serious people, but they are disagreements about the second decimal place, not about whether we have roughly the right book.
Defending Premise 2

The New Testament has a manuscript tradition that is early, abundant, and in broad agreement.

Precision check: the raw total is not itself the argument; the weight lies in the early witnesses and their wide geographic spread, not in sheer number. This is, in the end, a convergence argument—abundance, antiquity, geographic spread, cross-checking, the P75–Vaticanus stability, and the patristic citations all pointing the same way—not a single knockdown proof. And none of it speaks to whether the contents are true.

The numbers Abundance

The Greek New Testament survives today in over 5,800 catalogued manuscripts—from tiny papyrus fragments to complete codices—supplemented by an estimated 10,000 Latin manuscripts and thousands more in Syriac, Coptic, Armenian, Georgian, and other ancient versions, plus an enormous body of quotations in the early church fathers. Most classical texts survive in a few dozen to a few hundred copies, the earliest of which typically stand 700 to 1,000 years after the author. The New Testament’s witnesses are both far more numerous and far closer to the source.

Honest limit: The great majority of those Greek copies are in fact later, medieval manuscripts, so the raw total is not itself the argument.

The early reach Early · dating contested at the edges

The John Rylands fragment P52 is, by most reckonings, the oldest surviving fragment of any New Testament book—a piece of John’s Gospel copied perhaps within half a century, and on any dating within a few generations, of when John is thought to have written it. Beyond P52, substantial papyri such as P66 and P75 carry large portions of the Gospels from around AD 200, and the great codices Sinaiticus and Vaticanus give us essentially the complete New Testament by the fourth century.

Honest limit: The date of P52 is disputed—its first editor proposed roughly AD 125, while Pasquale Orsini and Willy Clarysse have argued for a wider range of about AD 125–175, and paleographic dating of a tiny fragment carries real uncertainty. One should not lean a case on a single scrap. Our manuscripts still begin after a gap of one to several generations from the autographs, and that early window—before our witnesses start—is the period we can least directly observe.

P75 and Vaticanus: stability across a century and a half The empirical check

Where the early papyrus P75 (Luke and John, dated roughly AD 175–225) overlaps with the fourth-century Codex Vaticanus, the two are remarkably close—so close that scholars concluded Vaticanus preserves a very early, carefully copied text. In other words, when we can actually compare a copy from around AD 200 with one from around AD 350, we find not wild drift but stability. That is precisely the empirical check the “telephone” worry demands, and the text passes it. A standard classification of manuscript quality, drawn up by Kurt and Barbara Aland, reserves its most careful category—the “strict text”—for copies made with unusual discipline, and P75 is its exemplar. That is what gives its agreement with Vaticanus real force: P75 is not merely old but demonstrably careful, so its closeness to a codex copied a century and a half later reflects a faithful line of transmission rather than a lucky match.

Honest limit: The honest form of the claim is narrow. Not every early papyrus was copied so strictly—the Alands label others, such as P66 and P45, “free.” P75’s date, like that of the other early papyri, rests on paleography and carries real uncertainty—some scholars would place it somewhat later—but the argument turns on how carefully it was copied, not on the exact decade. The stability we measure between P75 and Vaticanus is powerful corroboration that the text was being copied carefully; it is not a photograph of the first decades.

The reconstructed text barely moves Corroboration

When the textual scholar Gregory Lanier collated the modern critical editions against one another, the striking result was how little the reconstructed text moves even as the evidence behind it multiplies—and the most telling comparison is not between editions that share a lineage but between ones built on different foundations by different methods. The Tyndale House Greek New Testament (2017), compiled from a documentary base resting on Tregelles rather than on Westcott and Hort and weighing only witnesses from before the sixth century, agrees with the standard Nestle-Aland text in about 98.8 percent of its wording; and even the Byzantine (Majority) text, produced on the opposite editorial principle from a different pool of manuscripts, still agrees with the older critical text in roughly 92 to 93 percent. That editions reached by such contrary routes land so close together is real evidence the underlying text is recoverable, not an artifact of one school’s assumptions.

Honest limit: Part of the edition-to-edition agreement is simple editorial conservatism—later editors begin from the received critical text rather than starting over—which is exactly why the independent comparanda, the Tyndale House edition and the opposite-method Byzantine text, carry the weight rather than the raw Westcott-Hort-to-Nestle-Aland number. And none of it speaks to whether the contents are true: a stably transmitted text is one worth arguing about, not one thereby proved.

Objection — Ehrman: more variants than words in the New Testament

Ehrman’s headline statistic is arresting: there are, he reports, somewhere between 200,000 and 400,000 textual variants (scholars’ estimates differ) among the manuscripts—and famously, “there are more variations among our manuscripts than there are words in the New Testament.” The New Testament contains roughly 138,000 Greek words; the variants outnumber them. How can anyone claim to possess the original wording when the copies disagree with one another that many times?

Reply

The headline number is real, but it is a by-product of abundance, not of corruption. More manuscripts mechanically produce more recorded disagreements; a tradition surviving in three copies would have almost no variants, while one surviving in thousands generates hundreds of thousands. The decisive question is not how many variants there are but what kind they are. The overwhelming majority are spelling differences, slips of word order that Greek’s flexible grammar renders meaningless in translation, and obvious copying errors. Daniel B. Wallace, who has spent his career cataloguing the manuscripts, estimates that less than one percent of all variants are both “meaningful and viable”—that is, they both affect the sense and have a real claim to being original.

None of this is new. When John Mill published his Greek New Testament in 1707, its apparatus catalogued some 30,000 variant readings. The deist Anthony Collins seized on the figure, arguing in his Discourse of Free-Thinking (1713) that so many variants left the text of Scripture uncertain. The classicist Richard Bentley answered him the same year: the true text is not lost but dispersed among all the copies, and a reader who deliberately chose the worst reading at every point could not thereby lose a single article of faith. Ehrman himself concedes the point that matters most. In a written exchange appended to later editions of Misquoting Jesus, he agrees with his own teacher Bruce Metzger “that the essential Christian beliefs are not affected by textual variants in the manuscript tradition of the New Testament,” adding that if he and Metzger were put in a room to reconstruct the original, “there would be very few points of disagreement—maybe one or two dozen places out of many thousands.”

On the sorting Wallace proposes—two independent tests: is a reading meaningful, and is it viable—the great bulk are neither meaningful nor viable (the Greek “movable nu” and the two accepted spellings of the name “John” generate thousands of counted variants and touch no meaning at all). Only the meaningful-and-viable sliver is of any consequence. Some of its members are doctrinally weightless: at Romans 5:1 the manuscripts divide between echomen (“we have peace with God”) and echōmen (“let us have peace”)—a single vowel, on which no doctrine of justification depends either way. But it would be a cheat to pretend the whole category is that light: at Mark 1:41 the witnesses divide over whether Jesus was “moved with compassion” or “moved with anger” (the righteous indignation he openly shows in undisputed passages such as Mark 3:5), and at John 1:18 between “the only Son” and the reading that calls him “the only God.” These genuinely change how a single verse reads. Yet the choice lies between two orthodox readings, and neither overturns a teaching the rest of the New Testament does not establish on its own—the deity of Christ, to take the John 1:18 example, rests on a broad range of texts, not on how that one verse is decided. That is what the contested one percent is actually made of: real decisions for editors to weigh—some trivial, some exegetically rich—not one of which threatens an article of the faith.

Objection — The measured stability starts around AD 200 Hardest

Ehrman would say: you keep retreating to “substantially” what the authors wrote, but the early period you cannot observe is exactly where the most consequential changes would have happened, while the text was new, uncontrolled, and theologically up for grabs—so your measured stability after AD 200 is stability of an already-altered text.

Reply

The objection proves less than it needs to. It is a claim about a possibility in a period we lack direct evidence for—and the indirect evidence we do have constrains it. The second-century church fathers quote the Gospels and Paul copiously, and their citations, though often loose and from memory, show the same books in recognizably the same form as the text we reconstruct; the geographic spread happened early, leaving little time for a single controlled revision to reach every branch before it diverged; and the Christological changes Ehrman identifies were typically caught precisely because they entered the tradition after it had already branched—which is some evidence that the branching happened early enough to act as a check. A hypothetical wholesale alteration in the dark decades is conceivable, but conceivability is not evidence: the objection rests on a gap in the record, and the evidence that does bear on that gap tells against it. As an argument that we lack what the authors wrote, it does not hold.

The room: The substance of this argument is accepted by the great majority of working textual critics—and, on its central point, by Ehrman. There is broad agreement across the ideological spectrum that the New Testament is by far the best-attested text of antiquity, that the great majority of variants are trivial, and that no central Christian teaching hangs on a contested reading. Wallace supplies the viability taxonomy and the under-one-percent estimate; Orsini and Clarysse press the dating of P52; Ehrman presses the unobserved second century; Collins and Bentley (1713) disputed what Mill’s 1707 variant count showed. The live debates concern how to date particular early fragments and how to model the unobserved second-century transmission.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

This is what separates someone who knows the Manuscript Reliability Argument from someone who can defend it. Each gets its best form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: that the measured stability starts around AD 200 — the unobserved second century is where changes would have happened. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"There are hundreds of thousands of variants — more than words in the NT" Common

Strongest form

Bart Ehrman points out there are more textual variants among the manuscripts than there are words in the New Testament. If the copies disagree that much, how can you claim to have the original?

Reply

The count is inflated by abundance — more manuscripts mechanically produce more variants. But the vast majority are spelling differences, word order, and obvious slips that change nothing in translation. Daniel Wallace, who has spent his career cataloging the manuscripts, estimates that fewer than one percent of variants are both meaningful and viable. And Ehrman himself agrees with his teacher Bruce Metzger that the essential Christian beliefs are not affected by textual variants, adding that he and Metzger reconstructing the text together would differ in “maybe one or two dozen places.” The distinction matters: meaningful and viable is Wallace’s taxonomy and Wallace’s figure, not Ehrman’s — attributing it to Ehrman is the kind of thing a reader can check in a minute. What Ehrman does grant, and it is the more devastating concession, is that no essential Christian doctrine rests on a disputed text. A large, messy stream is exactly what lets us locate the original within it.

"It's a game of telephone — errors compound" Common

Strongest form

Like a whispered message passed down a line, each copy introduces drift, so by the time you reach our manuscripts the text has wandered far from the original.

Reply

Telephone fails because each person hears only the one prior whisper. Manuscript transmission is the opposite: copies branch into independent lines across regions and languages, so a corruption in the Egyptian stream is checked against the Byzantine and the Latin. We aren't reading the end of one chain — we're triangulating from many parallel chains back to their common source.

"The earliest copies are still a century or more late" Trickier

Strongest form

P52 is just a scrap. Substantial papyri (P66, P75) and full codices come generations after the autographs. That gap is enough time for serious alteration before our witnesses begin.

Reply

The gap is real, and it is the period we can least directly observe; the stability we measure between P75 and Vaticanus is powerful corroboration that the text was being copied carefully, not a photograph of the first decades. But the second-century church fathers quote the Gospels and Paul copiously, and their citations, though often loose and from memory, show the same books in recognizably the same form as the text we reconstruct; and the geographic spread happened early, leaving little time for a single controlled revision to reach every branch. A hypothetical wholesale alteration in the dark decades is conceivable, but conceivability is not evidence: the objection rests on a gap in the record, and the evidence that does bear on that gap tells against it. As an argument that we lack what the authors wrote, it does not hold.

"Scribes deliberately changed the text for theology" Trickier

Strongest form

Some scribes altered passages to sharpen Christology or settle disputes — the longer ending of Mark, the woman caught in adultery, the Johannine Comma. So the text was edited with an agenda, not merely miscopied.

Reply

Notice we can name those passages — which means the method works. The very reason you've heard of Mark 16:9–20 or the Comma is that textual criticism flagged them as later additions and modern Bibles bracket or footnote them. The intentional changes are few, identifiable, and none establishes a doctrine the rest of the New Testament does not teach independently — the Johannine Comma is the most theologically loaded interpolation in the tradition, and the doctrine of the Trinity rests not an ounce on it; the discipline catches them rather than being defeated by them.

"A reliable text isn't a true text" Hard

Strongest form

Grant that we have what the authors wrote. So what? You've shown the message was transmitted accurately, not that the message — miracles, resurrection — is true. The Iliad is well-transmitted too.

Reply

Exactly right, and that's all this argument claims. It removes one objection — "the text is a late legend, not what eyewitnesses said" — so the questions that do bear on truth can be argued on first-century evidence rather than dismissed in advance: were the authors honest and in a position to know, how early were the Gospels written, and, above all, did Jesus rise from the dead? It's the foundation that makes the other arguments worth having, not a substitute for them.

The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

The substance of this argument is accepted by the great majority of working textual critics — and, on its central point, by Bart Ehrman, the agnostic scholar whose Misquoting Jesus popularized the variant problem. There is broad agreement across the ideological spectrum that the New Testament is by far the best-attested text of antiquity, that the great majority of variants are trivial, and that no central Christian teaching hangs on a contested reading. The live debates are narrower and more technical: how to date particular early fragments (Pasquale Orsini and Willy Clarysse widen P52 to about AD 125–175); whether “original text,” “initial text,” or “living text” is the right editorial goal (David C. Parker presses the last); how much intentional theological alteration occurred and how early (Ehrman); and how to model the unobserved second-century transmission. These are real disagreements among serious people, but they are disagreements about the second decimal place, not about whether we have roughly the right book.

A fairness most defenders skip: Honest defenders concede the observations. Some alterations were intentional — Ehrman has demonstrated that — though whether they were theologically motivated is his further and more contested claim. The great majority of the Greek copies are later, medieval manuscripts, so the raw total is not itself the argument. Our manuscripts still begin after a gap of one to several generations from the autographs, the period we can least directly observe, and the P75–Vaticanus stability is not a photograph of the first decades. For some passages the original wording may be genuinely indeterminate. But the inference does not follow: this is a convergence argument — abundance, antiquity, geographic spread, cross-checking, the P75–Vaticanus stability, and the patristic citations all pointing the same way — and the evidence that does bear on the unobserved gap tells against wholesale alteration. As an argument that we lack what the authors wrote, it does not hold.

The bounded verdict: The manuscript evidence establishes, with as much confidence as historical method allows, that the New Testament has been accurately preserved — that the text we read today is, in all but a handful of marginal and well-flagged cases, what the first-century authors wrote. It is not a late, anonymous legend rewritten beyond recognition across the centuries, and on the narrower point that no essential Christian belief turns on a textual variant, even the discipline’s most prominent skeptic agrees. But it does not prove the contents of the New Testament true, nor show that the miracles happened or that the authors were honest and in a position to know. Reliable transmission is the foundation the arguments about truth stand on, not a substitute for them: we can be confident we are reading what the apostolic generation wrote, and whether what they wrote is true is the conversation that begins where this one ends.

    HS
    History student
    "Fine — the copying was careful. But the Gospels themselves were written decades after the events they describe. A source at that distance from what it reports shouldn’t be treated as history at all."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    Historians rely with confidence on Tacitus, whose Annals was written around AD 116, and on Suetonius, whose Lives of the Caesars appeared around AD 121, even though much of what they report happened decades to over a century before they wrote — a gap as large as or larger than the distance between the Gospels and the life of Jesus. So the objection does not hold as stated: a source can remain usable at such a remove, and the New Testament is simply being treated as an ordinary historical source rather than held to a uniquely severe standard. That parallel must be stated with care: it does not show that what the Gospels report is true, and temporal proximity does not guarantee the truth of what is reported. Whether the events actually happened is, for the New Testament as for Tacitus, a further question settled on other grounds.
