Full works · Gregory of Nazianzus (c. 329–c. 390)

The Five Theological Orations

Constantinople, AD 380 · complete, in the 1894 public-domain translation

These are historic witnesses, not Scripture. We present them complete, including passages that Christian traditions read differently.

Who Gregory was

Gregory of Nazianzus (c. 329–c. 390) was a bishop from Cappadocia, in what is now central Turkey. With Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nyssa he is one of the three Cappadocian fathers, who in the later fourth century refined the church’s vocabulary for the Trinity, distinguishing the one divine ousia (being) from the three hypostaseis (persons) and locating the distinction of the persons precisely in their relations of origin. Christians East and West honour him; in the East he is called simply “the Theologian,” above all on the strength of these five sermons.

Why he preached them

Gregory delivered these five orations at Constantinople in 380. He had two sets of opponents. The Eunomians taught that the Son is different from the Father in his very being, because only the Father is unbegotten. The Pneumatomachi, “Spirit-fighters,” many of whom granted the Son’s deity, denied the Spirit’s. Gregory describes the range of opinion among Christians of his day: some thought of the Spirit as “an Activity,” some as “a Creature,” some as God, and some “have been uncertain” (31.5). A year later the Council of Constantinople confessed the Spirit as “the Lord and Giver of life… who with the Father and the Son together is worshiped and glorified”—words more reserved than Gregory’s own “Is the Spirit God? Most certainly” (31.10).

What is in them

  • Oration 27: who should speak about God, and in what spirit.
  • Oration 28: the limits of what we can know of God. “It is impossible to express Him, and yet more impossible to conceive Him” (28.4).
  • Oration 29: the Son. Gregory answers the claim that a begotten Son must be a later or lesser God.
  • Oration 30: the Son, continued. The Bible passages raised against the Son’s deity, taken one by one, then the meaning of the Son’s titles.
  • Oration 31: the Holy Spirit. “What then? Is the Spirit God? Most certainly” (31.10).

What to look for

How the persons differ. Gregory fixed the vocabulary: the distinguishing character of the Father is that he is ingenerate, of the Son that he is begotten, of the Spirit that he proceeds—and these origins, he insisted, name relations, not degrees of divinity (29.16; 31.8–9). He refused, though, to explain how the Son is begotten or the Spirit proceeds: if either side tries, he says, “we shall both of us be frenzy-stricken for prying into the mystery of God” (31.8).

How Gregory reads “greater.” On “the Father is greater than I” (John 14:28), Gregory answers that “the Greater refers to origination, while the Equal belongs to the Nature” (30.7). The Father is the unbegotten source from whom the Son is begotten “without reference to time” (29.2), and being the source does not make the Father more God than the Son. Gregory grants the other reading too: that the Father is greater than the Son “considered as man” is, he says, “true indeed, but is no great thing” (30.7). Later teachers have used both readings.

God made man. In becoming man the Son did not shed or shrink his deity: “What He was He continued to be; what He was not He took to Himself” (29.19). He then sets the two natures side by side, line after line: “He was baptized as Man—but He remitted sins as God” (29.20). Gregory wrote some seventy years before the Council of Chalcedon (451). When he says Christ’s humanity “became God” (29.19) or speaks of God and man “intermingled” (30.3), he means one person who is truly God and truly man, not the humanity changed into the divine nature (he says Christ “continues to wear the Body which He assumed,” 30.14); the later council chose more guarded words, confessing the two natures “inconfusedly, unchangeably.”

Why the Spirit was revealed last. Gregory framed the slow, step-by-step revealing of the Spirit’s deity as providence rather than doubt: “The Old Testament proclaimed the Father openly, and the Son more obscurely. The New manifested the Son, and suggested the Deity of the Spirit. Now the Spirit Himself dwells among us, and supplies us with a clearer demonstration of Himself” (31.26). He then argues the Spirit’s deity from Scripture itself, in a long list of the Spirit’s titles and works (31.29–30).

How to read this edition

This is the English translation by Charles Gordon Browne and James Edward Swallow, published in 1894 in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers (second series, vol. 7). Its English is old (“thou,” “hath”), but it is a public-domain translation, and we print it as it stands.

The 1894 editors added introductions and footnotes. We have left those out: they are the editors’ work, not Gregory’s. We keep only their Bible references, which you can tap to read. A few point to books such as Sirach and Wisdom, which Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include and most Protestant Bibles do not; the tap-to-read feature does not cover those books. Where we found a reference misprinted or mistyped, we have corrected it, and we left out one we could not identify; the corrections are listed at the foot of this page.

The section numbers are the standard ones used in citing Gregory, so “Oration 29.19” means Oration 29, section 19. On the oration pages, tap a section number to link straight to it.

When Gregory speaks of our being “made God,” or asks how the Spirit could “deify” us (29.19; 30.14; 31.4; 31.28–29), he is using language common in the early church, often linked with 2 Peter 1:4 (“partakers of the divine nature”). He means sharing God’s life by grace, not becoming God by nature. Christian traditions use this language to different degrees.

Where Christian traditions read a passage differently, a short note marks it. The note names the question; it does not say which reading is right.
