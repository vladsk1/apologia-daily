Church Fathers & Creeds › On the Trinity

Full works · Hilary of Poitiers (c. 315–c. 367)

On the Trinity

Written in exile in Asia Minor, AD 356–360 · complete, in the 1899 public-domain translation

These are historic witnesses, not Scripture. We present them complete, including passages that Christian traditions read differently.

Who Hilary was

Hilary (c. 315–c. 367) was bishop of Poitiers, in Gaul (now western France). The first pages of this work tell of his search for God: he looked for the meaning of life among the philosophers and found it in the Scriptures (1.1–14). In 356, after a council of bishops at Béziers, the emperor Constantius II sent him into exile in Asia Minor; he had urged the bishops of Gaul to break communion with the bishops who led the Arian side. He returned to Poitiers about 361 and died about 367. Christians East and West honour him.

Why he wrote it

Hilary wrote these twelve books in exile, between about 356 and 360. His chief opponents were the Arians. As Hilary sums up their teaching, “the Lord Jesus Christ, created rather than born, bears the Divine Name by gift and not by right” (1.24); their own creed, which he quotes, calls the Son begotten but also “God’s perfect creature” (4.12–13), and he answers it through Books 4–6. He also answers Sabellius, who taught that the Father and the Son are one person: “Sabellius overlooked the eternity of the Son” (1.26). Hilary called the work Concerning the Faith; it has long been known as On the Trinity.

What is in it

Hilary sets out the plan himself (1.20–36).

  • Book 1: Hilary’s search for God, and the plan of the work.
  • Book 2: “the mystery of the Divine birth,” from the words of baptism, “in the Name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Ghost” (1.21).
  • Book 3: “I in the Father and the Father in Me” (John 14:11).
  • Book 4: the Arians’ creed, and the witness of the Law and the Prophets.
  • Book 5: from the Law and the Prophets, “there are not two gods, nor one isolated true God” (1.24).
  • Book 6: the Son’s true Sonship, proved “by the witness of the Father, by Christ’s own assertion, by the preaching of Apostles” and more (1.25), including Peter’s confession.
  • Book 7: from the Gospels, the Son is true God; against the Arians and Sabellius together.
  • Book 8: the unity of God: the Son’s birth “involves no duality in the Godhead” (1.28).
  • Book 9: the sayings raised against the Son’s deity, such as “None is good save One, even God” and “The Father is greater than I.”
  • Book 10: the Passion: the sorrow in Gethsemane and the cry from the cross.
  • Book 11: “My God and your God” (John 20:17), and the Son’s subjection to the Father (1 Corinthians 15:28).
  • Book 12: “The Lord created Me” (Proverbs 8:22); the Holy Spirit; and Hilary’s closing prayer.

What to look for

Born, not made. Hilary’s central case is that the Son is truly born of the Father, and so is God as the Father is God, not a creature. “He therefore, the Unbegotten, before time was begat a Son from Himself; not from any pre-existent matter, for all things are through the Son; not from nothing, for the Son is from the Father’s self” (3.3). This birth is not an event in time: “So He was born and yet has always existed” (12.31).

One in nature, not only in will. The Arians granted that the Father and the Son are one in will. Hilary answers that they are one in nature (8.17), and part of his answer rests on what believers receive in the Lord’s Supper (8.13–17).

How Hilary reads “greater.” On “The Father is greater than I” (John 14:28), Hilary answers that the Father is greater as the one of whom the Son is eternally born (“By the birth of the Son the Father is constituted greater”), and as the one who gives glory to the manhood the Son took, and that this does not make the Son less: “The Father therefore is greater, because He is Father: but the Son, because He is Son, is not less” (9.56). Being the source does not make the Father more God than the Son. Hilary also ties the saying to the Son’s humbling of himself in the Incarnation (9.51).

Christ’s sufferings. In Book 10 Hilary defends the deity of the Son against arguments drawn from his sorrow and his death. He holds firmly that Christ’s body was real and that he suffered (10.41, 10.47). But in the same book, guarding Christ’s deity, he says something the church has not followed: that Christ “felt the force of suffering, but without its pain” (10.23). The church confesses that Christ, truly man, felt the pain of his sufferings in his human nature. Readers disagree over how far Hilary meant to go; a note on that passage explains.

The Holy Spirit. Hilary is reserved in how he describes the Spirit, but firm on one point: the Spirit is not a creature. He calls him “that Divine Spirit” (1.36), the power of whose nature “subsists eternally” (12.55). Beyond that he will not go past what Scripture gives him: “I will not trespass beyond that which human intellect can know about Thy Holy Spirit, but simply declare that He is Thy Spirit” (12.56).

A book that ends in prayer. Hilary prays at the start (1.37–38) and closes the work with a long prayer to the Father: “Keep, I pray Thee, this my pious faith undefiled” (12.57).

How to read this edition

This is the English translation published in 1899 in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers (second series, vol. 9): Books 1–7 by E. W. Watson, Books 8 and 12 by E. N. Bennett, and Books 9–11 by S. C. Gayford. Its English is old (“thou,” “hath”), but it is a public-domain translation, and we print it as it stands.

The 1899 editors added introductions and footnotes. We have left those out: they are the editors’ work, not Hilary’s. We keep only their Bible references, which you can tap to read. Hilary’s Bible was a Latin one, translated from the Greek Old Testament, so his quotations sometimes read differently from a modern Bible, and it numbered most of the Psalms one lower than English Bibles do; where the editors gave that number (and, once, a Latin verse number) we have changed it to the English one. A few references point to books such as Baruch and Wisdom, which Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include and most Protestant Bibles do not; the tap-to-read feature does not cover those books. Where we found a reference wrong, we have corrected it; the corrections are listed at the foot of this page.

The section numbers are the standard ones used in citing Hilary, so “Book 10.23” means Book 10, section 23. On the book pages, tap a section number to link straight to it.

When Hilary speaks of God “drawing us upward to partake of His own Divine nature” (1.13), he is using language common in the early church, often linked with 2 Peter 1:4 (“partakers of the divine nature”). He means sharing God’s life by grace, not becoming God by nature. Christian traditions use this language to different degrees.

At the main places where Christian traditions read a passage differently, a short note marks it; the same questions come up elsewhere in the work. The note names the question; it does not say which reading is right. Where Hilary falls short of what later councils taught, a note says so plainly.
