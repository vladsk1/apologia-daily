Full works · Athanasius of Alexandria (c. 296–373)

On the Incarnation of the Word

Written by Athanasius of Alexandria, in Egypt, AD c. 318 (date disputed) · complete, in the 1892 public-domain translation

These are historic witnesses, not Scripture. We present them complete, including passages that Christian traditions read differently.

Who Athanasius was

Athanasius (c. 296–373) was bishop of Alexandria, in Egypt, from 328 until his death. As a young deacon he was at the Council of Nicaea in 325 with his bishop, Alexander, when the council confessed the Son “of one substance” with the Father. For nearly fifty years he defended that faith against Arius and his followers, and he was driven from his city five times. His three Discourses Against the Arians are also in this library. Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant Christians all honour him as a defender of the faith that the Son is fully God.

Why he wrote it

The book is the second of a pair. In the first, Against the Gentiles (not hosted here), Athanasius answered the worship of idols, and he opens this one by looking back on it: “Whereas in what precedes we have drawn out—choosing a few points from among many—a sufficient account of the error of the heathen concerning idols”, and “noted somewhat also of the divinity of the Word of the Father, and of His universal Providence and power” (1.1). Now he will “set forth also what relates to the Word’s becoming Man, and to His divine Appearing amongst us, which Jews traduce and Greeks laugh to scorn, but we worship”. He writes to Macarius, “true lover of Christ”, so that “all the more for the seeming low estate of the Word, your piety toward Him may be increased and multiplied” (1.1).

When it was written is not settled. The 1892 editor placed the pair before 319. Later scholars have argued for later dates, as late as his first exile (335–337); others keep the early date. The book itself does not mention Arius or his teaching; scholars weigh that silence differently.

What is in it

  • Creation and the fall (1–5): God made all things out of nothing through his Word, and made man in his image; turning from God, man began to fall back toward the nothing he came from.
  • What was God to do? (6–10): God’s goodness would not let his work perish, and his word that the sinner would die could not be broken; so the Word took a body and gave it to death for all.
  • The knowledge of God restored (11–19): the Word, the Image of the Father, came to renew the image in man and to make himself known by the works he did in the body.
  • His death and resurrection (20–32): why he died, why in public and on a cross, and why he rose on the third day; and the evidence that he is alive.
  • Answering the Jews (33–40): the prophecies of his birth and death, and of the time of his coming.
  • Answering the Greeks (41–55): why it is not absurd for the Word to take a body, and what he was doing in the world of Athanasius’s day.
  • Conclusion (56–57): read the Scriptures, look for his second coming, and live a life fit to understand them.

The whole work is on one page.

What to look for

The Word who made the world remakes it. Athanasius begins with creation, “that so it may be duly perceived that the renewal of creation has been the work of the self-same Word that made it at the beginning” (1.4). God “has made all things out of nothing by His own Word, Jesus Christ our Lord”, and made men “after His own image” (3.3). When they turned from him, they began to slide back toward nothing: “just as they have had their being out of nothing, so also, as might be expected, they might look for corruption into nothing in the course of time” (4.4).

What was God to do? God’s goodness would not let his work be lost: “it were not worthy of God’s goodness that the things He had made should waste away” (6.5). Yet God had said that sinners would die, and “God would not be true, if, when He had said we should die, man died not” (6.3). Repentance would not do: it “would, firstly, fail to guard the just claim of God”; “nor, secondly, does repentance call men back from what is their nature—it merely stays them from acts of sin” (7.3). Only the Word could: “He alone of natural fitness was both able to recreate everything, and worthy to suffer on behalf of all and to be ambassador for all with the Father” (7.5). So “He takes unto Himself a body, and that of no different sort from ours” (8.2), and “He gave it over to death in the stead of all, and offered it to the Father” (8.4).

The image restored. His best-known picture is a portrait spoiled by stains: “he whose likeness it is must needs come once more to enable the portrait to be renewed on the same wood”; so “the most holy Son of the Father, being the Image of the Father, came to our region to renew man once made in His likeness” (14.1–2). And because men had stopped looking up, he came where they were looking, “so that they who would not know Him from His Providence and rule over all things, may even from the works done by His actual body know the Word of God which is in the body, and through Him the Father” (14.8).

Truly God, and truly made man. Athanasius insists that the Word was not shut up in his body: “even while present in a human body and Himself quickening it, He was, without inconsistency, quickening the universe as well” (17.2); “He was at once walking as man, and as the Word was quickening all things, and as the Son was dwelling with His Father” (17.5). Yet the body was truly his: Scripture says he ate, was born and suffered “because the actual body which ate, was born, and suffered, belonged to none other but to the Lord”, and “having become man, it was proper for these things to be predicated of Him as man, to shew Him to have a body in truth, and not in seeming” (18.1). He is in himself “impassible and incorruptible and very Word and God” (54.3), and yet the body that suffered was his own.

Death undone, in public, on a cross. In his death “two marvels came to pass at once, that the death of all was accomplished in the Lord’s body, and that death and corruption were wholly done away by reason of the Word that was united with it” (20.5). He died in public because “it would be no resurrection did not death precede”, and had his death been in secret, “His Resurrection too had been hidden and without evidence” (23.1). He rose on the third day: “in order that the body might be proved to be dead, the Word tarried yet one intermediate day, and on the third shewed it incorruptible to all” (26.3).

Living evidence. Athanasius points to what his readers could see for themselves: Christians who once feared death now “choose rather to die than to deny their faith in Christ” (27.2), and the risen Christ is still at work, for “this work is not that of one dead, but of one that lives—and especially of God” (30.6). To the doubter he says: “let him who is incredulous about the victory over death receive the faith of Christ, and pass over to His teaching, and he shall see the weakness of death, and the triumph over it” (28.5).

How to read this edition

This is Archibald Robertson’s English translation, published in 1892 in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers (second series, vol. 4). Its English is old (“thou,” “hath,” “shew”), but it is a public-domain translation, and we print it as it stands. Words in square brackets are the translator’s.

The 1892 volume prints an introduction, a summary heading over every section but one, and footnotes, by Robertson. We have left those out: they are not Athanasius’s words. We keep only the Bible references, which you can tap to read. The small numbers inside each section are the 1892 printing’s, so “54.3” means section 54, part 3; we leave them as printed, including a few slips (in section 5 they run 2, 3, 4, 8, and section 10 prints a 1 after its first sentence, where the others start at 2). Tap a section number to link straight to it.

Athanasius read the Old Testament in Greek, so his quotations sometimes read differently from a modern Bible (“Ye shall see your Life hanging before your eyes, and shall not believe”, Deuteronomy 28:66, at 35.2). Two references point to the book of Wisdom, which Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include and most Protestant Bibles do not; Athanasius quotes it as “Wisdom” (4.6, 5.2), and Christian traditions differ on whether it belongs to the Old Testament. The tap-to-read feature does not cover that book. He also quotes “the most edifying book of the Shepherd” (3.1), an early Christian writing that is not in the Bible. Where we found a reference wrong, we have corrected it; the correction is listed at the foot of this page.

When Athanasius says that the Word “was made man that we might be made God” (54.3), he is using language common in the early church, often linked with 2 Peter 1:4 (“partakers of the divine nature”). He means sharing God’s life by grace, not becoming God by nature: in this book men were “destined, by the grace following from partaking of the Word, to have escaped their natural state” (5.1), and in his Discourses Against the Arians the Son “alone is very God from the very God” (1.39), while we are “made sons through Him by adoption and grace”, “called gods, not as the True God or His Word, but as has pleased God who has given us that grace” (3.19). Christian traditions use this language to different degrees.

In this book Athanasius speaks of Christ’s human nature mostly as his “body”, which the Word uses as his “instrument” (8.3, 43.4); he does not discuss Christ’s human soul. The Council of Chalcedon (451) later said in so many words that Christ’s humanity is complete: it confessed him “truly God and truly man, of a reasonable soul and body”, one and the same Christ. The book is about the Son; it names the Holy Spirit in its closing words of praise (57.3).

Athanasius writes as a controversialist of his time. Answering the Jews (33–40), he argues that the end of Jerusalem’s kings, prophets and Temple shows that the Christ has come. Christian traditions differ on the place of the Jewish people in God’s purposes after the coming of Christ (Romans 9–11). Athanasius says that “the Jews are trifling” (40.1) and asks “why are they so irreligious and so perverse” (40.4). He also speaks of “the counsels of the Jews against Him and the indignities offered Him at their hands” (34.1). He is no gentler with the Greeks, “whose only serious pursuit is jesting” (48.7). We print these words because they are part of the text. We do not speak that way of Jewish people or of those we disagree with, and we do not hold the Jewish people guilty, as a people, for the death of Christ. Athanasius’s account of the oracles falling silent and of whole peoples laying down their arms (46–52) describes the world as he saw it in his own day.

Copy-check (2026-11-22). The hosted text is CCEL’s transcription of the 1892 volume with the editor’s introduction, his summary heading over each section and the footnote markers removed (section 5 has no heading). It was compared word by word, by script, with the OCR of four archive.org scans of the 1892 volume (niceneandpostnic04unknuoft, selectlibraryofn04schauoft, selectlibraryofn04scha and cu31924096463454); because the scans run the editor’s footnotes into the text, each run of sixty words was located in the scan separately. 99.0%, 98.4%, 98.9% and 98.8% of the transcription’s 26,014 words match those scans directly; a test of every run of six words found four runs of fourteen or more words not confirmed in two scans: three at footnote numbers and page breaks, and one at a changed word (46). Every difference that two or more scans agreed on (56), and every short run of words that the scans print and the transcription lacks, or the reverse, was tested in context in the scans and read by hand; every occurrence of the spellings a transcription may modernise (‘shew’, ‘spake’, ‘nought’ and others) was checked in four scans. The quotation marks were checked to be paired in every section (the 1892 printing uses double marks; a sample was compared with the scans); other punctuation was not compared. The translator’s words in square brackets (‘[He teaches them]’) are kept. Nine corrections in Athanasius’s text, each confirmed in all four scans: four words the transcription changed (it reads ‘God’ where the printing has the prophet ‘Gad’, 39; ‘erected’ for ‘effected’, 46; ‘bold’ for ‘hold’, 48; and ‘impossible’ for ‘impassible’, 54); and five older spellings that the 1892 printing has and the transcription modernised (‘nought’ three times, ‘intreated’ and ‘shew’). No word was found dropped. One Bible reference corrected (6, the editor’s ‘Gen. ii. 15’, which does not fit the words); every reference was checked by script against the verse it points to, and the 29 that shared no words with the words before them were read by hand; the 2 that point to Wisdom were not checked this way. Eight Bible references in six of the editor’s footnotes, which the script first missed (they abbreviate Luke, John and Mark as ‘Luc.’, ‘Joh.’ and ‘Marc.’, or open with ‘See’), were recovered. Stray spaces left where footnote markers were removed have been closed up. The comparison record and the scripts are in docs/audits/fathers-incarnation-copycheck-2026-11-22.md and tools/fathers/.
