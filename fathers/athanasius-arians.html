Full works · Athanasius of Alexandria (c. 296–373)

Against the Arians

Written by the bishop of Alexandria, in Egypt, AD date disputed (the 1892 editor: 356–360) · complete, in the 1892 public-domain translation

These are historic witnesses, not Scripture. We present them complete, including passages that Christian traditions read differently.

Who Athanasius was

Athanasius (c. 296–373) was bishop of Alexandria, in Egypt, from 328 until his death. As a young deacon he was at the Council of Nicaea in 325 with his bishop, Alexander, when the council confessed the Son “of one substance” with the Father. For nearly fifty years he defended that faith against Arius and his followers, and he was driven from his city five times. His short book On the Incarnation is also in our Sources. Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant Christians all honour him as a defender of the faith that the Son is fully God. The “Athanasian Creed” is named after him, but it was written later, in the Latin West.

Why he wrote it

Arius, a priest of Alexandria, taught that the Son of God is a creature: that “God was not always a Father”, that “the Son was not always”, and that he was made “out of nothing”. Athanasius quotes these sayings from Arius’s poem, the Thalia (1.5, 1.9). He writes, he says, “to unrip ‘the folds of its breast-plate,’ and to shew the ill savour of its folly”, so that those it has deceived “may repent” (1.1). Then he answers, one by one, the Bible texts the Arians used.

When the Discourses were written is not settled. The 1892 editor placed them during Athanasius’s third exile, between 356 and 360, while noting that there is no conclusive evidence and that they seem to have been issued at intervals; later scholars have argued for earlier dates. The 1892 volume also prints a fourth Discourse. Its editor notes that, unlike the first three, it is not quoted in antiquity as part of the work, and that its place beside them is uncertain. We host the three Discourses only.

What is in it

  • Discourse I: Arius’s Thalia and why the question matters (1–10); the Son is eternal, a true Son and not a creature (11–29); the word “unoriginate” (30–34); the Son does not change (35–36); and the first of the texts the Arians used: “God hath highly exalted Him” (Philippians 2:9), “Wherefore God, even Thy God, hath anointed Thee” (Psalm 45:7) and “being made so much better than the Angels” (Hebrews 1:4) (37–64).
  • Discourse II: “faithful to Him that made Him” (Hebrews 3:2) and “He hath made Him Lord and Christ” (Acts 2:36) (1–18); why the Son is not a creature (19–43); and “The Lord created me a beginning of His ways” (Proverbs 8:22) and the verses after it (44–82).
  • Discourse III: “I in the Father and the Father in Me” and the other texts from John on the Son and the Father (1–25); the texts that speak of the Son made man, among them his not knowing the day (Mark 13:32), his growing in wisdom (Luke 2:52) and his fear before his death (26–58); and whether the Son was begotten by the Father’s will (59–67).

What to look for

True Son, true God. Athanasius sets out the faith in a few lines: the Son is “not a creature or work, but an offspring proper to the Father’s essence. Wherefore He is very God, existing one in essence with the very Father”; “He ever was and is and never was not. For the Father being everlasting, His Word and His Wisdom must be everlasting” (1.9).

Father and Son: two, but “the nature is one”. Father and Son are not one person under two names, and not one thing in two parts: “They are two, because the Father is Father and is not also Son, and the Son is Son and not also Father; but the nature is one”, and “all that is the Father’s, is the Son’s” (3.4). He returns often to an image from Scripture, which calls the Son “the radiance of His glory” (Hebrews 1:3): the light and its radiance. A light is never without its radiance: “when did man see light without the brightness of its radiance”? (1.12). So the Son always was. And the radiance is not a lesser light: “the radiance also is light, not second to the sun, nor a different light”, and “no one would say that they are two lights, but sun and radiance two, yet one the light” (3.4). Like every comparison drawn from created things, it helps the reader think; it is not a picture of God. The one God is Father, Son and Holy Spirit: we are baptized “into the Name of Father, Son, and Holy Ghost” (1.34), and “there is an eternal and one Godhead in a Triad, and there is one Glory of the Holy Triad” (1.18).

A rule for reading Scripture about the Son. Scripture, he says, “contains a double account of the Saviour; that He was ever God, and is the Son, being the Father’s Word and Radiance and Wisdom; and that afterwards for us He took flesh of a Virgin, Mary Bearer of God, and was made man” (3.29). The texts that speak of the Son as made, as anointed, or as not knowing the day are read by the second half of that rule. Of Mark 13:32 he writes that the Son said it “as man by reason of the flesh. For this as before is not the Word’s deficiency, but of that human nature whose property it is to be ignorant” (3.43).

Fully God and fully man. Only one who is God can join us to God, and only true flesh could save ours: “as we had not been delivered from sin and the curse, unless it had been by nature human flesh, which the Word put on … so also the man had not been deified, unless the Word who became flesh had been by nature from the Father and true and proper to Him” (on “deified”, see “How to read this edition” below; 2.70). The Word did not visit a man, as he came to the prophets: “He became man, and did not come into man” (3.30).

“Greater.” On “My Father is greater than I” (John 14:28), he notes that the Son says “greater”, not “better”, “not indeed in greatness, nor in time, but because of His generation from the Father Himself”; and in saying “greater” the Son “again shews that He is proper to His essence” (1.58). The Father is the source from whom the Son is eternally begotten: a relation of origin, never a difference of greatness, of time or of essence.

How to read this edition

This is John Henry Newman’s English translation, revised by Archibald Robertson, published in 1892 in the Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers (second series, vol. 4). Its English is old (“thou,” “hath,” “shew”), but it is a public-domain translation, and we print it as it stands. Words in square brackets are the translator’s.

The 1892 volume prints an introduction, chapter headings and long footnotes, by Newman and the 1892 editors. We have left those out: they are not Athanasius’s words. We keep only their Bible references, which you can tap to read. Athanasius read the Old Testament in Greek, so his quotations sometimes read differently from a modern Bible; where the editors gave the Greek or Hebrew numbering of a Psalm, we have changed it to the English one. A few references point to Baruch, Wisdom and Sirach, which Catholic and Orthodox Bibles include and most Protestant Bibles do not. Athanasius quotes from them (“in the book of Wisdom it is written”, 2.45); Christian traditions differ on whether they belong to the Old Testament. The tap-to-read feature does not cover those books. Where we found a reference wrong, we have corrected it; the corrections are listed at the foot of this page.

The sections are numbered through each Discourse, as the 1892 edition numbers them, so “2.22” means Discourse II, section 22. On the Discourse pages, tap a section number to link straight to it.

When Athanasius says that the Word became man “to deify us” (1.39), he is using language common in the early church, often linked with 2 Peter 1:4 (“partakers of the divine nature”). He means sharing God’s life by grace, not becoming God by nature: the Son “alone is very God from the very God”, while we are “made sons through Him by adoption and grace” (1.39, 3.19). Christian traditions use this language to different degrees.

When he says that Christ did not know the day “as man” (“that human nature whose property it is to be ignorant”, 3.43), or that he wept and was troubled, or that his “flesh” feared, he means Christ’s human nature; he explains that it is “the custom of Scripture to call man by the name of ‘flesh’” (3.30). The Council of Chalcedon (451) later said in so many words that Christ’s humanity is complete: it confessed him “truly God and truly man, of a reasonable soul and body”, one and the same Christ.

Athanasius writes as a controversialist of his time. He calls his opponents “Ario-maniacs” (1.4) and says the heresy “affects to array herself in Scripture language, like her father the devil” (1.1). He also speaks harshly of the Jews: he uses “Judaic” as a term of abuse for the Arians, and reads the fall of Jerusalem as a penalty: “The Jews then have the penal award of their denial” (2.42). We print these words because they are part of the text. We do not speak that way of Jewish people or of those we disagree with, and we do not hold the Jewish people guilty, as a people, for the death of Christ.

At the main places where Christian traditions read a passage differently, a short note marks it; the same questions come up elsewhere in the book. The note names the question; it does not say which reading is right.
