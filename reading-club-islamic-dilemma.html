The Qur’an speaks warmly of the Torah (Tawrat) and the Gospel (Injil) — calling them revelation from God, “guidance and light,” and telling Jews and Christians to follow them. Yet it also contradicts them on central points, from the crucifixion of Jesus to the very fatherhood of God. Christopher Frost works through the Qur’an itself — not from Christian premises — to lay out that tension and ask what it means. This club walks the book part by part, then helps you talk it over, thoughtfully and kindly.

About this study

The sessions follow the book's own structure — the introduction, the history, all four parts, and the conclusion, in order. Christopher Frost has seen this study and is happy for it to be published; it appears here with his permission.

The section titles are our own working headings, and the summaries and discussion questions are ours too. Nothing on this page reproduces the book's text. Every summary was written in our own words from a complete, cover-to-cover reading.

We deliberately keep the tone gentle and the verdicts modest — the case is framed as a genuine tension to raise respectfully rather than as a knockout blow, the Muslim view is stated at its strongest, and the Jesus of the Qur’an is represented accurately: recognisably the same person and yet, on the questions that matter most, profoundly different — and affirming a prophet while denying his deity is not, in the end, to honour him (John 5:23). That is our house standard for this kind of material, and it is ours rather than the book's.

The extra Apologia Daily links are clearly labelled as our own optional resources — not part of the book, and not the author's endorsement of them. A reader can complete the whole club without ever opening one.

Finish the study · Mastery check

Can you make the case — kindly?

One capstone for the whole book — eight prompts that walk through the argument, in two modes. Flashcards: read a prompt, answer it in your head (or out loud to the group), then tap to reveal a model answer — the book’s argument, put in our own words and in our own house tone. Self-rate quiz: take the same eight prompts one at a time, rate how well you answered each, and get an honest score at the end. If you can walk through all eight — graciously — you are ready to have the conversation, including the reply your friend is most likely to give.

01 · The argument
What is the “Islamic Dilemma”?
Tap to reveal →
The Qur’an affirms the Torah and Gospel as true, God-given revelation — yet it contradicts them on central points. So, the book argues, the same divine author cannot stand behind both, which puts real pressure on the Qur’an’s own claim. It’s built from the Qur’an itself, and this study offers it as a probability, not a proof.
02 · The fork
Why is it a “dilemma”?
Tap to reveal →
A Muslim faces two hard options. Affirm that the Qur’an endorses the Scriptures in the hands of its seventh-century readers — and face the contradictions. Or deny it — and contradict the Qur’an’s own repeated words. There is also a serious middle path, and it should be put at full strength: the Qur’an affirms the authentic prior revelation while standing as muhaymin — criterion and guardian — over a present text that contains that revelation in a partially distorted form. That is a real distinction, it genuinely blunts the strict, all-or-nothing version of the dilemma, and a Christian should grant as much. What it relocates rather than removes is Q 10:94, where the Qur’an sends Muhammad himself, in his own hour of doubt, to those who had been reading the Scripture before him — and one does not steady one’s doubts against a witness one regards as unreliable.
03 · The affirmation
How does the Qur’an speak of the Torah and Gospel?
Tap to reveal →
As genuine revelation from God. It calls itself their “confirmer” (musaddiq), names them “guidance and light,” commands the People of the Book to follow them (Q 5:43–47), and makes belief in the Books God sent down before a required article of faith (2:285; 4:136). Not one stray verse — a sustained theme across dozens.
04 · Which Bible?
Which Torah and Gospel does the Qur’an mean?
Tap to reveal →
The book argues it means the Scriptures Jews and Christians actually held in the seventh century — substantially the same as ours. The Qur’an tells people to consult their own Torah (Q 3:93), addresses the real Jews and Christians of the day, and the manuscripts (down to the Ethiopian Gospels whose radiocarbon dating brackets Muhammad’s lifetime) show the text hadn’t changed enough to matter.
05 · The corruption reply
“But the Bible was corrupted” — does that hold?
Tap to reveal →
Frost argues these verses chiefly describe twisting a text’s meaning or concealing it — rather than rewriting the words (and you can only conceal a Scripture you actually have). And a universal, coordinated corruption, across rival communities in many languages with no surviving trace, is very hard to credit historically. He adds a careful point: the Qur’an’s commands are addressed to the Scripture seventh-century readers actually held — so a corruption that rescues the Muslim case has to have come after the Qur’an, and the manuscripts close that window. Our own read of the manuscript record is the same: the text did not change.
06 · The divergences
Where do the Qur’an and the Bible actually differ?
Tap to reveal →
On the things that matter most: the Qur’an is traditionally read as denying Jesus’ crucifixion (Q 4:157), denies he is the divine Son, and rejects God as “Father” — while the Gospels affirm all three. (Our own note, not the book’s: Islam still affirms much about Jesus — virgin-born, Messiah, prophet — but it is a different portrait; the divergence over his divine identity is real, not manufactured. And the Gospels rest everything on what followed the cross — the bodily resurrection, without which, Paul says, our faith is futile, 1 Corinthians 15:17.)
07 · The tone
How should a Christian actually use this?
Tap to reveal →
As an honest question, not a weapon (1 Peter 3:15). State the Muslim view at its strongest and most charitable, name what Muslims genuinely affirm about Jesus, keep the verdict modest (“this is a real tension”), and invite a friend to look again — not to win a debate.
08 · The 60-second version
The whole thing, for a friend over coffee?
Tap to reveal →
The Qur’an calls the Torah and Gospel true, God-given Scripture and tells people to follow them — yet it contradicts them on Jesus’ cross, his divine identity, and God as Father. Since the Qur’an also affirmed those very Scriptures, it’s hard to see the same God standing behind both books. It’s a genuine tension, argued from the Qur’an itself — worth raising with a Muslim friend as a sincere question, and worth knowing both books well enough to raise it well.
