The Resurrection Predictions

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and stay clear and calm in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

According to the Gospels, Jesus did not merely happen to rise — he spoke beforehand, in veiled terms, of a coming death and a vindication beyond it. Mark records three "passion predictions" in which Jesus foretells his death and rising after three days. John has him say, "Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up" (John 2:19). The same saying appears, transformed and on hostile lips, in Mark: false witnesses at the trial report it (Mark 14:58), and passersby at the cross mock him with it (Mark 15:29).

The predictions also bear a mark of authenticity: embarrassment. The same sources show the disciples failing to understand the predictions, arguing about status, fleeing at the arrest, and not expecting the resurrection when it came. A church freely inventing both the predictions and its own founders' words would be unlikely to paint that unflattering portrait. What the argument can carry is a reasonable historical case that Jesus anticipated a violent death in Jerusalem and trusted God to vindicate him beyond it, and that he expressed this in veiled, embarrassing, and multiply attested ways that resist easy explanation as pure invention.

Honest scope: a skeptic can grant that Jesus predicted his rising without granting that it occurred, and can hold that the detailed predictions were sharpened in the telling. This argument does not by itself prove the resurrection; that case rests on the early creed of 1 Corinthians 15:3-7, the empty tomb, and the appearances. This is a supporting strand, not a standalone proof.

The argument, formally

He saw his death coming — and trusted God to vindicate him.

P1

Before the end Jesus spoke of a coming death and a vindication beyond it — the three Markan passion predictions, the riddling sign of Jonah, and the temple saying, which resurfaces on hostile lips at his trial (Mark 14:58).

P2

The predictions are authenticated by embarrassment: the disciples are shown failing to understand, arguing about greatness, fleeing, and not expecting the resurrection — a portrait no inventing church would paint of its founders around sayings it was inventing.

∴ C

Therefore Jesus went to Jerusalem expecting to die and foretold, in veiled terms, that God would vindicate him — a supporting strand, not a standalone proof; the resurrection itself rests on the 1 Corinthians 15:3–7 creed, the empty tomb and the appearances.

Defending Premise 1

Before the end Jesus spoke of a coming death and a vindication beyond it — the three Markan passion predictions, the riddling sign of Jonah, and the temple saying, which resurfaces on hostile lips at his trial (Mark 14:58).

Precision check: the argument is not that these texts are clear prophecies of Easter. It is more modest: that Jesus, before the end, spoke of a coming death and a vindication beyond it, and that this anticipation — if historical — is hard to square with the picture of a man simply swept up by events he did not see coming. The label “contested” belongs on any claim that Jesus foretold the resurrection in specific terms.

The passion-and-resurrection predictions Core material

In Mark’s Gospel, Jesus three times foretells his suffering, death, and rising — Mark 8:31, 9:31, and 10:33–34, each with Synoptic parallels in Matthew and Luke. The third is the most detailed, naming condemnation, handover to the Gentiles, mockery, flogging, and death.

The “sign of Jonah” Supporting · cryptic

Pressed for a miraculous proof, Jesus refuses and offers only a cipher: “no sign will be given... except the sign of the prophet Jonah. For just as Jonah was three days and three nights in the belly of the great fish, so will the Son of Man be three days and three nights in the heart of the earth” (Matthew 12:39–40). Luke’s version is barer still, omitting the three-days detail and comparing Jonah himself, as a sign to Nineveh, to the Son of Man (Luke 11:29–30).

The temple saying Strong · multiply attested

In John, Jesus says, “Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up,” which the narrator glosses as referring to “the temple of his body” (John 2:19–21). The same saying appears, transformed and on hostile lips, in Mark: false witnesses at the trial report, “I will destroy this temple that is made with hands, and in three days I will build another, not made with hands” (Mark 14:58), and passersby at the cross mock him with it (Mark 15:29).

It appears independently in Mark and John, two streams many scholars treat as largely separate. More telling, it surfaces not as a triumphant Christian slogan but as an accusation used against Jesus at his trial and as a taunt at his execution.

Against the grain: the temple-saying was awkward enough that Mark reports it via false witnesses and John must explain it (‘he spoke of the temple of his body’) — churches do not fabricate sayings they then have to manage.

Objection — “After three days vs on the third day — the predictions do not even agree”

Mark says after three days; Matthew says on the third day; he died Friday and rose Sunday — barely a day and a half. And Matthew 12:40 is starker still: ‘three days and three nights in the heart of the earth.’ The math fails the prophecy.

Reply

Jewish idiom counted part of a day as the whole (compare Esther 4:16 with 5:1): Friday, Saturday and Sunday could make ‘three days,’ and ‘after three days’ / ‘on the third day’ could name one interval. Matthew keeps both phrases pages apart without anxiety. And the ‘three days and three nights’ wrinkle is at least as consistent with an inherited saying the writers felt bound to preserve as with free invention, since a fabricator working only from hindsight had no need to leave the wrinkle in.

The room: E. P. Sanders, no friend of easy harmonization, treated Jesus’ conflict over the temple as among the most secure things we can know about him, and judged a saying about its destruction probably authentic; his verdict on the temple saying is widely shared. Dale Allison — a cautious, often skeptical historian — judges that Jesus before his crucifixion likely foresaw his own death and looked for his vindication by God. N. T. Wright argues that Jesus went to Jerusalem expecting both death and divine vindication. Bultmann long ago classed the detailed passion predictions as vaticinia ex eventu.
Defending Premise 2

The predictions are authenticated by embarrassment: the disciples are shown failing to understand, arguing about greatness, fleeing, and not expecting the resurrection — a portrait no inventing church would paint of its founders around sayings it was inventing.

Precision check: the criterion of embarrassment says material the early church would have found awkward, and so had no motive to invent, has a stronger claim to be historical (John P. Meier popularized the tool). It supports a defensible historical core — not verbatim foreknowledge.

The disciples look terrible The core warrant

After each prediction in Mark, the disciples conspicuously fail. Peter rebukes Jesus and is called “Satan” (Mark 8:32–33); the Twelve argue about who is greatest (9:34); James and John angle for thrones (10:35–37). And at the end they abandon him and flee. As Bart Ehrman observes, Mark has deliberately arranged things so that the disciples never do understand what Jesus is talking about. If a later community were fabricating prophecies to glorify its founder, the natural move would be to show the disciples grasping and trusting them. Instead the tradition preserves their dullness and desertion — an unflattering portrait of the very men who became the church’s pillars.

The temple saying as accusation Strong

A community inventing a prediction to prove its founder divine would not naturally cast it as the charge that helped get him killed. The embarrassment runs the right direction for authenticity.

The sign of Jonah is cryptic, not clean Suggestive

If you were manufacturing a proof-text after Easter, you would write a clear one. “On the third day I will rise” is clear. “The sign of Jonah” is a riddle that the Gospel writers themselves had to interpret — and interpreted differently, Matthew tying it to three days in the earth, Luke leaving it as Jonah-the-preacher to Nineveh. That very untidiness is a mark of antiquity: the saying looks like something handed down and puzzled over, not engineered.

Objection — Prophecy after the event (vaticinium ex eventu) Hardest

The Gospels were written decades after the events they describe, by believers who already knew how the story ended. Mark, the earliest, is dated by most scholars to around AD 70; John, the latest, to the 90s. On this reading, the “predictions” are not Jesus reading the future but the evangelists reading the past — composing or, more plausibly, sharpening sayings so that Jesus’ death and resurrection appear foreordained. The third passion prediction in particular reads like a checklist of the passion narrative that follows it: condemnation, Gentiles, mockery, spitting, flogging. For the detailed predictions, this is not a fringe view; it is a widely held critical position. The dog that did not bark is the independent, pre-Easter witness — and we do not have it.

Reply

The honest apologist should concede what is accurate here. The detailed third prediction may well have been sharpened. The neat “after three days” formula likely owes some of its precision to hindsight and to Hosea 6:2. We cannot reconstruct Jesus’ ipsissima verba, his exact words, and we should not pretend to. But the deflationary case has a soft seam. The ex eventu explanation works best against the clear, detailed predictions — and least well against exactly the awkward features noted above. If the evangelists were freely composing prophecies to fit the outcome, why leave the sign of Jonah a riddle? Why preserve the disciples’ failure to understand? Why let the temple saying stand as a hostile accusation rather than a Christian creed? A theory that explains the polished predictions by invention must still explain why the rough edges survived — and invention is a poor explanation of rough edges. Even Ehrman, pressing the apocalyptic logic, holds that Jesus’ message announced coming judgment and the vindication of the righteous. One need not believe in fulfilled prophecy to believe a condemned prophet anticipated his fate. And the “decades later” frame leaves out our earliest evidence: the creed Paul hands on in 1 Corinthians 15:3–7 shows that within a few years of the crucifixion the earliest Christians were already confessing his death, burial, resurrection, and appearances. That does not show the predictions were spoken before Easter; it shows the vindication they point to was proclaimed from the start. The critic keeps a real foothold: the gap between “Jesus anticipated vindication in veiled terms” and “Jesus prophesied his bodily resurrection on the third day” is wide, and the evidence does not close it.

Objection — “The disciples DID expect it — that is why they stole the body or hallucinated”

If he predicted it publicly, expectation theories revive: primed followers hallucinate, or stage the fulfilment. Your prediction premise feeds the naturalistic theories.

Reply

The texts you need for the predictions also record the expectation failing to form — incomprehension, flight, spices for a corpse, scepticism at the first reports. Priming that produces no expectation cannot produce hallucinations. As for staging: a hoax is a poor fit for men who went on proclaiming it, under threat, in the city of the execution. The prediction raises the stakes for every theory, including those. The disciples’ failure to understand also answers the skeptic’s sharpest rejoinder — if Jesus foretold his rising, why did no one wait at the tomb? — because the tradition itself says they did not understand him (Mark 9:32), an embarrassing admission about the church’s own pillars rather than a flattering invention.

The room: Meier popularized the criterion of embarrassment; Ehrman observes Mark’s deliberate arrangement of the disciples’ incomprehension, yet is among the best-known spokesmen of a robust skeptical wing that holds the whole package loosely. A generation ago, under Bultmann’s shadow, the passion predictions were near-automatically dismissed; in recent decades scholars have grown more willing to grant that Jesus foresaw his death. But the detailed predictions remain, for most critical scholars, partly shaped after the fact. The field has moved, but not to a consensus: there is no scholarly consensus that Jesus predicted his resurrection in specific terms, and an honest reader should not be told there is. This is a supporting strand, not a standalone proof.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: vaticinium ex eventu — the majority critical position, that the predictions were sharpened later. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"Prophecies after the event — the church wrote predictions into his mouth" Common

Strongest form

The easiest explanation: post-resurrection believers retrojected predictions to make Jesus look sovereign. The third passion prediction in particular reads like a checklist of the passion narrative that follows it: condemnation, Gentiles, mockery, spitting, flogging.

Reply

Then the retrojectors sabotaged themselves: they 'invented' predictions while also recording that nobody believed them — disciples confused, fleeing, women shopping for burial spices. Ex eventu authors harmonise; these texts preserve the dissonance. The detailed third prediction may well have been sharpened — but the trial's garbled temple-charge shows the saying circulating on hostile lips, and invention is a poor explanation of rough edges.

"After three days vs on the third day — the predictions do not even agree" Common

Strongest form

Mark says after three days; Matthew says on the third day; he died Friday and rose Sunday — barely a day and a half. And Matthew 12:40 is starker still: 'three days and three nights in the heart of the earth.' The math fails the prophecy.

Reply

Matthew keeps both phrases pages apart without anxiety. And the 'three days and three nights' wrinkle is at least as consistent with an inherited saying the writers felt bound to preserve as with free invention, since a fabricator working only from hindsight had no need to leave the wrinkle in.

Strongest form

"The guard story is Matthew’s apologetic fiction" Trickier

Strongest form

Only Matthew has guards — a transparent invention answering the theft rumor. You cannot cite fiction as hostile testimony.

Reply

Strip the guard entirely and P1 stands on Mark and John. But before discarding it: Matthew's account concedes a hostile rumor circulating 'to this day' — and the rumor itself ('disciples stole him while we slept') is evidence the tomb was known to be empty; you do not explain a body's absence unless it is absent. The polemic Matthew answers, whoever posted the guard, presupposes the tomb was vacant — and this argument does not need the guard at all.

"He predicted within a generation too — and was wrong; why trust the three-day call?" Hard

Strongest form

Jesus also said this generation will not pass before the Son of Man comes — a famous miss. A prophet with a failed prediction gets no credit for an alleged hit.

Reply

On a widely held reading, 'this generation' governs what the discourse itself calls 'these things' — the Temple's destruction, 'not one stone on another' — which happened in 70 AD, inside the generation; on that reading the discourse separates 'these things' from 'that day or that hour,' of which 'no one knows, not even the angels in heaven, nor the Son, but only the Father' (Mark 13:32) — the Son's not knowing the hour belongs to the genuine limits of his human consciousness, not to a deficiency in the divine nature he never ceased to possess. On a widely held reading of the discourse that is a hit rather than a miss — though Christians differ on how much else the discourse covers, and this site does not adjudicate between them. And methodologically: the death-and-vindication sayings stand or fall on their own evidence — a disputed reading of one apocalyptic discourse cannot un-empty a tomb.

"A risen man could have settled it — appear to Pilate, the Sanhedrin, Rome" Hard

Strongest form

If the point was a public, falsifiable test, the follow-through was strangely private — appearances to insiders. The watching judges never got their demonstration.

Reply

The appearances were less private than the objection allows — five hundred at once, a persecuting enemy (Paul), a sceptical brother (James). But the deeper answer is that the vindication was proclaimed from the start, in the city of the execution; on Matthew's report (Matthew 28:13–15; cf. Justin, Dialogue 108), the authorities' response was not 'show us' but 'say the disciples stole him,' conceding the vacancy. God's pattern in both Testaments is sufficient evidence for the willing, not coercive spectacle for the hostile — and a Sanhedrin tour would have left exactly the objection-maker we are answering, one miracle further back.

The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

The field has moved, but not to a consensus. A generation ago, under Bultmann’s shadow, the passion predictions were near-automatically dismissed. In recent decades scholars have grown more willing to grant that Jesus foresaw his death, and many are at least open to some authentic core behind the resurrection predictions. Sanders’ verdict on the temple saying is widely shared. But the detailed predictions remain, for most critical scholars, partly shaped after the fact.

A fairness most defenders skip: A robust skeptical wing, with Ehrman among its best-known spokesmen, holds the whole package loosely. There is no scholarly consensus that Jesus predicted his resurrection in specific terms, and an honest reader should not be told there is. The skeptic who says the detailed predictions may have been sharpened in the telling raises a possibility the evidence cannot rule out, and the believer who insists the Gospels preserve his predictions verbatim overreaches.

The bounded verdict: Our answer to the question is yes, in the form the evidence reaches: Jesus went to Jerusalem expecting to die and foretold, in veiled terms, that God would vindicate him beyond death. That expectation was not a disappointed hope. This is a supporting strand, not a standalone proof: the resurrection itself rests on other grounds — the early creed of 1 Corinthians 15:3–7, the empty tomb, the appearances — and on those grounds we hold that God raised him bodily.

    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    Lead with it and you lead with the weakest card. The clearest predictions are the ones most vulnerable to the charge of hindsight, and the survivors of the critical filter are precisely the vaguest — the detailed third prediction may well have been sharpened in the telling. So this argument does not, on its own, prove that Jesus possessed detailed foreknowledge of the resurrection, still less that such foreknowledge demonstrates his divinity; that is a limit on this argument, not on who he is. The believer who insists the Gospels preserve his predictions verbatim overreaches. Lead instead with what survives the sharpest criticism: a Jesus who went up to Jerusalem expecting to die and to be vindicated by God — real, early, and not the after-the-fact invention the deflationary reading needs. It is a supporting strand that coheres with the larger case; the resurrection itself rests on the early creed of 1 Corinthians 15:3–7, the empty tomb and the appearances, and on those grounds we hold that God raised him bodily.
