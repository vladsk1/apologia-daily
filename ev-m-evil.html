The Problem of Evil as Evidence for God

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and handle the hardest topic with care.

The argument in brief

Let it be said plainly, before any argument is offered: no argument removes the pain. The problem of evil is usually posed as an objection to God — if God exists, why is there evil and suffering? That objection is serious and it deserves to be felt, not explained away; this track is no substitute for grief or for sitting with someone who is hurting. What follows is a narrower observation, and it leaves the weight of suffering exactly where it is.

The observation is this: the very fact that we recognise something as genuinely evil — not just unpleasant or inconvenient, but truly wrong, like torturing a child for amusement — presupposes a real standard of good that the world has fallen short of. C. S. Lewis noticed it in his own atheist days: “A man does not call a line crooked unless he has some idea of a straight line.” And an objective standard of good points more naturally toward God, a personal source of the Good, than toward brute, impersonal fact. So the outrage that fuels the problem of evil, pressed hard enough, points back toward the God it was raised against.

In candor, this is a consideration, not a proof. It does not solve suffering; the evidential problem of evil (does this much suffering fit a good God?) remains live and unresolved, and a secular moral realist can hold objective evil without God — so one can keep the evil and decline the conclusion. It is a dispute about the best explanation, and it is no charge that unbelievers cannot be good. And even at its strongest it reaches a transcendent, personal source of the Good — not yet the triune God; that rests on revelation and the resurrection.

The argument, carefully

Three steps that turn the objection around.

P1

We recognise certain things as objectively, genuinely evil — not merely disliked, but acts that ought not to be.

P2

Objective evil presupposes an objective standard of good that such acts fall short of — “evil” is normative, not merely descriptive.

∴ C

An objective standard of good points more naturally toward God than toward brute fact, so even the recognition of evil points toward God. This does not explain why God permits suffering — and it is a consideration, not a proof.

This is a weighing of likelihoods, not a demonstration, and it is deliberately narrow — it is not a theodicy. Almost every objection either denies that evil is objective, offers a rival account of the standard (evolution), or presses the evidential problem (does this much suffering fit God?); spot which, and you are halfway to the answer.

Defending the premises

Why each premise is more plausible than its denial

P1 (that some things are objectively evil) is the load-bearing datum — and the one a thoroughgoing subjectivist denies. P2 (that objective evil needs a standard) is near-analytic; the contested move is the conclusion's inference to God, which is a weighing of explanations, not a proof.

P1 — evil is objective, not preference The load-bearing datum

Our response to the Holocaust, to torture, to cruelty presents itself — from the inside — as a response to something really wrong, not merely disliked. If evil were mere preference, moral protest would be incoherent; we would only be announcing our tastes. And note the irony: the popular form of the problem of evil insists on the reality of evil — which already assumes an objective wrong.

Honest narrowing: a sharper sceptic can sidestep this by running the problem as an internal critique (“granting your God and your standard, this evil is hard to square with him”) without claiming evil is objectively real. That version does not walk into the turnabout — it earns the defensive replies instead. The argument here engages the far more common objector who is genuinely, morally angry.

P2 — objective evil points to God Near-analytic; the inference is the contested part

Evil is a privation (Augustine) — a corruption of a good that ought to be whole, which presupposes the standard and the “ought-to-be.” And an objective ought, binding whether we like it or not, fits a personal, purposive source far better than indifferent matter, which has no way things are supposed to be. That is the is/ought gap: a complete physical description never yields the verdict “this is wrong.”

Honest narrowing: this is where the argument stops short of proof. A secular moral realist (Wielenberg) grants objective evil but makes the moral facts brute necessities needing no God; the theist replies only that a personal source better explains them (why these facts, why their authority, why we reliably know them) — a live, contested dispute, not a refutation.

The room: the move runs from Augustine (evil as privatio boni) and C. S. Lewis (the “crooked line” of his own atheist days) and is a cousin of the moral argument (Craig). The critics are weighty: the evidential problem is sharpest in William Rowe; its logical form was pressed by J. L. Mackie (who later conceded the free-will defense may show theism is not logically inconsistent); Alvin Plantinga’s free-will defense answers the logical version; and Erik Wielenberg holds objective evil with no God at all. No consensus that the turnabout succeeds; the evidential problem is genuinely unsettled.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Handle this gently — it touches real pain. Each objection in its strongest form first, then the reply. The hardest is flagged.

The one that matters most: Rowe’s evidential problem — not a contradiction, but the sheer weight of gratuitous-seeming suffering as evidence against God. The deep dive and its defensive companion engage it at length — take it with you, not just the reply below.

"The amount of suffering is evidence against God" Hardest

Strongest form

A good, all-powerful God would not permit gratuitous suffering. Rowe's fawn, burning to death unseen in a forest fire, serves no greater good anyone can point to. The sheer quantity of such suffering makes it unlikely that a good, omnipotent God exists.

Reply

Concede its force; this is the genuinely hard one, and no honest version pretends otherwise. The free-will defense and Plantinga's work are widely regarded as answering the logical version (that God and evil are contradictory) — but the evidential version remains live and unresolved, and the defensive essay engages it. Here, though, is the key: the turnabout and the evidential problem are about different things. The evidential problem asks whether this much suffering fits God. The turnabout asks what it even means to call it evil — and that it falls short of a real standard of good is not cancelled by the suffering's weight. Both can stand: evil is a hard problem for faith, and the reality of objective evil is a datum a godless universe must account for.

"Evil is just harm or suffering — no God needed" Common

Strongest form

Define evil naturally — as harm, pain, suffering — and no transcendent standard is required.

Reply

Harm and suffering are descriptive; calling them evil — something that ought not to be, that demands a response — is normative. Why should a configuration of matter that causes pain be wrong, rather than simply unwelcome? Naturalism can describe suffering in exhaustive detail and still not reach the ought-not-ness that makes it evil rather than merely unpleasant. The gap between "this hurts" and "this is wrong" is the whole question.

"Evil is just an evolved aversion" Trickier

Strongest form

Our sense of evil is an evolved reaction to things that harmed our ancestors — nothing more.

Reply

Grant the evolutionary history freely; it is very likely true. But it explains the feeling of revulsion, not the fact that genocide is truly wrong. If evil is only an aversion, then the torturer is not mistaken, merely differently wired — and almost no one believes that about real atrocities. We do not think the Nazi doctor had an unusual taste; we think he was wrong. Debunking the feeling leaves the objective wrongness we affirm standing: a genealogy of how we came to sense a fact is not yet a disproof of the fact.

"I can have objective evil without God" (Wielenberg) Hard

Strongest form

A secular moral realist need not deny objective evil. Erik Wielenberg holds that objective moral facts are brute, necessary features of reality, needing no divine ground — and that God is no less a brute posit, while his own view is more economical.

Reply

This is a serious position — reputable, not desperate — and it shows the argument is not a proof: one can keep objective evil and decline God. So concede that cleanly. The theist's claim is the more modest one: a personal source of the Good better explains the moral facts than free-floating necessities do — why these facts, why they have authority over us, and why survival-driven creatures reliably come to know them. Wielenberg answers with parity and parsimony; the exchange is genuinely live. It is a weighing of explanations, not a demonstration — but the theist has a real case, not a desperate one.

"Turning evil into an argument dodges the real pain" Trickier

Strongest form

Turning evil into an argument for God sidesteps the real suffering and the real challenge.

Reply

A fair caution, and it must be taken seriously rather than waved away — which is why the honest version opens by honoring the pain. This argument is not a pastoral answer to suffering, and it does not dismiss the force of the problem of evil. It is a narrower observation: that the reality of objective evil sits uneasily in a godless universe. To someone in grief, the right response is presence, not this argument. The two live on different levels and need not compete.

"Calling evil a 'privation' is just wordplay" Common

Strongest form

Calling evil a lack or privation rather than a real thing is a dodge that makes evil sound harmless.

Reply

It does the opposite. The privation view (Augustine) does not deny evil's reality or horror — a privation can be devastating, as blindness is a real lack of sight, no less terrible for being a lack. It is a metaphysical claim: evil is parasitic on good, a corruption of something that ought to be whole. And that "ought to be whole" is precisely the standard the argument points at. You cannot have a corruption without an original to be corrupted.

The honest state of the scholarship

Where the argument really stands

The logical problem of evil — that God and evil are strictly contradictory — is now widely regarded as answered, Mackie himself conceding the free-will defense may show theism is not logically inconsistent. The evidential problem is a different matter: it remains a live, serious, unsettled debate with careful work on both sides, and the turnabout concedes it rather than pretending it away. Secular moral realism of Wielenberg's kind is a reputable position; the moral-argument dispute it belongs to is genuinely contested.

The real fork: whether an objective standard of good is better explained by a personal source of the Good or left as a brute, free-floating fact. No neutral instrument settles it, and honest people land on both sides.

The bounded verdict: the turnabout is not a proof that forces the sceptic's hand; it does not solve suffering or deliver the God of any creed. What it does is quieter and, perhaps, more disarming: it asks the morally outraged objector to notice what his own outrage assumes — that cruelty is really, bindingly wrong — and where that straight line came from, by which he is so sure the world's cruelty is crooked. Even at full strength it reaches a transcendent, personal source of the Good, not the triune God; that this is the God who in Christ entered the suffering rather than explaining it away rests on revelation and the resurrection.

    One you weren’t coached on

    The objections above either denied objective evil or pressed the suffering. Here is a subtler one: a sceptic who refuses to supply objective evil at all, and runs the problem a different way. Reason it out cold, then reveal how the argument handles it. An honest “nailed it” is the first half of Gold.

    PH
    Philosopher
    "Clever, but I won't take the bait. I don't need to claim anything is objectively evil. I'll grant your God and your standard of good purely for the sake of argument, and then show that the evil we actually see is impossible to square with him. It's a contradiction inside your own system — so there's nothing for my outrage to 'presuppose,' and your turnabout never touches me."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    That is a fair and sophisticated move, and the honest answer is: you’re right that it sidesteps the turnabout. Run purely as an internal critique — “granting your God and your standard, this evil is hard to square with him” — it makes no claim that evil is objectively real, so there is indeed nothing for it to presuppose. The turnabout only engages the objector who means his outrage literally: that a real wrong has been done. But notice two things. First, the internal version forfeits the rhetorical force of righteous outrage — it is a cool logical exercise, not the cry of “this is monstrous” that gives the problem of evil its grip; most people who raise it do mean it literally. Second, the internal version is not unanswered — it just gets a different answer: the free-will defense, the evidential discussion, and the cross as presence, which the defensive essay engages at length. So nothing is refuted here; the turnabout simply has a defined scope. It is one consideration, aimed at the common objector — and it reaches a personal source of the Good, not the triune God, which rests on revelation and the resurrection.
