Christ Before Bethlehem: Preexistence in Paul

The deep dive explains the case. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild from memory Paul’s downward arc (sent, rich before poor, in the form of God, agent of creation), meet the “it is just the second Adam” objection, and hold the answer together in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

Most stories about Jesus run one way — upward: a teacher so revered that, over time, his followers lifted him higher until they called him God. The striking thing about the apostle Paul, our earliest Christian writer, is that his story runs the other way. His Christ is sent from the Father; he "though he was rich… became poor"; he was "in the form of God" before he was "born in the likeness of men"; he is the one "through whom are all things." The movement is not up but down: a descent, then a return.

The case is cumulative, and no one text has to carry it alone. Christ is sent — and in Galatians 4:4 the sending is named before the birth. He "became poor" (2 Corinthians 8:9), which presupposes a wealth held before the becoming. He was "in the form of God" before "taking the form of a servant" and being "born in the likeness of men" (Philippians 2:6–7) — an ordered sequence the first Adam, human from the first, never made. And he is the one "through whom are all things" (1 Corinthians 8:6; Colossians 1:16–17) — the agent through whom the world was made stands, by the logic of the claim, before and outside it. The strongest objection, James Dunn's, reads Philippians 2 as Adam Christology with no personal preexistence; the reply (Wright, Fee) is that "born in the likeness of men" is a coming-to-be human the first Adam never underwent — and that the case does not stand on Philippians alone.

Honest scope: this establishes preexistence, not, by itself, full deity. A figure could in principle preexist his birth and still be a creature; so preexistence is a step toward the deity of Christ, not identical with it, and whether the preexistent one is creature or Creator is the further work of the divine-identity essays. This also reframes the skeptic's challenge: Bart Ehrman grants a preexistent Christ in material older than Paul — what he disputes is not the date but the rank. The pre-Pauline dating of the Philippians hymn is contested (Fee), the meaning of harpagmos is one of the most disputed words in the New Testament, and Dunn's Adam reading is a serious position, not a rout — which is why the argument is built to survive even if the hymn is set aside.

Understand it

Not more prose — the load-bearing structure. See the skeleton, then learn to defend each bone.

The argument, formally

Paul presents a preexistent Christ — a descent from the Father, not a man later exalted.

P1

Paul's letters repeatedly present Christ in a downward arc: he is sent from the Father — "God sent forth his Son, born of woman" (Galatians 4:4), the sending named before the birth; he "though he was rich… became poor" (2 Corinthians 8:9); he was "in the form of God" before "taking the form of a servant" and being "born in the likeness of men" (Philippians 2:6–7); and he is the one "through whom are all things" (1 Corinthians 8:6; Colossians 1:16–17).

P2

Each of these describes a transition from a prior state, or a position outside the created order — not the elevation of a man who began at birth. "Became poor" presupposes a prior wealth; "born in the likeness of men" is a coming-to-be human the first Adam never underwent; the agent through whom the world was made is not among created things. The skeptical escapes (Dunn's Adam-Christology for the hymn, Wisdom-poetry, "sent" as merely functional) each answer one text; the cumulative pattern, and the named person who is its subject throughout, survive them.

∴ C

Therefore Paul presents a preexistent Christ — one who existed before Bethlehem, who came down and returned, not a man promoted upward over time. (This is a step toward the deity of Christ, not identical with it; whether the preexistent one is creature or Creator is the further work of the divine-identity essays.)

This establishes preexistence, not, by itself, full deity — a long step from "a man later deified," but not the whole distance to Nicaea. It reframes the skeptic's challenge too: Bart Ehrman grants a preexistent Christ in material older than Paul; what he disputes is not the date but the rank. And several readings are genuinely contested — the pre-Pauline dating of the hymn, the meaning of harpagmos, Dunn's Adam reading — which is why the case is built to survive even if the hymn is set aside.

Why "it's just the second Adam" is the popular skeptic's move.

The strongest form is James Dunn's: the Philippians hymn reads as Adam Christology — Adam grasped at equality with God and fell; Christ, the last Adam, refused and was exalted — every line contrasting two men, with no need to assert Christ personally existed before Bethlehem. The reply grants the Adam echoes but answers that a merely human second Adam was never "in the form of God" before being "born in the likeness of men" — a sequence Adam never made — and that the case does not stand on Philippians alone.

Defending the premises

Why each premise is more plausible than its denial

Support for P1 — the downward arc, pressed into letter after letter

Sent, and rich before he was poor

Paul never sets out to prove Christ preexisted; he assumes it in the offhand way a writer treats settled common ground. Christ is "sent": "God sent forth his Son, born of woman" (Galatians 4:4); "by sending his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh" (Romans 8:3). Taken alone, "sent" need not imply preexistence — prophets are sent — and that is granted; but in Galatians the sending is named before the birth. Then "though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor" (2 Corinthians 8:9): the logic is a transition from a prior state — he was rich, and became poor; a man born poor did not "become" poor for anyone's sake.

In the form of God, and the agent of creation

The fullest statement is the hymn of Philippians 2 — widely regarded as pre-Pauline, though debated (Fee argues Paul composed it). Christ "though he was in the form of God (morphē theou)… emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant (morphē doulou), being born in the likeness of men" (2:6–7). Morphē is the hinge: the same noun describes what he became — a servant — and his servanthood was no costume, so the "form of God" he began in is equally real. "Emptied himself" names no thing he emptied himself of — grammatically he emptied himself by adding the servant's form, not by subtracting deity (Fee). And in the reshaped confession of 1 Corinthians 8:6 and Colossians 1:16–17 he is the one "through whom are all things… by him all things were created" — an agent of creation who is not among created things.

Support for P2 — the transition logic, and why the case survives Dunn

The strongest objection, and the reply on the text

The most serious challenge is James D. G. Dunn's: the Philippians hymn is best read as Adam Christology — Adam grasped at equality with God and was condemned; Christ, the last Adam, refused, obeyed, and was exalted — every line contrasting two men, with no need to assert Christ personally existed before Bethlehem. Dunn located clear personal preexistence in John, not Paul. The Adam echoes are real, and the strongest reply engages them — Wright and Fee differently. Wright grants the echoes but shows the frame they cannot carry: a merely human second Adam was never "in the form of God" before being "born in the likeness of men," and that last phrase is the one the Adam reading cannot absorb, for to be "born in the likeness of men" is to come to be human — a transition the first Adam, human from the first, never underwent. Fee, more skeptical the Adam parallel is in view, adds that the paired morphē theou and morphē doulou resist reduction to two human postures.

Wisdom, "firstborn," and why Philippians is not the whole case

Two related objections. Judaism personified God's Wisdom as an agent of creation (Proverbs 8) without meaning a second divine person, so Paul's language may be Wisdom-poetry; but Wisdom there is a personification of an attribute, while Paul's Christ is a person with a name — sent, made poor, crucified, raised — a biography, not a personified quality. And "firstborn of all creation" (Colossians 1:15), read by Jehovah's Witnesses as the first creature, is answered by the word: prōtotokos marks priority and rank (David, the youngest son, is made "the firstborn," Psalm 89:27), and the next verse blocks the created-being reading — "by him all things were created" (1:16). The honest limit: even granting the Adam reading of the hymn, "became poor" and "through whom are all things" still stand — each faces its own skeptical reading, but those are separate challenges the Adam frame does not supply, so conceding Philippians does not concede the case, which rests on the cumulative pattern and the personal subject throughout.

The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: James Dunn's reading of Philippians 2 as Adam Christology — that the hymn contrasts two men, the first Adam and the last, and asserts no personal preexistence at all. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the reply below.

"Philippians 2 is Adam Christology — two men contrasted, no preexistence" Hard

Strongest form

James Dunn's reading, argued from the text and not to be caricatured: the hymn is best read as Adam Christology. Adam, made in God's image, grasped at equality with God and was condemned; Christ, the last Adam, faced the same archetypal choice, refused to grasp, obeyed unto death, and was exalted. On this reading every line maps onto a contrast between two men, and the hymn need not assert that Christ personally existed before Bethlehem. Dunn held the clearest statement of personal preexistence in the New Testament is in John, not Paul.

Reply

The Adam echoes are real, and the strongest reply grants them — then shows the frame they cannot carry. A merely human second Adam was never "in the form of God" before "taking the form of a servant" and being "born in the likeness of men"; that last phrase is the one the Adam reading cannot absorb, for to be "born in the likeness of men" is to come to be human — a transition the first Adam, human from the first, never underwent (Wright). Fee, more skeptical the Adam parallel is even in view, adds that morphē theou and morphē doulou resist reduction to two human postures. And crucially, the case does not stand on Philippians alone: even granting the Adam reading of the hymn, "became poor" and "through whom are all things" are separate challenges the Adam frame does not supply.

"The creation language is just Wisdom-poetry, not a preexistent person" Trickier

Strongest form

Judaism already personified God's Wisdom as an agent of creation "before" the world (Proverbs 8) without meaning a second divine person. So Paul's "through whom are all things" may be Wisdom-poetry, and "sent" and "became poor" may be functional or metaphorical rather than a literal descent — a way of speaking about God's action, not a person who came down.

Reply

The honest concession is that Wisdom-personification is a genuine part of the background, and Paul's language is surely coloured by it. But Wisdom in Proverbs is a poetic personification of an attribute of God; Paul's Christ is a person with a name, who is "sent," who "became poor," who was crucified and raised — a biography, not a personified quality. The Wisdom motif illuminates the creation language; it does not dissolve the personal subject who undergoes the descent.

"'Firstborn of all creation' means Jesus was the first created being" Common

Strongest form

Colossians calls Christ "the firstborn of all creation" (1:15). Jehovah's Witnesses and others read this plainly: the first and highest creature, made before the rest. And Colossians' own Pauline authorship is disputed, so even its testimony may be later than Paul.

Reply

"Firstborn" (prōtotokos) marks priority and rank, not manufacture. Scripture uses it exactly that way of David — "I will make him the firstborn, the highest of the kings of the earth" (Psalm 89:27) — though David was the youngest of Jesse's sons and, obviously, not literally born first. And the very next verse blocks the created-being reading: "for by him all things were created" (Colossians 1:16) — the one by whom all created things came to be is not himself one of them. As for authorship: grant the dispute, and the case does not fall, because the preexistence pattern runs through the letters no one contests Paul wrote.

"'Sent' and 'became poor' are metaphors, not a literal descent" Trickier

Strongest form

Prophets are "sent" without preexisting; a rich man who gives to the poor "becomes poor" without any prior heavenly wealth. So "sent" may be functional (God commissions his Messiah) and "became poor" may describe Jesus's earthly self-impoverishment — his ministry among the lowly — not a descent from a prior state. Read that way, none of it requires that Christ existed before Bethlehem.

Reply

For "sent" taken alone, the point is granted outright — which is exactly why the argument is cumulative rather than resting on it; but in Galatians 4:4 the sending is named before the birth. And 2 Corinthians 8:9 resists the flattening: "though he was rich… he became poor" is the grammar of a transition, and a man born poor did not "become" poor for anyone's sake — the sentence presupposes a wealth held before the becoming. No single verse has to carry the case; the same shape recurs — sent, rich-before-poor, in the form of God, agent of creation — and a metaphor read into each one still has to explain why the person who is its subject has a heavenly starting point in text after text.

"Even if Paul teaches preexistence, that doesn't make Christ God" Hard

Strongest form

Grant the whole downward arc. A figure could preexist his birth and still be a creature — a great angel, an exalted heavenly being — without being God. This is exactly Bart Ehrman's move: he accepts a preexistent Christ in material older than Paul, but reads him as a subordinate divine being, God's chief angel, not God. So even a successful preexistence case leaves the real question — creature or Creator — untouched.

Reply

Correct, and the argument concedes it in its own voice: this establishes preexistence, not, by itself, full deity. That is why the debate is not "late versus early" but a question of rank, and why this track defers the rank question to the divine-identity essays (the divine Name, the New Testament's whole witness). What preexistence does settle is decisive against the popular story: the earliest Christian writer does not present a man slowly promoted to heaven but one who came down — a long step from "a man later deified," even if not the whole distance to Nicaea.
