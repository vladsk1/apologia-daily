God Crucified: How the Cross Reveals Who God Is

The deep dive explains the case. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild from memory the four steps of the argument, hold the fences that keep it orthodox, answer its five objections, then hold it together in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

Mark tells the death of Jesus without ornament. Darkness has covered the land since noon. At the ninth hour the man from Nazareth cries out, in Aramaic, the opening line of a psalm of lament — “Eloi, Eloi, lema sabachthani?” which means, “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” — and a little later, with a loud cry, he breathes his last. Then comes the sentence Mark has been holding back. The voice from heaven has called Jesus God’s Son at his baptism, and again from the cloud on the mountain; unclean spirits have shrieked it. But the first human being in the story to confess it is a Roman officer, and what moves him, on Mark’s telling, is above all the manner of a death. “And when the centurion, who stood facing him, saw that in this way he breathed his last, he said, ‘Truly this man was the Son of God!’” (Mark 15:33–39)

That is a strange place to look for God. In the ancient world a crucified man was the opposite of divine: a slave’s death, a rebel’s death, and to a Jew a death under the curse of the Law. Yet Mark chooses that moment, and no other, for the confession his whole book has been driving toward. The earliest Christians, who included Jesus within the identity of the one God of Israel, included the crucified Jesus there too. So they read the cross not as the place where God was absent but as the place where the Son, who is truly God, made known most fully who God is: a God whose glory is not only enthroned above the world but poured out for it.

Who suffers there, and how, must be said with care. The Son, who is truly God, entered real human suffering and death on the cross — God is not a spectator above our pain. This is not to say that the divine nature “dies,” nor that the Father suffered (an error called patripassianism). The suffering is located in the Son according to his assumed human nature — one of the Trinity suffered in the flesh — not in the divine nature and not in the Father.

Honesty about scope is part of the case. This argument establishes a bounded claim: that the New Testament, which includes Jesus within the identity of the one God of Israel, deliberately includes his humiliation and death there too, and so presents the cross as a disclosure of who God is — a God whose glory is shown in self-giving love. It does not, by itself, prove that the claim is true. That the first Christians read the cross this way is a historical finding; whether they were right depends on whether God vindicated the crucified one, which is the question the resurrection evidence, beginning with the creed Paul quotes in 1 Corinthians 15:3–7, is meant to answer. It does not settle how the cross saves. And it is not a theodicy. It does not explain why this child suffers this way. It claims something different: that the God against whom we protest has not stayed out of reach of the protest.

Understand it

Not more prose — the load-bearing structure. See the skeleton, then learn to defend each bone.

The argument, formally

The cross as the place where the Son, who is truly God, made known most fully who God is.

The argument has four steps.

S1

The earliest Christians included Jesus within the unique identity of the one God of Israel.

S2

The Jesus they so included was not only the exalted and pre-existent Lord but the humiliated and crucified one: the New Testament tells his self-humbling and death in the very language Israel’s Scriptures use for God’s own exaltation, throne and worship.

S3

If the one who belongs to God’s identity is the one who humbled himself to death on a cross, then that self-giving is not foreign to who God is; it discloses it.

∴ S4

Therefore the earliest Christians read the cross as a revelation — on their own telling, the decisive revelation — of who God is.

Each step carries a different kind of weight. The first is a historical claim about early Christian belief, argued at length elsewhere in this Library and summarized below. The second is exegetical, and it is the heart of the argument. The third is a conceptual step, and it is only as safe as the Christology that governs it, so it needs two fences.

The first fence concerns who suffers. The historic confession is that the eternal Son of God, without ceasing to be God, took a complete human nature and in that nature suffered and died. The cross does not mean that the Father was crucified, that the divine nature as such bled or perished, or that the Trinity was torn apart. The second fence concerns how the cross saves. Christians across the traditions confess that “Christ died for our sins” (1 Corinthians 15:3), but how exactly the cross saves — the mechanism of the atonement — is a question on which sincere Christians have long differed, and this argument deliberately does not settle it. Its subject is narrower: not what the cross accomplished, but what it reveals.

Why “that is later theology read back into the texts” is the most serious academic challenge.

Developmental scholars argue that to speak of the cross as revealing “who God is” is to read the creeds of the fourth and fifth centuries back into first-century texts; we reply that the disagreement is less about dates than about what kind of being the earliest Christians took Jesus to be — and, we argue, the earliest worship answers it. The reply begins with a concession: the developed creedal vocabulary came later. The communication-of-properties language is the church’s later articulation, not a New Testament term. But the Philippians 2 hymn, widely regarded as pre-Pauline, places Christ’s “death on a cross” on the path to the name above every name and applies to him Isaiah’s oath that every knee will bow to God. The development is in the clarity, not in the substance.

Defending the steps

Why each step is more plausible than its denial

Support for S1 — Jesus within the identity of the one God

Included within the unique identity of the one God of Israel

The first premise is defended in full in our essays on Jesus as God in the New Testament, the Shema and the worship of Jesus, so a summary will do here. What the New Testament does, repeatedly and across independent authors, is something Bauckham has called including Jesus within the unique identity of the one God of Israel. Jesus is placed on the Creator’s side of the line that Jewish monotheism drew between God and everything else: he shares God’s throne, God’s acts of creation, God’s eternal name, and — most tellingly — God’s worship.

The worship is the decisive marker, and it is early

Paul preserves an Aramaic prayer untranslated into his Greek-speaking churches: Marana tha, “Our Lord, come!” That the invocation survives in Aramaic is the point. It carries us back behind Paul’s Greek to the Aramaic-speaking Judean church — to the very first community, calling on the risen Jesus as the Lord who will come. Larry Hurtado documented a pattern of devotion to Jesus that emerged, in his phrase, “amazingly early,” among Jewish believers who would have regarded worshiping any creature as blasphemy.

An honest caution

Bauckham’s “divine identity” framework is influential but not uncontested: James D. G. Dunn and, from a different angle, James F. McGrath have questioned how sharply the boundary of divine identity can be drawn, and Hurtado — broadly an ally — preferred to frame the data as devotional practice rather than in Bauckham’s identity categories. Nothing in this argument depends on Bauckham’s vocabulary as such. It depends on what the worship evidence shows: that the first Christians gave Jesus the devotion Jewish monotheism reserved for God alone.

Whose argument this is

The phrase “God crucified” belongs, as a title, to the British New Testament scholar Richard Bauckham. We draw on his exegesis of particular texts, and on that alone: it is a historian’s and an interpreter’s argument, to be weighed like any other, and nothing here rests on his wider theology. The texts themselves are the evidence.

Support for S2 — the humiliation told in the language of God’s own glory, throne and worship

It would be possible, in principle, to include an exalted Jesus in God’s identity while treating his earthly life and death as a regrettable prelude — something he passed through on the way to glory and left behind. The New Testament does not do that. In three places it tells the humiliation itself in the language of God’s own glory, throne and worship; in a fourth, Mark, the death itself becomes the moment of confession.

Isaiah’s throne and Isaiah’s Servant

Isaiah had seen “the Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up” (Isaiah 6:1); and when the same prophet turns to the Servant who is despised and crushed, he says that he too shall be “high and lifted up” (Isaiah 52:13), reaching for the very words of the throne-vision. The God who “inhabits eternity,” Isaiah adds, is the same God who dwells “with him who is of a contrite and lowly spirit” (Isaiah 57:15). And John draws the threads together without apology: Isaiah, he says, “saw his glory and spoke of him” — of Jesus (John 12:41). The exaltation of the crucified Servant is told in the language of YHWH’s own throne.

The verbal link is closer in Hebrew than in English. The same two roots, “be high” (rûm) and “be lifted up” (nāśā’), stand together in all three verses: of the Lord enthroned (6:1), of the Servant (52:13), and of “the One who is high and lifted up” (57:15). And 57:15 adds a second thread. The word rendered “contrite” there comes from the same Hebrew root as “crushed” in the Servant song — “he was crushed for our iniquities” (53:5).

Bauckham proposes that early Christian readers made exactly this connection, using a Jewish interpretive habit later rabbis would list among their rules — gezerâ shāvâ, the practice of reading together passages that share a distinctive word. That is his proposal, and it is an inference about how the texts were read, not a report of what any first-century reader wrote down. But John’s own text shows the pairing at work. Within three verses he quotes Isaiah 53:1 (John 12:38: ‘Lord, who has believed what he heard from us?’) and Isaiah 6:10, the commission that follows the throne-vision, and then says that Isaiah “saw his glory” (John 12:38–41). The Servant song and the throne-vision sit together in John’s mind, and the glory he says Isaiah saw belongs to Jesus.

The pairing also explains one of John’s most characteristic habits of speech. In the Greek Old Testament the Servant of Isaiah 52:13 will be “lifted up” and “glorified” — two verbs John uses again and again of the cross. “As Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up”; “When you have lifted up the Son of Man, then you will know that I am he”; “And I, when I am lifted up from the earth, will draw all people to myself,” which John glosses, “He said this to show by what kind of death he was going to die.” And as the cross approaches: “The hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified”; “Now is the Son of Man glorified, and God is glorified in him.” For John the lifting up on the cross is the exaltation. He does not tell a story of humiliation followed by glory; he tells a story in which the humiliation is where the glory shows.

The honest limit: Bauckham’s specific reading of the Isaiah passages is a distinctive proposal; it is suggestive and well grounded in John’s own juxtapositions, but it has not been weighed as widely as his wider framework, and it should be presented as one strong reading, not a consensus. By itself the Servant’s exaltation does not make the Servant divine — Isaiah’s God exalts the humble. It is John who draws the further inference, assigning the glory of the throne-vision to Jesus.

Philippians 2: the downward path and the name

The second witness is older than any Gospel. In Philippians 2:6–11 Paul quotes what most scholars regard as a pre-Pauline hymn. Christ, “though he was in the form of God, did not count equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men. And being found in human form, he humbled himself by becoming obedient to the point of death, even death on a cross. Therefore God has highly exalted him and bestowed on him the name that is above every name, so that at the name of Jesus every knee should bow, in heaven and on earth and under the earth, and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father.”

A note of care on “emptied himself”: Christ “emptied himself” by adding — taking the form of a servant and veiling his glory (the Greek names no thing he emptied himself of). Not that he set aside, lost, or diminished his deity. He remained what he was and took up what he was not; full deity and immutability are intact.

“Every knee should bow… every tongue confess” is lifted from Isaiah 45:23. In its original context this is one of the most fiercely monotheistic passages in all of Scripture. In Isaiah, YHWH declares, “I am God, and there is no other,” and swears that to him alone “every knee shall bow, every tongue shall swear allegiance.” The hymn takes that text and applies it to Jesus.

The hardest word in the passage, harpagmos (“a thing to be grasped”), is genuinely contested, but on the reading defended by Roy Hoover and developed by N. T. Wright the verse renders something like: “he did not regard equality with God as something to be used for his own advantage.” The shape of the hymn is then striking: precisely because he was equal with God, he did not treat that equality as a perk to be exploited, but spent it in self-giving. And the “therefore” of verse 9 rewards the self-humbling of one who was already “in the form of God.” The same one who descends to “death on a cross” — the slave’s death — receives the name and the worship that Isaiah reserves for God. The hymn does not say he became worthy of the divine name in spite of the cross. It places the cross on the path to the name. On the orthodox reading the descent reveals the divine character rather than suspending the divine nature.

Revelation 5: the Lamb on the throne

In Revelation 4 John sees the throne of God and the worship of heaven offered to the Creator: “Worthy are you, our Lord and God, to receive glory and honor and power, for you created all things.” Then in chapter 5 he is told of the Lion of the tribe of Judah, and turns to see instead, “between the throne and the four living creatures and among the elders,” “a Lamb standing, as though it had been slain.” The new song is sung to the Lamb precisely because of his death — “Worthy are you… for you were slain, and by your blood you ransomed people for God” — and the chapter ends with every creature in heaven and on earth and under the earth and in the sea saying, “To him who sits on the throne and to the Lamb be blessing and honor and glory and might forever and ever!” By the book’s end the city has one throne, “the throne of God and of the Lamb.”

The setting makes the point sharp. In the very same book an angel twice refuses the worship John offers and redirects it to God (Revelation 19:10; 22:8–9); the Lamb receives it without a word of protest. And the Lamb is not worshiped in spite of the wounds. He is identified by them. The figure who shares the worship of the Creator is the slaughtered one.

Mark and the cry of dereliction

The fourth witness is the darkest, and it returns us to Mark. This is why Mark (and Matthew, 27:46) can place on the lips of the dying Jesus the opening cry of Psalm 22, “My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?” (Mark 15:34). The one who shares the divine identity enters, on the cross, the depths of human god-forsakenness. On the divine-identity reading this is not a lapse into something less than God but the fullest disclosure of who God is: the God of Israel is the God who gives himself, to the uttermost, for his people.

A note of care on who suffers: It is the incarnate Son, in his full humanity, who suffers, dies, and cries out — so that God himself, in the person of the Son, has entered our suffering and death for us. Not that the divine nature suffered or changed, and not that the Father was crucified or forsaken (the ancient error of patripassianism). The Father is not the Son; the impassible God does not perish; the cross reveals who God is, it does not make God mortal. Nor was the Son torn from the Father or the Trinity divided: the Father never ceased to love the Son, and the union of the persons was never broken. The forsakenness is real, borne in the Son’s human experience, not a severing within the one God.

Psalm 22 is a prayer of the righteous sufferer, and Jesus prays it as his own. Bauckham reads the cry as Jesus taking up that prayer not only for himself but in solidarity with all who have prayed it — entering, from the inside, the condition of the godforsaken. The psalm itself does not end in the dark. It turns to a God who “has not despised or abhorred the affliction of the afflicted, and he has not hidden his face from him, but has heard, when he cried to him” (22:24), and it closes with the nations turning to the LORD (22:27–28). We should not use that ending to drain the cry of its anguish; Mark records the first verse, not the last. But the Christian claim about the cry is the one our essay on suffering makes: there is no pit so deep that God in Christ has not been there first. This does not explain the pit. It populates it.

Support for S3 — who suffers, and how the church says it without heresy

Where the idea comes from

Early in the second century, Ignatius of Antioch, on his way to execution in Rome, asked the Roman Christians not to hinder his martyrdom: “Permit me to be an imitator of the passion of my God.” Writing to Polycarp he held the paradox together in one breath: “Look for Him who is above all time, eternal and invisible, yet who became visible for our sakes; impalpable and impassible, yet who became passible on our account; and who in every kind of way suffered for our sakes.” Ignatius did not explain how the impassible one could become passible. He simply would not let go of either half.

Cyril of Alexandria’s Second Letter to Nestorius, read and ratified at Ephesus in 431 and again at Chalcedon in 451, insisted that the one Christ truly suffered while the divine nature as such did not, and the Second Council of Constantinople in 553 anathematized anyone who would not confess that “our Lord Jesus Christ who was crucified in the flesh is true God and the Lord of Glory and one of the Holy Trinity.” Constantinople II’s formula concerns the one person of the Son crucified “in the flesh.” Historians sometimes call it the “theopaschite” formula, and it is orthodox: it is neither the error that the Father suffered (patripassianism) nor the error that the divine nature itself suffered.

In the Reformation era Martin Luther, in his Heidelberg Disputation of 1518, set a “theologian of glory” against a “theologian of the cross,” arguing that God is truly known through suffering and the cross rather than by reasoning up to his invisible attributes from his works. (Luther is cited as a historical milestone in the use of the theme; this argument does not adjudicate any intra-Christian dispute.) In the twentieth century the German theologian Jürgen Moltmann pressed the theme much further in The Crucified God (1972), arguing that the cross is an event within God’s own life in which God himself suffers. That last step is one the classical faith declines to take.

How the church says “God died” without heresy

The New Testament already shows how. Paul writes that the rulers of this age “crucified the Lord of glory” (1 Corinthians 2:8); Peter tells the crowd in Jerusalem, “you killed the Author of life” (Acts 3:15). Neither means that glory or life itself died. Both name the person by his divine titles and predicate of him what he underwent in his human nature. Later theology called this the “communication of properties” (communicatio idiomatum): because the Son is one person with two natures, what belongs to either nature may truly be said of the one person.

Cyril of Alexandria stated the rule with care, and every clause of his sentence is load-bearing:

“On this account we say that he suffered and rose again; not as if God the Word suffered in his own nature stripes, or the piercing of the nails, or any other wounds, for the Divine nature is incapable of suffering, inasmuch as it is incorporeal, but since that which had become his own body suffered in this way, he is also said to suffer for us; for he who is in himself incapable of suffering was in a suffering body.”

Notice what Cyril refuses on both sides. He refuses to say that the divine nature was wounded. He equally refuses to say that only a man suffered while God watched: “he is also said to suffer for us,” and the “he” is God the Word. The suffering is real, and it is the Son’s own, because the body that suffered is his own body. That is why the Second Council of Constantinople could require Christians to confess that the one “crucified in the flesh is true God and the Lord of Glory and one of the Holy Trinity” — and why that confession is neither the error of saying the Father suffered nor the error of saying the divine nature as such suffered. The Council of Chalcedon in 451 drew the boundary that has defined orthodox Christianity across Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant lines ever since: one and the same Christ, “truly God and truly man,” made known “in two natures, inconfusedly, unchangeably, indivisibly, inseparably.”

Why the third step follows

If the one who hangs on the cross is personally the Son of God, then what he does there — giving himself, to the point of death, for others — is the act of a divine person, done in his human nature. That act therefore tells us who that person is. And since the Son is one God with the Father and the Spirit, it tells us who God is. What the Son undergoes in the flesh — hunger, wounds, death — he undergoes in his human nature, not in the divine nature as such; but the self-giving is his own free act, begun, as Philippians puts it, by one who was already “in the form of God.” So the cross discloses not that God is mortal but what kind of love God is: not a power that saves itself, but a love that gives itself.

Support for S4 — what the cross-as-revelation claim does and does not establish

What the argument establishes

This argument establishes a bounded claim: that the New Testament, which includes Jesus within the identity of the one God of Israel, deliberately includes his humiliation and death there too, and so presents the cross as a disclosure of who God is — a God whose glory is shown in self-giving love. It argues that this reading is not a later imposition but is present in some of the earliest christological material we have, and that it can be stated without heresy, by the classical grammar of one person in two natures.

What it does not establish

It does not, by itself, prove that the claim is true. That the first Christians read the cross this way is a historical finding; whether they were right depends on whether God vindicated the crucified one, which is the question the resurrection evidence, beginning with the creed Paul quotes in 1 Corinthians 15:3–7, is meant to answer. It does not settle how the cross saves: some traditions emphasize Christ bearing the penalty of sin in our place; others, his victory over the powers of sin and death; others, the satisfaction of divine honor, or the recapitulation of humanity, or the transforming power of self-giving love. These differ in emphasis, and each tradition argues its case — but they are the family’s internal debate, held within a shared confession the New Testament states plainly. And it is not a theodicy. It does not explain why this child suffers this way. It claims something different: that the God against whom we protest has not stayed out of reach of the protest.

The verdict

The objection that a crucified man cannot reveal God does not hold as a presumption: it assumes that divine glory and self-humbling exclude one another, and the earliest Christian witnesses deny that they do — the Philippians hymn exalts to the name above every name the one who went to “death on a cross,” and in Revelation the slain Lamb is worshiped with the one on the throne. Nor does the charge of incoherence hold as a presumption: the claim, made of one person in two natures, does not affirm and deny the same thing of the same subject in the same respect, and while philosophers still debate how fully the two-natures account succeeds, a formal contradiction has not been shown.

The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the most serious academic challenge is flagged above.

The most serious academic challenge: the developmental objection — that the idea of the cross revealing “who God is” reads the creeds of the fourth and fifth centuries back into first-century texts. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the reply below.

“This reads later theology back into the texts” Hard

Strongest form

The most serious academic challenge comes from the developmental reading of early Christology. Bart Ehrman argues in How Jesus Became God that the historical Jesus did not claim divinity, that his earliest followers held an “exaltation” Christology — God raised the man Jesus and adopted him to divine status — yet he also finds a pre-existent Christ already in material older than Paul, a figure he takes to have been conceived at first as a subordinate divine being — God’s chief angel, or a divinity of a lesser order — whose elevation to the co-equal God confessed at Nicaea in 325 is the later step. James Dunn read the Philippians hymn as Adam Christology, a contrast between two men rather than the descent of a pre-existent person. And in its academic form the agency reading is subtler still: the New Testament historian James F. McGrath, contesting Bauckham’s and Wright’s “divine-identity” category, grants that John’s language about Jesus is genuinely exalted yet argues that Second Temple Judaism could let God share his name and functions with a supreme agent without a second God arising. On any of these views, to speak of the cross as revealing “who God is” is to read the creeds of the fourth and fifth centuries back into first-century texts.

Reply

The reply begins with a concession, and it is narrower than it first appears. The developed creedal vocabulary came later: the New Testament hands us the data — the throne, the name, the worship, the tensions — but not the fourth-century grammar that later systematized it. The communication-of-properties language above is the church’s later articulation, not a New Testament term. What the developmental objection gets wrong is the next step. The disagreement is not really about dates — Ehrman himself finds a pre-existent Christ in material older than Paul’s letters — but about what kind of being the earliest Christians took him to be. And the evidence answers that question with the one thing his developmental thesis cannot easily accommodate: the earliest worship. The Philippians hymn, widely regarded as pre-Pauline, already applies an exclusive-worship text of YHWH to Jesus (the pre-Pauline dating is itself debated — Gordon Fee argues Paul composed the hymn — but the worship-of-Jesus datum stands either way).

The hymn does not merely include an exalted Jesus in God’s worship; it places “death on a cross” on the road to the name above every name. So the pairing of humiliation and divine identity is not a late Johannine refinement. It is in a text Paul was quoting in the mid-50s or early 60s, and on the majority view one he inherited. Against Dunn, N. T. Wright and Gordon Fee argue that the hymn describes one who was already “in the form of God” before “taking the form of a servant” and being “born in the likeness of men” — a transition Adam never made. And against McGrath’s agency reading, the same line holds here that holds elsewhere: grant that God’s name and functions could be entrusted to a supreme agent, and the worship itself still could not be. (McGrath replies that the decisive line in Jewish worship was sacrifice, which Jesus does not receive; we think a shared doxology and the invocation Marana tha reach past that line, but the point is contested.) The development is in the clarity, not in the substance.

“Isaiah 52:13 and 6:1 share a phrase, not a meaning” Trickier

Strongest form

A careful reader — and not only a skeptical one — can press the exegetical link hard. First, the Hebrew pairing of “high” and “lifted up” is not reserved for God. Isaiah uses the same language of everything proud that God will bring low: “against all that is proud and lofty, against all that is lifted up… against all the cedars of Lebanon, lofty and lifted up” (Isaiah 2:12–13). A shared phrase is thin ground for a claim about identity. Second, gezerâ shāvâ is named in lists of rabbinic interpretive rules that are later than the New Testament, so attributing it to the earliest Christians is a reconstruction. Third, the referent of John 12:41 is itself debated: “he saw his glory” may point to the throne-vision of Isaiah 6, to the Servant of Isaiah 53, or to both. On this view Bauckham has found a pattern in the Hebrew that the first readers need never have seen.

Reply

Much of this should simply be granted. Nothing here claims that the prophet Isaiah, writing centuries earlier, intended a cross; the claim is about how the New Testament writers read him. The gezerâ shāvâ mechanism is Bauckham’s proposal, a plausible reconstruction rather than an attested report, and the phrase “high and lifted up” can certainly be used of proud men. But the context of Isaiah 2 is the point, not a counter-point: there the human “high and lifted up” is exactly what the LORD will humble, “and the LORD alone will be exalted in that day” (2:11, 17). That makes the pairing more striking, not less: the proud are brought low, yet the crushed Servant is exalted in the very words of the throne-vision. By itself the Servant’s exaltation in Isaiah does not make the Servant divine — Isaiah’s God exalts the humble. It is John who draws the further inference, assigning the glory of the throne-vision to Jesus. And the argument does not stand on the Hebrew echo in any case. Whatever John’s exact referent in 12:41, he sets Isaiah 53 and Isaiah 6 side by side and assigns the glory Isaiah saw to Jesus; he uses the Greek Servant’s two verbs, “lifted up” and “glorified,” for the cross; and the Philippians hymn and Revelation 5 make the same pairing of humiliation and divine worship without any appeal to Isaiah’s vocabulary at all. The verbal echo is corroboration. The convergence of witnesses from different streams of early Christianity is the case.

“If God cannot suffer, the cross cannot reveal God” — and its mirror image Hard

Strongest form

This objection comes from two opposite directions, and both deserve a full hearing.

From the side of critics of incarnational doctrine, the problem is coherence. The philosopher of religion John Hick, in the 1977 collection The Myth of God Incarnate, compared the bare assertion that the historical Jesus was also God to the assertion that a circle drawn in pencil on paper is also a square: without an explanation, he argued, the words have no content. On this view “God crucified” is not a profound paradox but a contradiction. Either the one on the cross is impassible God, in which case he did not truly suffer, or he truly suffered, in which case he is not impassible God.

From the other side comes a theological version of the same dilemma, pressed by Moltmann and many twentieth-century theologians after him. If the divine nature cannot suffer, they argue, then the cross reveals only a suffering man, and God remains a spectator. To let the cross reveal God, we must say that God suffers in his own being — that the cross is an event between the Father, who suffers the loss of the Son, and the Son, who suffers death. Moltmann wrote with Auschwitz in view, and his question deserves respect: whatever else the cross is, it is not a deity who answers Ivan Karamazov’s protest from a safe distance.

Reply

The classical reply, which this argument holds, refuses both horns, for neither allows the distinction the creeds draw: the one divine person truly suffers, in his human nature, while the divine nature as such does not. Cyril’s sentence puts it in a line: God the Word suffers in the body that is his own. That is not a contradiction of the circle-and-square kind, because it does not affirm and deny the same thing of the same subject in the same respect. It is a claim about one person with two natures, and philosophers such as Thomas V. Morris have argued at book length that the charge of formal incoherence does not go through. Whether such two-natures accounts fully succeed is itself debated among philosophers; the point here is that the charge of formal contradiction is contested, not obvious. The creeds fenced the mystery rather than dissolving it. But a mystery is not a contradiction.

Nor is the classical position the cold Greek import it is often said to be. Paul Gavrilyuk’s study of the Fathers argues that the old story of theology’s “fall” into Hellenistic philosophy misreads them: divine impassibility functioned for them as a qualifier marking God’s transcendence over creaturely passion, held together with a real suffering of the Son in the flesh. Thomas Weinandy, defending impassibility at length, argues that it is precisely the Son’s suffering as man that is redemptive. On this account the cross does reveal God — not because the divine nature was wounded, but because the one who freely bore the wounds is the Son himself, and what he chose to do discloses who he is. So we decline Moltmann’s step of locating suffering in the divine being itself, while honoring his question. The God of the classical creeds is not a spectator: in the person of the Son he entered our suffering and death for us.

“A crucified God is a scandal, not a revelation” Common

Strongest form

The oldest objection is the one Paul himself names: “we preach Christ crucified, a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles” (1 Corinthians 1:23). The historian Martin Hengel, in the standard study of the subject, gathered the ancient sources to show that crucifixion was what Josephus called “the most wretched of deaths,” reserved for slaves, violent criminals, and rebels against Rome, deliberately public and deliberately obscene. For a Jew the offense cut deeper, and touched God directly: “a hanged man is cursed by God” (Deuteronomy 21:23). So “crucified Messiah” was not merely an unhappy phrase; it was very nearly a contradiction in terms. If a crucified Messiah was nearly a contradiction, a crucified God would seem to be one outright.

Reply

Paul’s answer is not to soften the offense but to relocate wisdom. “For the foolishness of God is wiser than men, and the weakness of God is stronger than men” (1 Corinthians 1:25). The scandal is conceded as a scandal; what is denied is that our sense of what is fitting for God is the measure of God. This is not a license to believe anything: the claim must still be coherent (objection 3) and vindicated (the resurrection). And there is a historical point here, worth stating with its limits. Inventors do not sabotage their own inventions; the offensiveness of the cross explains why no one would fabricate it. It does not, by itself, show that the first Christians were right to see God revealed there; for that, a further event is needed — the vindication they claimed had occurred. The scandal clears the ground; it does not, alone, raise the dead. Our essay on the crucified Messiah develops that argument in full.

“The cry shows that Jesus died in despair” Trickier

Strongest form

The last objection takes the cry of dereliction at face value and draws the opposite conclusion. In the eighteenth century Hermann Samuel Reimarus, in the fragments published after his death by Lessing, read the cry as the words of a man whose hopes had collapsed: Jesus had expected God to establish his kingdom, and on the cross he found that God had not come to his aid. On this reading the cry is not a revelation of God but the most honest line in the Gospels, preserved because it could not be erased.

Reply

The reply grants part of this. The cry is raw, and Christians should not hurry past it; Luke and John do not record it, and Matthew keeps it. Its anguish is real, and on the orthodox account it is the real anguish of the Son in his human experience. But Reimarus’s reading requires us to set aside the narrative Mark actually wrote, in which Jesus three times predicts his death and says that the Son of Man came “to give his life as a ransom for many” (Mark 10:45), and in which the cry stands within five verses of the centurion’s confession. One may judge Mark’s framing to be theology rather than history; that is a further argument, and it rests partly on wider questions, including whether the resurrection happened. Grant, as many historians do, that the cry is authentic precisely because it was awkward. Its content still tells against despair of God: the dying man addresses God as “my God” in the opening line of Israel’s own lament — and whatever one concludes about whether that line evokes the psalm’s later turn to vindication (Psalm 22:24), a contested question, lament prayed to God is the speech of trust under anguish, not its abandonment. Whether that trust was vindicated — whether God did come to his aid — is, again, the resurrection question.

Touch the originals

Where this comes from — primary sources, not our summary

Mark 15:33–39 · Psalm 22the death where Jesus is first confessed by a human being as the Son of God
“And when the centurion, who stood facing him, saw that in this way he breathed his last, he said, ‘Truly this man was the Son of God!’” The first human being in Mark’s story to confess it is a Roman officer. The cry of Mark 15:34 opens Psalm 22, which turns to a God who “has not despised or abhorred the affliction of the afflicted, and he has not hidden his face from him, but has heard, when he cried to him” (22:24). Mark records the first verse, not the last.
Isaiah 6:1 · 52:13 · 57:15 · John 12:41the throne and the Servant
“The Lord sitting upon a throne, high and lifted up” (6:1); the Servant too shall be “high and lifted up” (52:13); the God who “inhabits eternity” dwells “with him who is of a contrite and lowly spirit” (57:15); Isaiah “saw his glory and spoke of him” — of Jesus (John 12:41). The exaltation of the crucified Servant is told in the language of YHWH’s own throne. Bauckham’s reading of the Isaiah echo is one strong reading, not a consensus; gezerâ shāvâ is his proposal, not an attested report.
Philippians 2:6–11 · Isaiah 45:23the downward path and the name
The hymn Paul quotes places “death on a cross” on the path to “the name that is above every name,” and applies to Jesus the text in which YHWH swears that to him alone every knee shall bow. Widely regarded as pre-Pauline (debated: Gordon Fee argues Paul composed it); the worship-of-Jesus datum stands either way.
Revelation 4–5; 22:1, 3the Lamb on the throne
“A Lamb standing, as though it had been slain,” the new song is sung to the Lamb precisely because of his death — “Worthy are you… for you were slain”; “To him who sits on the throne and to the Lamb be blessing and honor and glory and might forever and ever!”; “the throne of God and of the Lamb.” The angel twice refuses worship (19:10; 22:8–9); the Lamb receives it. The figure who shares the worship of the Creator is the slaughtered one.
Ignatius · Cyril · Constantinople II · Chalcedonthe grammar of one person in two natures
Ignatius: “Permit me to be an imitator of the passion of my God.” Cyril: “the Divine nature is incapable of suffering… but since that which had become his own body suffered in this way, he is also said to suffer for us.” Constantinople II (553): “crucified in the flesh is true God and the Lord of Glory and one of the Holy Trinity.” Chalcedon (451): “truly God and truly man… in two natures, inconfusedly, unchangeably, indivisibly, inseparably.”
Bauckham · Hurtado · McGrath · Dunn · Ehrman · Moltmann · Gavrilyuk · Weinandy · Brownthe state of scholarship
That the earliest Christians worshiped Jesus very early, within a Jewish monotheistic frame, is widely granted, though how to describe it is not: Bauckham’s “divine identity,” Hurtado’s “devotional pattern,” and McGrath’s agency model are live, competing accounts of the same data. The theological question of divine impassibility is vigorously debated: the passibilist view associated with Moltmann became common among theologians in the twentieth century, while a substantial recent literature, Gavrilyuk and Weinandy among it, has defended and retrieved the classical position. The interpretation of the cry of dereliction remains contested among New Testament scholars; Raymond Brown’s The Death of the Messiah surveys the range. Bauckham is cited as an exegete and historian on the divine-identity question only; his wider theological positions are not adopted. What does not depend on any of these disputes is the textual pattern itself: in Philippians, in John and in Revelation, the self-humbling and death of Jesus are told in the language of God’s own glory, throne and worship; and Mark makes that death the place where Jesus is first confessed by a human being as the Son of God.

    SK
    Skeptic
    “Look at how your own hymn ends: ‘to the glory of God the Father.’ That is a sign that this worship is offered through an agent. Jesus is the agent God honors, not God himself — so the cross is the story of a man God exalted, not a revelation of who God is.”
    One strong way to answer — in a 1 Peter 3:15 tone

    “The clause relates the Son to the Father within one act of worship; it does not reduce it to the honor paid an agent, for the text applied to Jesus is the one in which YHWH swears that this homage is his alone (Isaiah 45:23). Grant that God’s name and functions could be entrusted to a supreme agent, and the worship itself still could not be. (McGrath replies that the decisive line in Jewish worship was sacrifice, which Jesus does not receive; we think a shared doxology and the invocation Marana tha reach past that line, but the point is contested.) And the Lamb who shares the worship of the Creator in Revelation 5 is not an agent honored beside God; he is worshiped with him, in the same doxology — contrast the angels who refuse worship, Revelation 19:10; 22:9. And I would say it plainly: the agency reading does not account for this worship — an agent may bear God’s name, but not share God’s worship, and the crucified one does; so the objection that a crucified man cannot reveal God does not hold as a presumption — the Philippians hymn exalts to the name above every name the one who went to “death on a cross,” and in Revelation the slain Lamb is worshiped with the one on the throne. Whether the first Christians were right to read the cross this way depends on whether God vindicated the crucified one, which is the question the resurrection evidence is meant to answer.”
