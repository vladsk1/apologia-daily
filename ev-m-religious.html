The Argument from Religious Experience

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and locate exactly where its weight rests.

The argument in brief

Across every documented culture and century, enormous numbers of ordinary people report not merely belief but encounter — contact with something beyond the world and, in the theistic traditions, with a God who has addressed, comforted, convicted, or loved them. The argument from religious experience asks whether that vast body of testimony counts as evidence. Stated crudely — "people experience God, therefore God exists" — it is too quick, since people also report pink elephants. The serious versions are more disciplined: an apparent experience of God carries genuine, defeasible evidential weight, much as my apparent experience of a tree carries weight unless I have reason to think I am dreaming or drugged.

Its two sharpest modern defenses come from Oxford's Richard Swinburne and the American philosopher William Alston. Swinburne's Principle of Credulity says that if it seems to a subject that something is present, it probably is, absent special reason to doubt — the same default by which we run all of perceptual life, and one that is asymmetric (failing to experience God is not evidence of his absence). Alston argues that forming beliefs about God on the basis of apparent awareness of him is a doxastic practice — a socially established, self-monitoring way of forming beliefs, like sense perception, which likewise cannot be proven reliable without circularity yet is rational to trust.

In candor, the argument's honest version says exactly where it runs out. It is not a public proof that compels a determined skeptic, who can always invoke a defeater — neurology, projection, or the conflict of traditions — and the experiencer cannot hand the experience over for inspection the way one hands over a fossil. Above all, the diversity of the world's reports (an impersonal Brahman, no-self, a personal triune God) blocks the inference from experience alone to the Christian God in particular. What survives is more modest and more durable: for the person who has had such an experience, it can supply real, rational warrant; and the cross-cultural weight of such testimony is a genuine data point a complete worldview must explain rather than ignore. It is one strand in a cumulative case; which God is true rests on revelation — on Christ and Scripture — and on the historical case for the resurrection.

The argument, carefully

Three lines — and the first is a disciplined claim, not the crude one.

P1

Across cultures and centuries, enormous numbers of people report an apparent experience of God — not merely belief, but encounter.

P2

By the Principle of Credulity, an apparent experience is probably veridical — it carries real, defeasible weight — absent a special reason to doubt.

∴ C

So religious experience is genuine, defeasible evidence for God: real rational warrant for the experiencer, and a data point any worldview must explain — not a proof, and not by itself the Christian God.

This is deliberately modest. It does not claim to prove God the way a syllogism proves a conclusion, and it does not establish which God — the diversity of reports blocks that. Nearly every objection either attacks the credulity default (P2) or supplies a defeater (brain, projection, the conflict of traditions); spot which, and you are halfway to the answer.

Defending Premise 1

Across cultures and centuries, enormous numbers of people report an apparent experience of God — not merely belief, but encounter.

Precision check: P1 (the breadth of testimony) is barely contested as a fact; the fight is over P2. And P1 is not “people have experiences of God, therefore God exists” — stated that way it is plainly too quick, since people also have experiences of pink elephants. The scale of the phenomenon Barely contested as fact

This is not a handful of eccentrics. Across radically different societies, in every century, large numbers of ordinary people report contact with a transcendent reality. Across every documented culture, enormous numbers of ordinary people report not merely belief but encounter: contact with something beyond the world and, in the theistic traditions, with a God who has addressed, comforted, convicted, overwhelmed, or loved them.

Honest narrowing: breadth establishes that something widespread is being reported; it does not, by itself, tell you what was encountered. The scale makes the testimony a data point worth explaining; it does not settle the object.

William James: the reports as data Corroborating the data

The modern conversation begins with the Harvard psychologist and philosopher William James, whose Varieties of Religious Experience (the Gifford Lectures delivered at Edinburgh in 1901–1902 and published in 1902) took the reports seriously as data rather than dismissing them as pathology. James catalogued conversions, mystical raptures, and quiet assurances, and proposed that mystical states share four marks: they are ineffable (resistant to words), noetic (they feel like genuine knowledge, not just emotion), transient, and passive (they happen to the subject). The noetic mark matters most for our purposes: the experiencer does not feel he is having a mood. He feels he is learning something true.

One data point: Pascal’s Memorial Illustration

In the autumn of 1654, the mathematician Blaise Pascal sewed a scrap of parchment into the lining of his coat, and carried it there until he died. On it he had scrawled a date, an hour, and a single ecstatic word: Fire. “God of Abraham, God of Isaac, God of Jacob, not of the philosophers and scholars. Certitude, certitude, feeling, joy, peace.” Pascal’s “Memorial” is one data point in something vast.

Honest limit: Pascal’s parchment will not convince a skeptic, and it was never meant to. But it records something the skeptic must reckon with: that across the centuries, sober and brilliant people, in numbers beyond counting, have reported not that they reasoned their way to God but that he found them.

Objection — “People experience God, therefore God exists” is too quick

Stated crudely — “People have experiences of God, therefore God exists” — the argument is plainly too quick, since people also have experiences of pink elephants.

Reply

The serious versions are more disciplined. They do not claim that religious experience proves God the way a syllogism proves a conclusion. They claim that an apparent experience of God carries genuine evidential weight — a defeasible presumption in favor of its own truthfulness — much as my apparent experience of a tree in front of me carries weight unless I have reason to think I am dreaming or drugged. The argument asks: is it reasonable to treat all of that testimony as worthless by default? Or does it, like any large body of apparent perception, deserve a presumption of innocence?

The room: P1 is barely contested as a fact. The modern conversation begins with William James (The Varieties of Religious Experience, 1902), who took the reports as data; the dispute is over what the breadth of testimony shows (see Premise 2).
Defending Premise 2

By the Principle of Credulity, an apparent experience is probably veridical — it carries real, defeasible weight — absent a special reason to doubt.

The load-bearing premise, and where the fight is: whether an apparent experience of God earns the same default trust we give perception, and whether the defeaters override it. Swinburne’s formulation: “if it seems (epistemically) to a subject that x is present, then probably x is present—in the absence of special considerations” defeating that appearance. It claims defeasible weight, not proof — and it is asymmetric.

Swinburne: the default by which we run perceptual life The load-bearing premise

This is not a special pleading for religion. It is the principle by which we run ordinary perceptual life. If I treated every apparent perception as guilty until proven innocent, I could never get started — I would need independent evidence for the reliability of my eyes, but that evidence would itself come through my eyes. Rationality requires trusting appearances by default. Swinburne’s move is simply to apply the same default to apparent experiences of God: they too should be taken as veridical unless we have positive reason to doubt them.

Honest narrowing: whether the Principle of Credulity is too permissive is disputed.

Testimony — and the asymmetry Companion principle

Swinburne pairs the Principle of Credulity with a Principle of Testimony — that we should normally believe what others report — so that the experiences of others count too.

Honest limit: importantly, the Principle of Credulity is asymmetric: it says that if you seem to experience God, that counts for God; it does not say that if you fail to experience God, that counts against him.

Alston: Christian Mystical Practice as a doxastic practice Strong · contested

Alston’s central idea is the doxastic practice — a socially established, self-monitoring way of forming beliefs, complete with its own checks and overriders. Sense perception is one such practice; memory is another; reasoning from premises is another. None of these can be proven reliable without circularity, since any proof would have to use one of the practices. Yet it is rational to engage in them, because we have no independent court of appeal above our well-established practices. Christian Mystical Practice (CMP) — the practice of forming beliefs about God on the basis of apparent direct awareness of him — is, structurally, just another such practice. It is socially established, it has internal standards (a “spirit” that contradicts Scripture and the saints is suspect), and it cannot be shown to be unreliable without arguments that, applied evenhandedly, would also discredit sense perception.

Honest narrowing: whether religious experience counts as perception in a strict sense, rather than a different kind of cognition, is disputed. The parity defense invites the “proves too much” worry below; the debate is genuinely unsettled, and neither Swinburne nor Alston has won the field.

Objection — Alston’s parity proves too much

If every self-supporting doxastic practice is innocent until proven guilty, then astrology and crystal-gazing could claim the same shelter — so the parity argument proves too much and must be wrong.

Reply

Alston’s defenders answer that CMP, unlike astrology, has internal overriders and a coherent object: a spirit that contradicts Scripture and the saints is suspect, and the practice checks itself. Astrology, besides lacking a coherent object, has no morally transforming, self-correcting tradition; a mature religious practice is not epistemically on all fours with it, whatever verdict the wider debate reaches. That said, this is exactly a point critics press, and it is honest to say the debate continues and is genuinely unsettled — the reply blunts the parody without closing the question.

What this warrant is — and is not Scope

For the person who has had such an experience, it can supply real, rational warrant for belief — warrant a third party is not entitled to dismiss as obviously irrational — and the vast, cross-cultural weight of such testimony is a genuine data point that a complete worldview must explain rather than ignore.

Honest limit: it does not function as a public proof that should compel a determined skeptic, because the skeptic can always invoke a defeater — neurology, projection, or the conflict of traditions — and the experiencer cannot hand over the experience for inspection the way one hands over a fossil. It does not establish, on its own, that the Christian God in particular exists; the diversity of reports blocks that inference from experience alone. It is best understood not as a freestanding proof but as one strand in a cumulative case. Which God is true is not something experience alone yields; that rests on revelation — on Christ and Scripture — and on the historical case for the resurrection.

Objection — the conflict of traditions Hardest

The world’s traditions report experiences of different and incompatible objects. The Advaita Vedantin experiences an impersonal Brahman in which all distinction dissolves; the Theravada Buddhist experiences no self and no God at all; the Christian experiences a personal, loving, triune God who addresses her as “thou.” These cannot all be veridical descriptions of the same reality, because they contradict one another. A perceptual practice that systematically delivers incompatible outputs across its practitioners is exactly what an unreliable practice looks like. You cannot quarantine your own tradition’s reports from the discrediting force of the others without special pleading. The philosopher J. L. Mackie pressed versions of this, treating the diversity of religious experience as evidence that such experiences track culture and psychology rather than any divine object.

Reply

This is the strongest objection, and it limits the argument rather than destroying it. The reports conflict over what was encountered; what survives the diversity is only the thinner claim that many experiencers took themselves to meet something beyond the physical. That is a shared report, not a shared object: it does not suggest the traditions met the same reality under different names. Alston grants the problem and offers a frank, limited response: it does reduce the rational confidence an outsider can take from any one tradition’s reports, but it does not show that engaging in one’s own well-established practice is irrational, any more than the existence of rival scientific paradigms makes doing science irrational. John Hick took a bolder route, proposing that the traditions are culturally conditioned responses to a single ineffable “Real,” refracted through different conceptual lenses. Hick’s pluralism, though, comes at a steep cost: it requires saying that no tradition perceives the Real as it is, which most believers — and most experiencers — would reject as a description of what they take themselves to have met. It also cannot be squared with the Christian claim itself: that God has not stayed hidden behind every lens but has made himself known in Christ (John 1:18; 14:9). So the objection shows that religious experience cannot, by itself, settle which God is real — it under-determines theology. It does not show that there is nothing transcendent being encountered, and it leaves the experiencer’s own warrant largely intact while sharply reducing the experience’s force as public proof to an outsider. That is a real concession, and the argument is healthier for making it.

The room: the modern conversation begins with William James (The Varieties of Religious Experience, 1902), who took the reports as data; its sharpest contemporary defenses are Richard Swinburne’s Principle of Credulity (The Existence of God) and William Alston’s doxastic-practice epistemology (Perceiving God, 1991), widely regarded as a landmark. The critics are weighty: Sigmund Freud (projection), J. L. Mackie (the diversity objection), John Hick (a pluralist “Real” behind all traditions), the neuroscientist Andrew Newberg and the Canadian psychologist Michael Persinger. The philosophical defenses are taken seriously in the academy; neither side has won, and the contested points below are genuinely unsettled. The brain-chemistry and wish-fulfillment defeaters are answered in the objections card below.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Three objections deserve to be stated at full strength, not knocked down as straw — plus the epistemologists’ parity worry. The hardest of all is flagged below.

The one that matters most: the conflict of traditions — the world’s reports describe different and incompatible objects. The deep dive puts it at full strength and answers it honestly — take it with you, not just the reply below.

"It's just brain chemistry — a magnet can trigger it" Trickier

Strongest form

Religious experience has a known cause, and the cause is the brain, not God. Newberg's SPECT scans show a neural signature at the peak of contemplation; Persinger claimed a "God helmet" could induce a "sensed presence" with magnetic fields. If you can manufacture it with a magnet, it tells you nothing about a real God.

Reply

Two replies meet it. First, the logic: every experience has a neural correlate — seeing a tree lights up the visual cortex — so finding the brain-state of perceiving God no more debunks it than finding the brain-state of perceiving your mother. "It happens in the brain, therefore it is only the brain" is a reductionist fallacy. Second, the empirics: the God-helmet result, the most cited piece of hard debunking, failed independent replication — a 2005 Swedish double-blind team (Granqvist et al.) found the magnetic field had no relationship to reported experience; suggestibility predicted it instead, and the equipment came from Persinger's own lab. Newberg's findings are real but are correlations, which he himself declines to read as proof God is illusory.

"Different religions report contradictory experiences" Hard

Strongest form

The traditions report experiences of different and incompatible objects — an impersonal Brahman, no self and no God at all, a personal triune God. These cannot all be veridical. A perceptual practice that systematically delivers incompatible outputs is exactly what an unreliable practice looks like, and you cannot quarantine your own tradition's reports without special pleading. (Mackie pressed versions of this.)

Reply

This is the strongest objection, and the honest answer is that it limits the argument rather than destroying it. The reports conflict over what was encountered; what survives is only the thinner claim that many experiencers took themselves to meet something beyond the physical — a shared report, not a shared object. Alston grants it frankly: diversity reduces the confidence an outsider can take from any one tradition's reports, but it does not make engaging in one's own well-established practice irrational, any more than rival scientific paradigms make doing science irrational. So the objection shows religious experience cannot, by itself, settle which God is real; it does not show nothing transcendent is encountered, and it leaves the experiencer's own warrant largely intact while sharply reducing the experience's force as public proof.

"It's just wish-fulfillment" (Freud) Trickier

Strongest form

Freud argued religion is an "illusion" — a belief driven by wish rather than evidence. Faced with an indifferent nature, the helpless psyche projects a cosmic Father who promises protection and justice. Many religious experiences arrive exactly when people most want comfort — grief, fear, crisis — and that fit is suspicious.

Reply

Two replies temper it. First, pressed to a conclusion about truth, Freud's argument is a genetic fallacy: showing why someone wants a belief to be true tells us nothing about whether it is true — people who long for water in a desert sometimes long because there really is an oasis. Second, the projection thesis fits a great deal of religious experience badly, because so much of it is unwelcome: the conviction of sin, the terrifying holiness that flattened Isaiah ("Woe is me, for I am undone"), the call that wrecks a comfortable life. Freud's theory predicts a consoling Father; it strains to explain a God experienced as a consuming fire one would rather avoid. As a partial sociological account it has value; as a universal solvent it overreaches.

"Alston's parity proves too much" Hard

Strongest form

If every self-supporting doxastic practice is innocent until proven guilty, then astrology and crystal-gazing could claim the same shelter — so the parity argument proves too much and must be wrong.

Beyond that, astrology has no morally transforming, self-correcting tradition; a mature religious practice is not epistemically on all fours with it. The honest state of the scholarship

Where the argument really stands

Swinburne and Alston are major figures, and Perceiving God is widely regarded as a landmark — but neither has won the field. Sympathetic critics worry that Alston's parity argument proves too much (astrology might claim the same shelter); his defenders answer that Christian Mystical Practice has internal overriders and a coherent object, and the debate continues, genuinely unsettled. Whether religious experience is perception in a strict sense, and whether the Principle of Credulity is too permissive, are both disputed; almost no one denies the diversity objection has some force.

The real fork: whether an apparent experience of God earns the default trust we give perception, and whether the defeaters override it. It is fair to call the philosophical question contested, with no consensus in sight.

The bounded verdict: the argument does not establish, on its own, that the Christian God in particular exists — the diversity of reports blocks that inference from experience alone — and it is not a public proof that should compel a determined skeptic, who can always invoke a defeater and who cannot inspect the experience. What it does establish is more modest and more durable: for the person who has had such an experience, it can supply real, rational warrant a third party is not entitled to dismiss as obviously irrational; and the vast, cross-cultural weight of such testimony is a genuine data point a complete worldview must explain rather than ignore. It is best understood not as a freestanding proof but as one strand in a cumulative case, joined to the cosmological, design, and moral considerations. Which God is true is not something experience alone yields; that rests on revelation — on Christ and Scripture — and on the historical case for the resurrection.

    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    Granted, and the honest defender concedes it up front rather than being blindsided: this is not a public proof that compels, and the experiencer genuinely cannot hand the experience over for inspection the way one hands over a fossil. But two things follow, and neither rescues the dismissal. First, non-transferable warrant is still real warrant — you cannot hand me your perception of a tree either, yet it rationally grounds your belief, and a third party is not entitled to call you irrational for trusting it. Second, the sheer cross-cultural scale of the testimony is itself a public datum: not a proof, but a genuine data point a complete worldview must explain rather than wave away as worthless. And the argument never claimed more — it is one strand in a cumulative case, and which God is true rests on revelation and the historical case for the resurrection, not on experience carrying the whole weight alone.
