The Minimal Facts Argument

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and stay clear and calm in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

The minimal facts argument defends the resurrection of Jesus without first assuming the Bible is inspired. It begins from a small core of facts that the large majority of critical historians grant — sceptics included: Jesus died by Roman crucifixion; his disciples sincerely believed the risen Jesus appeared to them; the church persecutor Paul was converted by what he took to be such an appearance; and Jesus' sceptical brother James was similarly converted. The earliest of this evidence is not late legend but an early creed Paul cites in 1 Corinthians 15:3–7, which most scholars (including atheists such as Gerd Lüdemann) trace to within roughly two to five years of the crucifixion.

The argument then asks which explanation accounts for all these facts together. Shared bereavement visions do not explain Paul, a hostile persecutor, or James, a former sceptic; the stolen-body hypothesis cannot explain why the disciples would suffer for what they would know to be a fraud, nor why Paul would convert; and the legend hypothesis founders on chronology. The resurrection, by contrast, covers the whole set without ad hoc patching, so it is offered as the best historical explanation.

The honest scope: this is an inference to the best explanation, not a mathematical proof, and it does not by itself establish every Christian doctrine. It argues that, judged by ordinary historical criteria, the resurrection is the most reasonable account of facts even sceptics concede. History removes the historical excuse for unbelief, not the metaphysical one; the most natural explanation of those facts together — for anyone not antecedently committed to ruling miracles out — is that Jesus rose bodily from the dead.

The argument, formally

Build the case only from facts the majority of critical scholars grant.

P1

A core set of facts is granted by the majority of critical scholars, sceptics included: Jesus died by crucifixion; the disciples sincerely believed he appeared to them; the church persecutor Paul and the sceptic James were converted by what they took to be appearances.

P2

The resurrection explains all of these facts together; every naturalistic rival — hallucination, theft, legend — fails at least one of them.

∴ C

Therefore the bodily resurrection of Jesus is the best explanation of the agreed evidence.

This is an inference to the best explanation, not a proof from scripture — which is its power: it begins from what the other side already grants, established by ordinary historical criteria.

Then the key move — why these facts and not the whole New Testament?

The method deliberately uses only data points with the strongest evidence and the broadest scholarly acceptance. You concede nothing about inspiration or inerrancy; you simply ask which hypothesis best explains facts that sceptical historians themselves affirm.

Defending Premise 1

A core set of facts is granted by the majority of critical scholars, sceptics included: Jesus died by crucifixion; the disciples sincerely believed he appeared to them; the church persecutor Paul and the sceptic James were converted by what they took to be appearances

Precision check: a “minimal fact” is not simply a fact Habermas finds persuasive. It must satisfy two deliberately demanding tests: it must be strongly evidenced — typically by multiple independent lines of testimony — and it must be accepted by the great majority of scholars who study the subject, including the sceptical ones. In his stricter methodological statements Habermas has never counted the empty tomb as a Minimal Fact at all, since it does not command the near-unanimity the others do. What the sceptics concede is the starting line the argument reasons from, never the finish line it claims to have already reached.

Crucifixion: the bedrock Strong

That Jesus died by Roman crucifixion is attested by all four Gospels, by Paul, and — outside the Christian sources entirely — by the Jewish historian Josephus and the Roman historian Tacitus, who called the new movement a “pernicious superstition” and had no motive to flatter it. This is why even John Dominic Crossan, co-founder of the Jesus Seminar and one of the most sceptical figures in the field, can write that Jesus’ execution “is as sure as anything historical can ever be.” Habermas does not cite Crossan because Crossan is friendly; he cites him because Crossan is hostile, and the concession therefore counts.

The bedrock is not merely that Jesus was crucified but that he died. A 1986 study in the Journal of the American Medical Association reviewed the physiology and concluded that interpretations “based on the assumption that Jesus did not die on the cross … appear to be at odds with modern medical knowledge.” Josephus records that he recognised three of his own acquaintances among a group being crucified and successfully begged the Roman commander Titus for their release; they were taken down at once and given the best medical care available — and even so, two of the three died. And the arch-sceptic David Friedrich Strauss, writing in 1864, ridiculed the theory that Jesus survived the cross and revived in the tomb: a man who “had stolen half-dead out of the sepulchre,” who “crept about weak and ill,” could not possibly “have given to the disciples the impression that he was … the Prince of Life.”

Honest limit: The non-Christian sources do not all carry the same weight. Josephus and Tacitus state the execution outright (the Josephus notice carries later Christian embellishment, but its core reference to the crucifixion under Pilate is widely accepted as authentic); Lucian, Mara bar Serapion, and the Talmud presuppose or allude to it. The Mara bar Serapion letter never names Jesus, and the Talmud’s “hanged” is not simply Roman crucifixion; the identification in both is debated.

The earliest report: the creed Paul received Strong · early

If the crucifixion is the bedrock, the earliest report of what the first disciples proclaimed is the true foundation of the positive case — not a Gospel written decades later but a formula older than any of them, embedded in a letter that even the most sceptical scholars date to the early-to-mid 50s. Paul writes: “For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received, that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures, and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve” (1 Corinthians 15:3–5). The verbs “delivered” and “received” are the technical vocabulary of passing on fixed oral tradition, and the passage’s balanced clauses, its non-Pauline phrasing, and its Semitic features have persuaded scholars across the spectrum that Paul is here quoting a formula he did not compose but inherited.

Jesus was crucified around AD 30. Paul, by his own account, was converted within a few years of that, and tells the Galatians that three years after his conversion he went up to Jerusalem and stayed with Cephas (Peter), also seeing James the Lord’s brother. Whether Paul first received the creed at his conversion or on that Jerusalem visit, the tradition traces back to within roughly two to five years of the crucifixion — and its content back earlier still, to the community that coined it. The gap is far too narrow for a legend to fill.

Honest limit: John Kloppenborg’s analysis is representative in concluding that the core is a pre-Pauline formula, even while he is more cautious than some about confident claims for its precise Aramaic wording, its Jerusalem origin, and its very earliest dating. The intervals are approximate (“within a few years”), since the exact figures are debated.

What even the sceptics grant Strong · granted by critics

Gerd Lüdemann, an atheist New Testament scholar, wrote that “it may be taken as historically certain that Peter and the disciples had experiences after Jesus’ death in which Jesus appeared to them as the risen Christ.” E. P. Sanders: “That Jesus’ followers (and later Paul) had resurrection experiences is, in my judgment, a fact. What the reality was that gave rise to the experiences I do not know.” The Anglican scholar Reginald Fuller called the disciples’ Easter faith “one of the indisputable facts of history.” Even Bart Ehrman, no ally of the apologetic case, grants that “it is a historical fact that some of Jesus’ followers came to believe that he had been raised from the dead soon after his execution.”

Honest limit: Not one of these scholars is here conceding, as a matter of historical argument, that Jesus rose from the dead. Lüdemann, Sanders, and Ehrman each propose or leave open a naturalistic explanation of the experiences, and to enlist any of them as a witness to the resurrection itself would flatly misrepresent them. What they concede is narrower — that the earliest followers really did have experiences they took to be encounters with a living Jesus, and that the tradition reporting it is astonishingly early.

Paul and James Paul firm · James lower tier

Paul is arguably the single most valuable witness in the entire discussion. His testimony is early; it is written in his own hand rather than reported at second or third remove; and it comes from a former enemy of the movement he would later die proclaiming. When he lists the appearances in 1 Corinthians 15 he is naming men he had personally met, and his account of his Jerusalem visits in Galatians 1–2 shows a gospel he received independently of the Jerusalem apostles yet which they publicly endorsed — not a modern-style audit of anyone’s memory, but powerful evidence that the message Paul preached and the message of Peter and James were one and the same from the start. James’ family is remembered as thinking Jesus out of his mind, and James did not believe during the ministry.

Honest limit: In his 2010 study Licona counts only three facts as historical “bedrock” — the crucifixion, the disciples’ appearance-experiences, and Paul’s conversion — and relegates the conversion of James and the empty tomb to a lower tier of “second-order” facts. He sets James aside not because the datum is doubted — the scholars who grant it range from atheist to conservative — but only because too few specialists have written on it to establish the near-unanimity his own criterion demands.

Objection — the “consensus” is a head-count of the religiously committed

When Habermas says a fact is “accepted by the majority of scholars,” the historian Richard Carrier, among others, presses the count directly: which scholars, counted how? Habermas’s bibliography is not a randomised survey; it is a compilation of those who have chosen to publish on the resurrection — a population that may over-represent the religiously committed. A statistical critique published by Michael Alter and Darren Slade argued that the population of authors behind such tallies is heavily weighted toward clergy and seminary-linked writers, so that the consensus claims cannot be taken at face value.

Reply

This is a fair methodological warning and the figures should be handled with the corresponding caution. Habermas has answered on two fronts. First, the head-count was never what bears the weight: of his two criteria, he holds the demand for strong, multiply-attested evidence to be “by far the most crucial,” while the survey of scholarly opinion is only a secondary check that “may be mistaken or it could change.” Second, whatever bias his catalogue carries runs the opposite way from the one alleged: he deliberately over-collected the most radical writers, so that, by his own account, his tally leans toward the sceptical end rather than the devout. Neither point makes the underlying dataset transparent, and the call for an auditable count stands. And the strongest minimal facts do not depend on a contested headcount at all: that Jesus was crucified and that the disciples sincerely believed they had seen him alive are conceded by named sceptics — Crossan, Lüdemann, Ehrman — on the merits of the evidence, quite apart from any survey.

Objection — the “minimal” facts may not be minimal enough

Some items on the list smuggle in more than the consensus actually concedes. “The disciples believed they saw the risen Jesus” is nearly universally granted; “they saw group appearances,” as the creed reports, is granted by fewer; and “the tomb was empty” by fewer still.

Reply

This tiering is not merely a critic’s complaint; it is built into the method by its most rigorous practitioner. The honest version of the argument keeps these tiers distinct, building first on the genuinely common ground and only then arguing — separately and openly — for the more contested items. The empty tomb is precisely the fact Habermas flags as more disputed, and a careful presentation of the argument should treat it as the soft point in the chain rather than as a settled premise. The case is strongest when it leans on the facts the critics themselves grant, and weakest when it inflates “majority” into “virtually all.”

The room: Gary Habermas developed the approach over four decades and Michael Licona pressed it into its fullest scholarly form. On the foundational data there is genuine and broad agreement across the spectrum: that Jesus was crucified and that his followers had experiences they sincerely interpreted as encounters with the risen Christ. Carrier and Alter & Slade press the count. None of Lüdemann, Ehrman, or Dale Allison thinks the disciples were lying; the dispute is about the source of their experiences.
Defending Premise 2

The resurrection explains all of these facts together; every naturalistic rival — hallucination, theft, legend — fails at least one of them

Precision check: the argument is not a deduction. It is an inference to the best explanation, the same form of reasoning a historian or a detective uses: among the live hypotheses, which one accounts for the whole body of data with the most explanatory scope and power, the least strain, and the fewest unsupported assumptions? Its force is cumulative; no single fact, taken alone, forces the resurrection. Licona’s conclusion is stated with care: the resurrection is “the best historical explanation of the relevant historical bedrock” — the best explanation, reached by ordinary historical method, not a mathematical proof. It establishes that the most natural explanation of those facts together — for anyone not antecedently committed to ruling miracles out — is that Jesus rose bodily from the dead.

The method: weighing rivals Bounded · with an honest limit

Licona’s 2010 monograph borrows the criteria for adjudicating hypotheses from the philosophy of history — explanatory scope, explanatory power, plausibility, degree of being ad hoc, and illumination — and applies them as a working historian would to any contested event. A disciplined inference to the best explanation entertains all the live hypotheses and asks which accounts for the whole body of data with the most scope and the least strain — and which eliminates the causes that are simply not adequate to the effect. The argument is that each rival explanation can cover one or two facts but breaks on the others, while a single hypothesis — that Jesus actually rose — covers all of them at once.

Honest limit: Charles Sanders Peirce called this reasoning abduction, and did not hide its weakness: it “is a weak kind of argument,” one that “often inclines our judgment so slightly toward its conclusion that we cannot say that we believe the latter to be true; we only surmise that it may be so.” Stated crudely, the pattern is the textbook fallacy of affirming the consequent, because some other cause might produce the same facts. The comparative move blunts that worry without dissolving it entirely, since one can always ask whether the true explanation is one no one has yet proposed; what it delivers is the kind of verdict a jury delivers: not deductive certainty, but the best account of the evidence.

Hallucination Two features resist it

The strongest form of the visionary objection is not the private individual hallucination but the well-documented phenomenon of shared bereavement and religious visions — the kind Bart Ehrman presses, citing collective experiences such as Marian apparitions, where many people sincerely report seeing a figure together. That deserves a direct answer. Two features resist it: first, the appearances include people with no grief and no prior expectation — Paul, a hostile outsider actively persecuting the movement, and James, whose family is remembered as thinking Jesus out of his mind, and who did not believe during the ministry — whereas bereavement visions arise among those already devoted to and longing for the one they have lost; and second, no documented apparition tradition on record has produced a community that concluded a particular person had been bodily raised and then proved willing to suffer and die proclaiming it.

Theft and legend Each breaks on a different fact

The stolen-body hypothesis might explain an empty tomb, but it cannot explain why the disciples would suffer, and in documented cases die, for what they would then know to be a fraud, nor why Paul, attacking the movement from the outside, would convert. The legend hypothesis founders on chronology: the resurrection was being preached in Jerusalem within a very few years — on the early Christian account, within weeks — and the tradition itself traces to within a few years of the crucifixion, far too early for legend to have done its slow work. The core claim is anchored in a creed dated within two to five years of the cross.

Honest limit: An empty tomb by itself admits of theft or relocation; visionary experiences by themselves admit of hallucination. What the naturalistic alternatives struggle with is the conjunction. The empty tomb is the more disputed item in that conjunction, and the argument is strongest when it leans on the facts critics themselves grant.

Independent lines, and a classification of the rivals Disputed whether exhaustive

Timothy and Lydia McGrew have pressed this underlying structure furthest, treating the empty tomb, the appearances to the disciples, and the separate conversions of the outsider Paul and the sceptic James as causally independent lines — so that a single naturalistic mechanism (one shared hallucination, one stolen body, one growing legend) would have to misfire in each unrelated channel at once. From their case this page takes only that qualitative independence, not the probabilistic calculation they attach to it.

The philosopher Andrew Loke structures the case not as a list of the rivals someone happens to have proposed, but as a classification meant to cover every possibility: either no one actually claimed to have seen Jesus alive after his death (the reports are legend), or those who claimed it experienced nothing, or people had experiences caused only within their own minds, or experiences caused by something outside them that was not Jesus, or experiences caused by Jesus himself — whether he had survived the cross or had been bodily raised. If the categories really do exhaust the options, any new alternative must fall into one of them, and so must face whatever evidence tells against that category as a whole.

Honest limit: Whether the classification is truly exhaustive, and whether each branch is decisively closed, are themselves disputed; the framework sharpens the weighing of rivals rather than turning it into a proof.

Objection — Allison: probability is in the eye of the beholder Hardest

Dale Allison, one of the most respected New Testament scholars alive, grants much of the data. His objection cuts deeper than any single fact: the inference from the facts to the resurrection cannot be made independently of the worldview the investigator brings to it. “Probability,” he writes, “is in the eye of the beholder.” Whether the resurrection is the best explanation depends in part on one’s prior estimate of whether such a thing can happen at all — and that prior is not itself settled by the historical data.

Reply

This is the strongest objection in the literature, and it should be conceded as far as it goes. The minimal facts do not, by themselves, compel assent from someone whose background convictions assign a vanishingly small prior to any miracle. The honest reply is not to deny this but to reframe what the argument claims. It does not claim to coerce the committed naturalist. It claims that, if one is open to the possibility that God might raise the dead, the historical evidence then makes the resurrection the most reasonable explanation of an otherwise baffling set of facts. The argument removes the historical excuse for unbelief; it cannot remove the metaphysical one, and it should not pretend to. Allison himself judges the resurrection appearances and the empty tomb to be better evidenced than most sceptics allow — he simply will not take the last step on historical grounds alone. What stays his hand is a metaphysical prior, not a gap in the historical evidence.

The room: The naturalistic alternatives are still defended in the field. Gerd Lüdemann argued for visions rooted in guilt and grief; Bart Ehrman appeals to well-documented bereavement visions; Allison holds the question open. The minimal facts argument contends that no naturalistic mechanism yet proposed accounts for the whole set without straining badly somewhere; its critics contend that the resurrection hypothesis strains worse, because it requires a miracle. That is the real fault line, and it is a philosophical one as much as a historical one. The most candid statement of the limit comes from the other side: the process philosopher Charles Hartshorne, a non-Christian, granted that he could neither explain away the evidence Habermas pressed nor agree with Flew’s denial of it, before naming the real obstacle: “My metaphysical bias is against resurrections.”
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: Dale Allison’s — “probability is in the eye of the beholder”. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"Sincere belief proves experience, not resurrection" Common

Strongest form

Granted the disciples believed it — people sincerely believe false things all the time. Their conviction tells us about their psychology, not about an event.

Reply

Sincere people believe false things. But the experiences include Paul, a hostile persecutor, and James, a former sceptic, with no grief to project. Sincere belief alone proves little — the argument rests on what best explains all the granted facts together, and no naturalistic mechanism yet proposed covers the whole set without straining badly somewhere.

"Consensus is just Christian scholars agreeing" Common

Strongest form

Which scholars, counted how? Habermas’s bibliography is not a randomised survey; it is a compilation of those who have chosen to publish on the resurrection — a population that may over-represent the religiously committed. Richard Carrier presses the count directly, and a statistical critique by Michael Alter and Darren Slade (SHERM 3.2, 2021) argued that the population of authors behind such tallies is heavily weighted toward clergy and seminary-linked writers, so that the consensus claims cannot be taken at face value.

Reply

The facts are granted across the spectrum — by atheist, Jewish, and agnostic scholars: Lüdemann on the experiences, Crossan on the crucifixion, Sanders on the experiences as ‘a fact’. They grant them on the merits of the evidence, by ordinary historical criteria, not because of faith. Neither point makes the underlying dataset transparent, and the call for an auditable count stands.

"Extraordinary claims need extraordinary evidence" Hard

Strongest form

Even granting the facts, a miracle is always the least probable explanation. Any natural account, however strained, beats a supernatural one.

Reply

This is the strongest objection in the literature, and it should be conceded as far as it goes: the minimal facts do not, by themselves, compel assent from someone whose background convictions assign a vanishingly small prior to any miracle. But probability must also weigh explanatory power: a hypothesis that covers every fact cleanly can be more rational than a string of separate strained naturalisms, one per fact. The argument removes the historical excuse for unbelief; it cannot remove the metaphysical one, and it should not pretend to.

"Group hallucinations happen — think of Marian apparitions" Trickier

Strongest form

Crowds at Fatima reported shared visions. Why not the disciples — grief-stricken, expectant, primed to see their teacher?

Reply

The disciples were not expectant — Jewish hope was a general resurrection at the end of history, not a crucified messiah mid-history; the texts show flight and despair. Mass events like Fatima involve ambiguous lights interpreted variously, and no documented apparition tradition on record has produced a community that concluded a particular person had been bodily raised and then proved willing to suffer and die proclaiming it. Nor does any of it explain Paul or James.

"The facts come from biased sources" Trickier

Strongest form

Everything traces back to the New Testament — documents written by believers to make converts. You cannot build history from propaganda.

Reply

Historians use interested sources constantly — nearly every ancient source has a viewpoint; the tools (early dating, multiple attestation, embarrassment) exist precisely to test them. By those tools these facts pass, which is why non-Christian scholars grant them. And Tacitus and Josephus corroborate the frame from outside.

"Best explanation is subjective" Hard

Strongest form

One person finds resurrection the best explanation, another finds any natural story better. There is no neutral way to score explanations.

Reply

Historians score explanations every day by public criteria: explanatory scope, explanatory power, plausibility, lack of ad-hoc devices. The resurrection covers all the facts without patches; the rivals each need auxiliary hypotheses stacked on auxiliary hypotheses. That comparison is an argument you can have — not a shrug — though the last step, from “best historical explanation” to “and therefore God acted,” is one the evidence invites but cannot compel.

The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

On the foundational data, there is genuine and broad agreement: across the spectrum, scholars accept that Jesus was crucified and that his followers had experiences they sincerely interpreted as encounters with the risen Christ. That much is not seriously contested. What is contested is everything downstream — the cause of those experiences, the historicity of the empty tomb, and above all whether history can license the supernatural conclusion at all. The minimal facts argument contends that no naturalistic mechanism yet proposed accounts for the whole set without straining badly somewhere; its critics contend that the resurrection hypothesis strains worse, because it requires a miracle. That is the real fault line, and it is a philosophical one as much as a historical one.

A fairness most defenders skip: "The disciples believed they saw the risen Jesus" is nearly universally granted; "they saw group appearances," as the creed reports, is granted by fewer; and "the tomb was empty" by fewer still. This tiering is not merely a critic's complaint; it is built into the method by its most rigorous practitioner. Licona counts only three facts as historical "bedrock" and relegates the conversion of James and the empty tomb to a lower tier of "second-order" facts. Tellingly, he sets James aside not because the datum is doubted — the scholars who grant it range from atheist to conservative — but only because too few specialists have written on it to establish the near-unanimity his own criterion demands. And Habermas has never counted the empty tomb as a Minimal Fact at all. The honest version of the argument keeps these tiers distinct. Where presenters blur the tiers, treating every fact as equally bulletproof, they hand the critic an easy and deserved rebuttal.

The bounded verdict: The minimal facts argument establishes that a small set of facts about the death and aftermath of Jesus is firmly grounded and broadly conceded, and that the most natural explanation of those facts together — for anyone not antecedently committed to ruling miracles out — is that Jesus rose bodily from the dead. It does not prove the resurrection in the way a theorem is proved; it makes it the best explanation, which is the most any historical argument about any event can claim. Its power is that it begins where even the critic stands; its limit is that the last step, from "best historical explanation" to "and therefore God acted," is one the evidence invites but cannot compel.

    Drill 3 · The common objection (worked example)

    Hold the line on extraordinary claims

    Start here: draft your reply, then reveal a model answer to compare. This is the worked example — Drill 4 then makes you do it cold across the whole objection set.

    SK
    Skeptic
    "Extraordinary claims require extraordinary evidence. However you arrange the facts, a miracle is always less likely than any natural explanation."
    One strong way to answer — in a 1 Peter 3:15 tone

    "That slogan sounds neutral, but notice what it builds in — a prior against miracles, so a resurrection starts at near-zero. That is a starting assumption, not evidence. Here is the actual situation: facts granted by sceptical scholars — the crucifixion, the disciples’ sincere experiences, the conversions of Paul and of James — each need explaining. Shared bereavement visions do not explain Paul, a hostile persecutor, or James, a former sceptic; legend cannot outrun a creed dated within five years. One hypothesis covers everything without patches. History removes the historical excuse for unbelief, not the metaphysical one; the most natural explanation of those facts together — for anyone not antecedently committed to ruling miracles out — is that Jesus rose bodily from the dead."

    BE
    Believer
    "If we only argue from what sceptics grant, aren't we admitting the Gospels can't really be trusted?"
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    No. The approach is an apologetic strategy, not a verdict on the trustworthiness of Scripture. In Habermas's words it is "not a prescription for how a given text should be approached … or how a sermon is written," and its deliberate restriction to what critics grant "should never be concluded" to indicate "a lack of trust in Scripture … Nor should it cause others to question or doubt their beliefs." The minimal-facts case is a bridge built to be crossed from the sceptic's side; the believer who already trusts the Gospels has simply been handed one more reason to do so — a corroboration, not a new foundation.
