The Argument from Undesigned Coincidences

The deep dive tells you what the argument is. This track makes you able to use it — reconstruct it from memory, answer its hardest objections, and hold the line in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

The argument from undesigned coincidences points to small, unplanned ways the Gospels and Acts interlock. One account will mention an offhand detail that, without intending to, answers a puzzle left open by another — like a witness who casually fills a gap a different witness never thought to explain. A classic example: John reports that Jesus asked Philip where to buy bread for the crowd, and only Luke separately notes that this miracle happened near Bethsaida, Philip's hometown — the natural person to ask. Neither writer flags the connection; it simply fits. When accounts dovetail this way with no sign of contrivance, the most natural explanation is that both report real events that the writers actually knew.

The conclusion drawn is that these documents are best explained as well-informed, substantially independent testimony to real events, rather than late legend or coordinated invention — which tends to harmonize on the surface but rarely interlocks at this unplanned level of detail.

The honest scope: the argument was developed by William Paley (1790) and J.J. Blunt (1847) and revived more recently by Lydia McGrew, and it remains relatively under-engaged in mainstream critical scholarship. It is best treated as a cumulative, probabilistic line of evidence for reliability — real but contested evidence, not a settled result or a single knockdown proof. Whether what that testimony reports is true — up to and including Easter morning — is argued on its own ground, in the historical case for the resurrection.

Understand it

Not more prose — the load-bearing structure. See the skeleton, then learn to defend each bone.

The argument, formally

Three lines. The whole weight rests on the two premises.

P1

When two accounts interlock — one casually answering a question the other leaves open, with no sign of contrivance — the best explanation is that both report real events that the writers actually knew.

P2

The Gospels and Acts contain many such undesigned coincidences: offhand details in one account that unintentionally explain puzzles in another.

∴ C

Therefore, these documents are best explained as well-informed, substantially independent testimony to real events, not as late legend or coordinated invention.

This is an inference to the best explanation, not a deductive proof — so its force is cumulative. Skeptics grant P1 (it's how we test ordinary witnesses); the live question is whether the cases in P2 are genuine interlocks or just coincidences read in after the fact.

Then the conceptual step — why is "undesigned" the key word?

A forger smooths the seams; an honest witness leaves loose threads that only another witness ties off. The fit is too casual to be planted, yet too neat to be chance — exactly the signature of substantially independent testimony to the same reality.

Defending Premise 1

When two accounts interlock — one casually answering a question the other leaves open, with no sign of contrivance — the best explanation is that both report real events that the writers actually knew

Precision check: an undesigned coincidence is a particular shape of fit between two texts. One account mentions a detail that, on its own, is puzzling or incomplete; a second account, written for unrelated purposes and showing no awareness of the first’s difficulty, contains an offhand detail that resolves it. The fit is too specific to be likely chance, yet too casual and unflagged to look planted. Two features of the structure must be marked at the outset. First, it is an inference to the best explanation, not a deductive proof: the cases raise the probability of reliability, they do not establish it with certainty. Second, the force is cumulative: no single coincidence is meant to carry weight; the argument lives or dies on the pattern.

How real witnesses behave The core warrant

The first premise is the part skeptics tend to grant, because it is simply how we evaluate testimony everywhere else. Detectives, historians, and courts routinely treat accounts that agree at incidental, uncoordinated points as more credible than accounts that agree only on the headline. The reason is probabilistic. If two witnesses to a car accident independently mention, without prompting, that the streetlight was out—one because it explains why she did not see the other car, the other because he tripped on the curb in the dark—the convergence is striking precisely because neither was trying to corroborate the other. Coordinated liars produce agreement on the main story; what they rarely produce is a web of small, mutually explanatory details that none of them flagged.

Honest limit: the contemporary defender Lydia McGrew frames this explicitly in the language of probability: each coincidence is a piece of evidence whose likelihood is far higher if the sources are well-informed and independent than if they are not. No premise guarantees its conclusion; the cases raise the probability of reliability, they do not establish it with certainty.

What fabrication tends to look like Strong

A single author building a narrative, or a community polishing a legend, works from one storyline and smooths the seams; loose ends get tied off or dropped. What is hard to manufacture is a detail in document A that quietly answers a question raised—and left unanswered—in document B, with neither author appearing to notice. That requires either genuine knowledge of the underlying facts or a feat of cross-document coordination far more elaborate than the simpler hypothesis that the events happened and the writers knew about them. So the probability of the interlocking pattern is high if the reports are well-informed and substantially independent, and low if they are invented or derived from a single tidy source. That likelihood gap is the whole engine of the argument.

A distinguished pedigree Historical background

The argument’s first systematic exponent was William Paley, in Horae Paulinae (1790), who noticed that Paul’s letters and the narrative of Acts repeatedly confirm one another at small, oblique points that no forger would have thought to coordinate. The Cambridge divine John James Blunt, half a century later, extended the method across the whole Bible in his Undesigned Coincidences in the Writings Both of the Old and New Testament; for Blunt these incidental agreements were “an argument of their veracity”—internal marks that the writers told the truth. Its modern revival is largely the work of the philosophers Timothy and Lydia McGrew, above all Lydia McGrew’s Hidden in Plain View (2017), which sharpened the case with explicit probabilistic reasoning and direct engagement with the objection from literary dependence.

Honest limit: the argument went quiet for over a century, neglected as biblical scholarship turned to source and form criticism, and it is not, at present, a front-line debate in academic New Testament studies. The mainstream guild has largely not engaged the revived argument, and a fair observer should treat that silence as neither endorsement nor refutation.

Objection — “Later writers just copied and harmonized the earlier ones”

The argument quietly assumes the texts are independent, when the dominant view in New Testament studies is that they are not. On the standard two-source hypothesis, Mark was written first, and Matthew and Luke both used Mark (and a shared sayings source). If that is right, then a “coincidence” between Mark and Luke may be nothing more mysterious than Luke copying and lightly editing his source. Apparent dovetailing is an artifact of redaction: a later author adjusting, expanding, or recombining material he inherited, which will naturally produce details that “fit” because they descend from a common text.

Reply

The honest concession is large: if a coincidence holds only between texts in a known dependence relation—especially Mark and a Gospel that used Mark—then redaction is a live alternative explanation, and such cases carry less weight. McGrew herself accepts this discipline; she treats different cases as having different strengths, and even sets aside examples she judges too weak to lean on. A defender cannot simply wave away Markan priority, the majority view. The stronger reply is more targeted: the objection does not reach the strongest cases. When a later author notices a connection in his source, the natural editorial move is to state it—to clarify, to smooth, to make the link explicit for the reader. But the strong undesigned coincidences do the opposite: the explaining detail sits in a different document, dropped for an unrelated reason, with neither writer flagging the link. A copyist who spotted that Philip was from Bethsaida and that the feeding happened near Bethsaida would have written, “Jesus asked Philip, because Philip was a local.” The fact that no Gospel says this—that the reader has to assemble it—is precisely what redaction does not predict. Second, the strongest cross-Gospel cases (like Philip/Bethsaida) draw the explaining detail from a Gospel not in the relevant source relation, so simple copying does not account for them.

The room: Paley and Blunt built the argument; Timothy and Lydia McGrew revived it, with endorsements from scholars such as Craig Keener and the apologist J. Warner Wallace. Richard Carrier, the most prominent popular critic, presses literary dependence. McGrew’s most rigorous statement of the underlying probability is an article in the philosophy journal Erkenntnis, “Undesigned Coincidences and Coherence for an Hypothesis”—a treatment of the formal logic of varied, dovetailing evidence rather than a verse-by-verse defense.
Defending Premise 2

The Gospels and Acts contain many such undesigned coincidences: offhand details in one account that unintentionally explain puzzles in another

The strength of Premise 2 is in the examples, and the honest way to present them is to show the range—including which kinds are strong and which are weaker. After the appropriate concessions to literary dependence and selection bias, the case rests most securely on the Acts–Epistles material and the best-shaped cross-Gospel cases, not on every example ever paraded.

Philip and Bethsaida Among the cleanest

According to John’s Gospel, Jesus turns to one disciple in particular at the feeding of the crowd: “Where are we to buy bread, so that these people may eat?” Of the twelve, why Philip? John does not say. But Luke, telling the same story, drops a geographical note that John omits: the feeding took place near the town of Bethsaida. And John, far earlier and for an entirely unrelated reason, mentions in passing that “Philip was from Bethsaida, the city of Andrew and Peter.” Lay the three details side by side and the puzzle dissolves: Jesus asked Philip because they were near Philip’s hometown, and Philip would know the local bakeries and markets. No single Gospel makes that point. The case is among the cleanest because the explaining detail sits in a completely different part of John, planted for an unrelated narrative reason chapters earlier, while the geographical anchor comes from a different Gospel altogether.

The “green grass” Hold loosely

Mark alone notes that Jesus had the crowd sit down “on the green grass.” Around the Sea of Galilee the hills are brown and parched for most of the year; the grass is green only briefly, after the winter rains, in spring. And John alone, in his account of the same event, mentions that “the Passover, the feast of the Jews, was at hand”—and Passover falls in spring. Mark says nothing about the season; John says nothing about the grass; together they cohere.

Honest limit: this particular case is also where the literary-dependence objection bites hardest. If John knew Mark, he could simply have read Mark’s “green grass,” inferred the rainy spring season, and added his own note that Passover was near; the green-grass case should be conceded as weak if John used Mark, and dropped from the load-bearing cases.

Herod’s court Across the Gospels and into Acts

Luke records an odd episode in which some Pharisees warn Jesus that Herod Antipas wants to kill him, and Jesus replies, “Go and tell that fox…” How would Jesus’ circle have such specific intelligence about the tetrarch’s intentions and court? Luke himself supplies the thread elsewhere, again incidentally: among the women supporting Jesus was “Joanna, the wife of Chuza, Herod’s household manager.” And the book of Acts, listing the leaders of the church at Antioch, names “Manaen, who had been brought up with Herod the tetrarch.” The movement had inside connections to Herod’s household—which is exactly why it could report what went on there. None of the three texts points out the link; each was written for its own purpose. Blunt’s classic version of this cluster runs through Matthew’s remark that Herod spoke about Jesus “to his servants” in Matthew 14:2, paired with Luke’s Joanna.

The Acts–Epistles collection for Jerusalem Strong · contested

Paley’s original domain, which many defenders regard as the strongest of all. The traditional reasoning is that the interlocks there cannot be explained as one document tidying another, because the genuine letters were certainly not written with Acts in view. Take the collection for Jerusalem. Paul’s letters of this period are preoccupied with it—a relief fund he is gathering from the Gentile churches for the poor believers in Jerusalem (Romans 15:25–28; 1 Corinthians 16:1–4; 2 Corinthians 8–9), to be carried by delegates the churches themselves would accredit. In Acts this vast concern is almost invisible—yet as Paul turns toward Jerusalem, Acts names, without explaining why, a company travelling with him: Sopater of Berea, Aristarchus and Secundus of Thessalonica, Gaius of Derbe, Timothy, and the Asians Tychicus and Trophimus (Acts 20:4). Acts gives the roster; the letters give the reason; neither points to the other. The places where the fit is imperfect are as telling as where it holds: the party adds two men from Asia, which the letters never enroll in the collection, and includes no one from Achaia, whose gift the letters press hardest. A harmoniser stitching Acts to the letters would have matched the provinces cleanly and supplied the missing Corinthian; the actual list, loose in both directions, reads like a separate record of the same enterprise.

Honest limit: the “Acts is independent of the letters” premise can no longer be presented as settled. A significant revisionist line—Richard Pervo’s Dating Acts (2006) and the work of Joseph Tyson and the Acts Seminar—dates Acts to the early second century and argues that its author knew and mined the Pauline letters as a source. Even granting the contest, the Acts–Epistles cases remain among the argument’s strongest—not because dependence is impossible, but because the shape of these interlocks is not what literary borrowing predicts. Paley’s own version also leaned on a fainter trace, Acts 24:17, “to bring alms to my nation”—possibly a glance at the collection, though it reads most naturally as aid to the Jewish people; the case does not rest on that verse.

Objection — Carrier: there are no undesigned coincidences, and the Acts–Epistles tier is not safe Hardest

The most forceful objection comes from the mythicist historian Richard Carrier, whose essay is titled “There Are No Undesigned Coincidences: The Bible’s Authors Are Simply Changing Up Their Sources.” He does not grant that the Acts–Epistles cases are the safe tier and then quarrel only about the Gospels. He argues the reverse. The only coincidences with any chance of being undesigned, he writes, are the “internal” ones—Gospel to Gospel—and even those he attributes to shared sources; the Acts–Epistles correspondences he dismisses outright, on the ground that, as he puts it, “Obviously the author of Acts used those Epistles as a source text.” He even names the Philip-and-Bethsaida case specifically—reading it not as two independent witnesses but as John inventing Philip’s Bethsaida origin to fit the location he found in Mark and Luke. Stated this way, on Carrier’s telling there is no tier the dependence move cannot reach.

Reply

The reply must be made on the merits rather than by waving the dependence question away. Grant Carrier his premise for the sake of argument: suppose the author of Acts had read Paul’s letters. The trouble is that Acts does not behave like a document built out of them. It never verbatim-quotes the letters—not once—and at the points where a borrower would most want to lean on his source, Acts diverges from it. The sharpest example is the very one critics press as a contradiction: Galatians has Paul insist that after his conversion he did not go up to Jerusalem but went away into Arabia (Gal 1:17), while Acts narrates a prompt visit to Jerusalem and a different sequence of trips. A writer mining Galatians to manufacture verisimilitude would have had reason to smooth exactly this; this tells against borrowing. The point should not be overstated, however: a redactor with a theological agenda can diverge from a source deliberately, so the divergences are suggestive rather than decisive. The real weight falls on the shape of the agreements: the points where Acts and the letters do converge have the offhand, question-and-answer character of genuine interlocks, which is not what deliberate harmonization produces. Harmonizers connect the dots; these texts leave them unconnected. So the Acts–Epistles material survives Carrier’s objection not because dependence has been ruled out a priori—it has not—but because the pattern fits independent knowledge better than it fits a redactor reworking the letters.

The same answer disposes of Carrier’s reading of Philip/Bethsaida. John’s own feeding scene never names Bethsaida at all (John 6:1 places it only on “the other side of the Sea of Galilee”), so the location Carrier says John was fitting appears nowhere in John’s account; and the load-bearing detail—that Philip was “from Bethsaida”—sits in John 1, five chapters earlier, inside a disciple-list, planted for the unrelated purpose of introducing Andrew and Peter, with no narrative signal pointing forward to the feeding at all. That is the opposite of how invention for effect works: an inventor cashes in his invention; he does not bury the one detail that makes it pay in a context where no reader would think to look for it. Carrier’s alternative is available in principle, but it is a far less economical hypothesis than that John simply knew Philip was from Bethsaida. On this case, Carrier’s reading does not hold.

Objection — “These are just coincidences you’re reading in after the fact”

Search any two long, overlapping texts for details in one that “explain” details in the other, and you will find some by sheer chance—the human mind is a relentless pattern-finder. Defenders parade the hits and never count the misses: they do not tabulate how many puzzles in Gospel A find no answer in Gospel B, nor how often the accounts flatly conflict. Without a denominator, a pile of impressive-looking fits tells us little.

Reply

The selection-bias objection calls for a real concession: defenders have not, on the whole, done the rigorous work of counting misses, and the argument would be stronger if they had. The probabilistic framing helps but does not fully answer it. What can be said is twofold. First, the genuine cases are not mere “fits” but question-and-answer shaped—one text raises a specific oddity (why Philip?) that it never resolves, and another casually resolves it. That asymmetric structure is considerably rarer than a loose thematic resemblance, and harder to generate by chance trawling. Second, the claim is not that one fit is decisive but that the number and specificity of well-shaped cases, concentrated in documents purporting to report the same events, is what an honest-and-informed hypothesis predicts and a fabrication hypothesis does not. Still, the candid verdict is that quantifying the pattern remains the argument’s least developed flank. That is a gap in the defense’s bookkeeping, not a defeater: a missing count does not turn question-and-answer interlocks into chance fits.

The room: Lydia McGrew defends the argument foremost; Carrier is its most prominent popular critic, pressing literary dependence from the standard two-source hypothesis. On the Acts–Epistles tier, Pervo, Tyson and the Acts Seminar date Acts to the early second century and argue its author mined the letters; the major Pauline letters (Romans, 1–2 Corinthians, Galatians) are today accepted as Paul’s by the great majority of critical scholars. The live, unsettled questions are how much the consensus of literary dependence erodes the cross-Gospel cases, and whether the pattern survives a properly disciplined accounting of hits and misses; it should be presented as real but contested evidence, not as a settled result. It does not prove the Gospels inerrant and does not, by itself, establish that any miracle occurred.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

This is what separates someone who knows the argument from someone who can defend it. Each gets its best form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: Carrier’s — the Acts–Epistles tier is not safe, and defenders never count the misses. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"These are just coincidences you're reading in after the fact" Common

Strongest form

Hunt through any long text for "fits" and you'll find some by chance. Apologists are pattern-matching — confirmation bias dressed up as evidence.

Reply

Defenders have not, on the whole, done the rigorous work of counting misses, and the argument would be stronger if they had. But two things guard against the charge. First, the genuine cases are question-and-answer shaped: one text raises an oddity it never explains, the other casually resolves it without noticing. That asymmetry is hard to manufacture by reading-in. Second, the argument is explicitly cumulative — no single case is meant to carry it. A handful might be chance; dozens running the same direction is exactly the pattern an honest-and-informed hypothesis predicts and a fabrication hypothesis does not.

"Later writers just copied and harmonized the earlier ones" Trickier

Strongest form

If Matthew and Luke used Mark (and a shared source), the "fits" are just literary borrowing — a later author tidying the earlier one, not independent memory.

Reply

Where texts are in a known dependence relation, redaction is a live alternative and those cases carry less weight — the green-grass case among them. But copying produces the opposite of what we see. A copyist who noticed Philip's hometown would have stated the connection, not left it implicit. In the real cases the explaining detail sits in a different document, dropped for an unrelated reason, with neither writer aware he's solving the other's puzzle. And the strongest cross-Gospel cases, like Philip and Bethsaida, draw the explaining detail from a Gospel not in the relevant source relation. Where critics say Acts mined Paul's letters, Acts never verbatim-quotes them and diverges rather than smoothing toward them — dependence is not ruled out, but the pattern fits independent knowledge better.

"Even granting truthful reporting, that doesn't get you miracles" Trickier

Strongest form

Suppose the writers are honest and well-informed about ordinary details. Honest reporters still get extraordinary claims wrong. The coincidences only touch the mundane parts.

Reply

Granted — and the argument's claim is precise. It makes one thesis harder to maintain: that the Gospels are detached, late, free-floating legend. That is corroboration, not proof. Whether what that testimony reports is true — up to and including Easter morning — is argued on its own ground, in the historical case for the resurrection.

"The examples are cherry-picked and ignore the contradictions" Hard

Strongest form

For every tidy coincidence there's an apparent contradiction between the Gospels. Parading the fits while burying the discrepancies is selective and misleading.

Reply

The two phenomena actually reinforce each other. The presence of unresolved surface tensions is itself evidence the writers weren't colluding — collaborators harmonize. So you have apparent independence (the tensions) plus deep agreement at unplanned points (the coincidences): precisely the profile of multiple genuine witnesses to one set of events. Many alleged contradictions also dissolve on closer reading, but even granting some, they strengthen rather than undercut the case for independence.

"A clever single author could plant all of these on purpose" Hard

Strongest form

A skilled novelist seeds details in chapter two that pay off in chapter twenty. Why couldn't one author (or a coordinating school) deliberately scatter these "undesigned" hooks for verisimilitude?

Reply

Then they'd appear within one work, where an author controls both ends. But the strongest cases span separate documents by different writers — the unexplained detail in one, the explanation in another — with no editorial signpost connecting them. To plant those deliberately, each author would need to anticipate exactly which gaps the others would leave and salt his text accordingly, across documents not written as a set. That's a far less probable hypothesis than ordinary witnesses who simply knew the facts.
