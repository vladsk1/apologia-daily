Archaeological Confirmation of the Bible

The deep dive tells you what the argument is. This track makes you able to use it — reconstruct it from memory, answer its hardest objections, and hold the line in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

Does archaeology confirm the Bible? The argument is that when a document's checkable claims — places, persons, titles, customs — are repeatedly verified by independent excavation, it earns a strong presumption of reliability, including in the parts we cannot directly test. This is the same yardstick historians use for any ancient source, not a special pleading for Scripture. The biblical narratives have passed this test again and again, especially from the monarchy onward, with striking specificity.

The confirmations are concrete. The Tel Dan Stele names the "House of David"; the Pilate Stone names Pontius Pilate as a Roman official in Judea; seals and bullae tie to named figures like Hezekiah. The Hittites, once doubted, were unearthed as a major empire, and Luke in Acts repeatedly names real officials with their correct, changing titles. These are risky, incidental details a distant legend-writer would likely botch — the texture of careful reporting.

The honest scope: archaeology cannot excavate a miracle, so getting the setting right does not by itself prove the supernatural claims. There are also genuine scholarly disputes over interpretation and dating (Jericho, the Exodus). What the evidence does establish is that these authors were well-informed reporters of their world, dismantling the "late legend" theory and earning the texts a serious historical hearing. Whether what the biblical authors wrote is true turns above all on the resurrection.

The argument, formally

Three lines. The whole weight rests on the two premises.

P1

A document whose checkable claims — places, persons, customs, political details — are repeatedly confirmed by independent archaeology has earned a strong presumption of reliability.

P2

The biblical narratives have had their checkable claims confirmed by archaeology again and again, especially from the monarchy onward, with remarkable specificity.

∴ C

Therefore, the biblical narratives have earned a presumption of historical reliability, including in the parts we cannot directly test.

The logic is a straightforward inductive inference — if both premises hold, the conclusion follows with high probability. Skeptics rarely deny P1 (we use it for all ancient texts); the real fight is over P2 and how much the confirmations actually carry.

Then the conceptual step — what kind of text are we dealing with?

A text that gets the incidental, falsifiable background right — the kind of detail a later legend-writer would botch — is behaving like reportage, not myth. That shapes how we should read even its claims we cannot dig up.

What it gives is a credible historical stage on which the further questions — how faithfully the text was transmitted, how early it was written, and whether what its authors wrote is true, which turns above all on the resurrection — can be argued on their own merits.
Defending Premise 1

A document whose checkable claims — places, persons, customs, political details — are repeatedly confirmed by independent archaeology has earned a strong presumption of reliability

Precision check: archaeology cannot excavate a miracle. No trowel will ever turn up the resurrection, the parting of the sea, or the voice from Sinai; those are not the kind of thing that leaves a stratum. What archaeology can do is test the Bible’s checkable claims—its places, persons, political titles, and customs—against independent physical evidence. This is an inductive inference, not a deductive proof; the conclusion follows with high probability if both premises hold.

The standard we already use The core warrant

The principle is not special pleading invented for Scripture. It is the ordinary yardstick historians apply to every ancient text. When a document’s incidental, verifiable details repeatedly check out, it earns a presumption of reliability in the parts we cannot directly test. We extend that courtesy to Herodotus and Tacitus; the Bible asks only for the same treatment, not a privileged one.

Honest limit: confirming a reliable setting is not the same as confirming every event set within it—a novel can be set in a meticulously accurate London.

Why incidental accuracy matters Strong

It is the small, unmotivated details that are hardest to fake from a distance. A governor’s precise title, the location of a city gate, the name of a local coin, a regional custom—these are exactly the things a later legend-writer, working centuries after the fact and in another place, tends to botch. Roman provincial administration, for instance, reshuffled titles constantly; a forger writing generations later would have no reliable way to know which title applied in which province in which decade. Getting such details right signals proximity to the events.

A famous illustration is Luke’s use of the rare title politarch for the city officials of Thessalonica (Acts 17:6). The word was unknown from classical literature, and critics once cited it as an error; inscriptions later turned up using exactly that term for exactly those officials in Macedonia. The right comparison is not whether a city existed—any author could name a real city—but whether the author knew the granular, shifting administrative furniture that only a well-informed near-contemporary would.

The classicist’s verdict on Acts Scope-limited

The pivotal figure on the New Testament side is the Oxford Roman historian A. N. Sherwin-White, whose 1963 Sarum Lectures examined the book of Acts not as a theologian but as a classicist assessing a Roman-era source. His verdict became one of the most quoted lines in the field: “For Acts the confirmation of historicity is overwhelming. … Any attempt to reject its basic historicity even in matters of detail must now appear absurd.” For the New Testament, the framework reliability of Acts that Sherwin-White praised remains broadly respected even among non-Christian classicists.

Honest limit: Sherwin-White was vindicating Acts’ framework of persons, events, and customs, not its theology, and we should be just as precise about scope as he was. Likewise Albright’s broad confidence later proved too sweeping at points—his datings have been revised, and his “conquest model” is now widely doubted—though his program reset the terms of the debate.

Objection — “Getting the scenery right doesn’t make the miracles true”

A novel set in real London with real street names is still fiction. Accurate geography and politics show the author knew the setting—not that the supernatural claims happened.

Reply

Correct—and the argument doesn’t claim otherwise. Archaeology can’t excavate a resurrection. What it establishes is that these authors are careful, well-informed reporters, not distant myth-makers inventing a vanished world. That dismantles the “late legend” theory and earns the texts a serious historical hearing: a source this reliable on the testable parts deserves to be weighed, not waved away, on the rest. Whether what the biblical authors wrote is true turns above all on the resurrection.

The room: Sherwin-White vindicated the framework of Acts as a classicist; Albright led the archaeological reply to nineteenth-century skepticism, and Dever (What Did the Biblical Writers Know and When Did They Know It?) both credits and critiques the Albright school. The field is best pictured not as “Bible-believers versus skeptics” but as a spectrum.
Defending Premise 2

The biblical narratives have had their checkable claims confirmed by archaeology again and again, especially from the monarchy onward, with remarkable specificity

The confirmations cluster in two kinds: named persons and physical settings. Both are strongest when the find is specific and the figure was previously unattested or even doubted. The gain is real but bounded: an inscription can establish that a person existed and held the office the text assigns him; it cannot by itself confirm what he did, still less any theological claim the text makes about him.

Named persons Strong

The single most celebrated find is the Tel Dan Stele, discovered in 1993 by Avraham Biran’s team at Tel Dan in northern Israel. The fragmentary basalt victory monument, written in Aramaic and dated to the ninth century BC, contains the phrase bytdwd—“House of David.” Before its discovery, some scholars had argued that David was a legendary figure with no more historical reality than King Arthur; here, within roughly a century of his lifetime, a foreign king (most likely Hazael of Damascus) refers to the southern kingdom as the dynasty of David. For the New Testament, the touchstone is the Pilate Stone, a damaged limestone block found in 1961 at Caesarea Maritima, whose partial inscription names [Pon]tius Pilatus and his title, [praef]ectus Iuda[ea]e—prefect of Judea.

The named-person confirmations are not a scattered handful but a disciplined corpus: the Purdue scholar Lawrence Mykytiuk has catalogued more than fifty figures of the Hebrew Bible whose existence is confirmed by contemporary inscriptions, each admitted only under an explicit protocol—a match of name, patronymic, title, and period specific enough to rule out coincidence—so that unqualified candidates are excluded rather than counted. To these one can add a roster of Israelite and Judahite kings independently named in Assyrian and Babylonian records—Ahab of Israel, for instance, is named on the Kurkh Monolith of Shalmaneser III among the coalition that met Assyria at the Battle of Qarqar in 853 BC—together with inscribed seals (bullae) bearing biblical names, including a clay seal impression of King Hezekiah.

Honest limit: the Tel Dan Stele is not proof of the biblical David’s exploits, but it is powerful evidence that a real dynastic founder named David stood at the head of Judah’s royal house; the reading bytdwd is accepted by most scholars, while a minority (some in the Copenhagen School) contest the word-division. The Pilate Stone is the principal contemporary epigraphic evidence of Pilate. The Caiaphas ossuary, inscribed twice with the name “Joseph son of Caiaphas,” is widely—though not universally—identified with the high priest Caiaphas of the Gospels.

Doubted, then confirmed Strong

The Old Testament mentions the Hittites some four dozen times, yet for decades they were little more than a name in the Bible and in scattered Egyptian and Assyrian records, and some skeptical critics treated them as a sign of the text’s unreliability. Then, in 1906, Hugo Winckler began digging at Boğazköy in central Turkey and uncovered the ruins of Hattusa, the capital of a vast empire, together with thousands of cuneiform tablets. A people once doubted turned out to have ruled an empire.

The most instructive case is Belshazzar. Named as king of Babylon in Daniel 5, he was for a long time unknown to any other source, and an earlier generation of critics treated Daniel’s “King Belshazzar” as a telltale blunder betraying a late, ill-informed author. Then the cuneiform record answered—a cylinder of Nabonidus naming Bel-shar-usur as his eldest son, and the Nabonidus Chronicle and the “Verse Account,” which report that Nabonidus resided for years at Tema in Arabia and entrusted the kingship to that son—showing Belshazzar to be a real figure who wielded royal authority in Babylon as his father’s co-regent. The Assyrian king Sargon II is named in the Bible only once, in Isaiah 20:1; his independent existence was doubted, until Paul-Émile Botta’s excavation of his palace at Khorsabad, begun in 1843, confirmed him as a distinct ruler whose own annals record the very campaign against Ashdod that Isaiah reports.

Honest limit: the Belshazzar find establishes the man and his authority, though not by itself the book’s date or the events it narrates. And the same pattern has an unresolved case that cuts the other way: “Darius the Mede” has not been confirmed—no cuneiform source names such a king, and critics from H. H. Rowley to Lester Grabbe read this as a plain historical error; proposals exist (Wiseman: Darius is Cyrus himself; Whitcomb: the governor Gubaru) but none has won a consensus. The honest verdict is a genuine open question—not a solved case the apologist may claim, and not the decisive blunder the skeptic needs it to be. An identification that is unconfirmed is not the same as one that is refuted.

Israel before its kings Bounded

The people of Israel is attested outside the Bible earlier than any of its kings. The Merneptah Stele, an Egyptian victory monument discovered by Flinders Petrie at Thebes in 1896 and dated to about 1208 BC, lists the peoples and towns the pharaoh claimed to have crushed in Canaan—and among them, written with the hieroglyphic sign for a people rather than the sign used for a settled city-state, stands the name Israel. It is the earliest reference to Israel outside the Bible, and the only widely accepted one in any Egyptian text.

Honest limit: the claim is bounded—it fixes that a people called Israel was present in Canaan by the late thirteenth century BC, not its size or political form, and nothing for or against the Exodus or the conquest.

Settings and events — and Acts Strong

The physical sites are equally concrete. Hezekiah’s Tunnel in Jerusalem—the 533-meter conduit that 2 Kings 20:20 and 2 Chronicles 32 credit to Hezekiah—survives, and near its outlet a paleo-Hebrew inscription discovered in 1880 (the Siloam Inscription) describes the moment two crews of tunnelers, digging toward each other through bedrock, broke through and met. In John’s Gospel, the Pool of Bethesda is described with the oddly specific detail of “five porticoes” (John 5:2)—a feature many scholars once dismissed as symbolic invention until excavation near St. Anne’s Church revealed a twin pool divided by a central wall. Sennacherib’s own annals, inscribed on clay prisms, corroborate his campaign against Hezekiah’s Judah; the Cyrus Cylinder reflects the Persian policy of repatriating subject peoples that frames the return in Ezra.

The book of Acts furnishes its own cluster, several of them turning on the same test of correct, shifting titles. The Delphi inscription preserving a letter of the emperor Claudius names Gallio as proconsul of Achaia and dates his term to about AD 51–52; because Acts 18:12 has Paul brought before this same Gallio at Corinth, it anchors Paul’s Corinthian mission in absolute time. Luke correctly styles Sergius Paulus the proconsul of Cyprus (Acts 13:7). The long sea-voyage narrative of Acts 27–28 is all the more telling because Luke never pauses to point to any of it: he places the small island of Cauda (27:16) correctly, and calls the storm-wind a euroaquilo (27:14), a word long suspected as a Lukan coinage until it was found inscribed on a Roman wind-rose at Thugga in North Africa.

Honest limit: what the spade confirms in the Gospel pool is the place and its first-century use, not the healing. The Gallio stone dates the official, not the apostle; it is the synchronism with Acts that does the work. Attempts to identify Sergius Paulus with one of the epigraphically attested Sergii Paulli are suggestive but remain debated, so the secure point is Luke’s correct usage, not a personal match. The Erastus pavement may name the Erastus of Romans 16:23, though the identification is plausible rather than secure. None of these is a point of doctrine. The pattern that matters is not any single artifact but the convergence: epigraphy, geography, numismatics, and custom all pointing the same direction across centuries and genres. No one of these is decisive alone; together they are weighty.

Objection — Finkelstein: the load-bearing narratives are where the evidence thins out Hardest

Finkelstein would press: you keep retreating to “framework” and “presumption,” but the central narratives your tradition cares about—a grand united monarchy, a conquest, an Exodus on the biblical scale—are precisely the ones the archaeology fails to confirm and in places contradicts. The names you can verify, like Pilate or the House of David, are the easy, late, or incidental ones; the load-bearing theological history is exactly where the evidence thins out. Israel Finkelstein of Tel Aviv University, whose “Low Chronology” redates the monumental architecture once credited to Solomon’s tenth century into the ninth, argues with Neil Asher Silberman that the united monarchy of David and Solomon, far from being the glittering empire of 1 Kings, was at most a modest highland chiefdom, and that much of the Pentateuch and Deuteronomistic History was composed as late-seventh-century Judahite ideology projected onto an invented past. Finkelstein rejects the “minimalist” label and regards himself as a centrist; his is an argument about scale and dating, not the wholesale denial of David’s existence. On the Exodus, there is no direct Egyptian record of a mass Israelite departure; on the conquest, Kathleen Kenyon’s excavation of Jericho in the 1950s redated its great destruction to roughly 1550 BC—well before any plausible date for Joshua.

Reply

The verifications are real and not confined to trivia—the House of David in the ninth century, Hezekiah’s tunnel and seal, the Assyrian and Babylonian synchronisms with named Judahite kings, the precise administrative texture of Acts: these touch the spine of the historical narrative, not merely its margins. And the track record runs in one direction more often than the other. Repeatedly—the Hittites, the existence of David, the title of Pilate, the politarchs, the five-portico pool—confidently asserted “errors” were overturned by later discovery, which counsels humility before declaring the unconfirmed to be refuted. But the reply grants the observation in Finkelstein’s point, not his inference: archaeology does not deliver a grand united monarchy or a sweeping conquest, and where the evidence is silent archaeology supplies no direct confirmation, only the general presumption earned elsewhere—but silence is not refutation, and an unconfirmed narrative is not thereby an invented one. What it yields is a reliable historical setting and a strong presumption of careful reportage—not a confirmation of every event.

The disputed sites turn heavily on interpretation and dating rather than on clean refutation. Kenyon’s 1550 BC dating of Jericho rested largely on the absence of certain imported Cypriot pottery, and Bryant G. Wood challenged her in 1990; Wood’s revision is a minority view and has not won the field, and the honest summary is that the chronology is disputed, not that the Bible has been vindicated. Nor does the text itself require a single blitzkrieg: Joshua reports only Jericho, Ai, and Hazor burned (Joshua 6:24; 8:28; 11:11), and Judges 1 lists city after city left unconquered. On the Exodus, the Egyptologist James K. Hoffmeier argues that the sojourn and wilderness traditions fit their Egyptian setting closely, but his evidence shows that the traditions are at home in an Egyptian setting and so supports their plausibility, not the event itself: no find names Israel in Egypt or traces its departure, and critics, Finkelstein and Silberman among them, date many of the Egyptian details to the seventh century BC. On all three—the Exodus, the conquest, and the scale of David and Solomon’s kingdom—honesty requires saying plainly: these are contested, and on some the burden of evidence does not currently favor a maximal reading. But neither has archaeology disproved them: the Exodus and the conquest are unconfirmed and disputed, not refuted.

Objection — “You’re cherry-picking the hits and ignoring the misses”

Any large ancient text mentioning real places will land some confirmations by chance. Apologists parade the successes and quietly drop everything still unverified or in tension.

Reply

Two things blunt this. First, the confirmations aren’t generic “a city existed” hits—they’re specific and risky: exact titles (Luke’s “politarchs” of Thessalonica, once thought an error, then found on inscriptions), named officials, local customs. Random luck doesn’t produce that. Second, the unverified items are mostly things archaeology simply can’t reach, and where the record does strain—Jericho, a large-scale Exodus—the honest verdict is unconfirmed and disputed, not refuted. Silence is not refutation.

The room: Finkelstein (a centrist, not a minimalist) presses scale and dating; the genuinely minimalist position belongs to the Copenhagen School, above all Thomas L. Thompson, for whom the confirmations apologists cite are late, ambiguous, or beside the point. The leading critic of minimalism is not a believer defending Scripture but William G. Dever, who calls the Copenhagen School’s skepticism a species of postmodern “nihilism” that the data do not support—though his endorsement is limited: he affirms the historical framework while rejecting many specific biblical claims and most miracle accounts. Kenneth Kitchen, a maximalist, argues that the texts fit their claimed ancient Near Eastern settings far better than a late invention could—a real argument, but contested: scholars such as the Deuteronomy specialist J. G. McConville have urged more caution about how tightly the treaty parallels can date the text. What the great majority of archaeologists reject is either pole’s caricature: that the Bible is a seamless historical record, or that it is historically worthless.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

This is what separates someone who knows the argument from someone who can defend it. Each gets its best form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: Finkelstein’s — the load-bearing narratives are exactly where the evidence thins out. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"But archaeology has also contradicted the Bible" Trickier

Strongest form

The conquest of Jericho, the size of the Exodus, the grandeur of David and Solomon's kingdom — this is not fringe. Israel Finkelstein — a centrist who rejects the “minimalist” label — argues on Low Chronology grounds that the tenth-century monumental architecture belongs to the ninth, and that the united monarchy was a modest highland polity rather than the empire of 1 Kings. Separately, the genuinely minimalist Copenhagen school holds the Bible unusable as a historical source at all.

These are disputes over interpretation and dating, not slam-dunk refutations. Jericho hinges on dating: Kenyon's excavation redated its destruction to around 1550 BC, well before Joshua; but that dating rested largely on the absence of certain imported pottery, and though most archaeologists still follow it, it is disputed, not settled; absence of evidence in nomadic or small-scale episodes is not evidence of absence. Meanwhile the long-term pattern runs the other way — repeated cases where the text was vindicated after skeptics declared it wrong (the Hittites, the existence of David, the title of Pilate, the politarchs). Honesty means granting the open questions while noting which way the track record points. But grant the observation, not the inference: archaeology does not deliver a grand united monarchy or a sweeping conquest, and on some of those points the current evidence does not favor a maximal reading; yet silence is not refutation, and an unconfirmed narrative is not thereby an invented one. Nor does the text itself require a single blitzkrieg: Joshua reports only Jericho, Ai, and Hazor burned (Joshua 6:24; 8:28; 11:11).

"Archaeology is too ambiguous to confirm anything" Hard

Strongest form

Digs yield broken pots and fragmentary inscriptions that experts read in opposite ways. Identifications are provisional and politically charged. You can't build reliability on so contested a discipline.

Reply

Ambiguity is real at the margins, but some finds are decisive: an inscription that spells out "Pontius Pilate, prefect of Judea," or "House of David," is not a Rorschach blot. The case rests on the convergence of many independent lines — epigraphy, geography, numismatics, customs — not one disputed sherd. When dozens of independent witnesses point the same direction, the cumulative weight outruns any single ambiguity.

"Confirming the Old Testament setting says nothing about Jesus" Hard

Strongest form

Even granting that Kings and Acts have an accurate backdrop, the Gospels are a different genre with theological aims. Archaeological reliability elsewhere doesn't transfer to the central Christian claims.

Reply

The reliability is argued per book, and the Gospels and Acts pass the same test directly: Luke names real officials with their correct (and changing) titles and tracks Roman administration precisely, and John locates real pools — the very things a careless or late inventor gets wrong. Establishing that the evangelists are sober reporters of their world doesn't prove the resurrection, but it removes the "obvious legend" excuse and earns the Gospels a serious historical reading.

The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

The field is best pictured not as “Bible-believers versus skeptics” but as a spectrum. At one end sit the minimalists (Thomas L. Thompson, Niels Peter Lemche, Philip R. Davies), whose denial that the Bible is usable for history is now a distinctly minority view — William G. Dever’s critique landed, and the Tel Dan Stele embarrassed the strongest “David is pure myth” claims. In the broad center sit Israel Finkelstein and Amihai Mazar, who disagree sharply over the tenth-century chronology but share the conviction that the texts contain real historical memory wrapped in later ideology; at the other end stand maximalists like Kenneth Kitchen. The live debates — the date of the united monarchy, the historicity and scale of the Exodus and conquest, how to read tenth-century strata at sites like Khirbet Qeiyafa — are genuine and unresolved. What the great majority of archaeologists reject is either pole’s caricature: that the Bible is a seamless historical record, or that it is historically worthless. For the New Testament, the framework reliability of Acts that A. N. Sherwin-White praised remains broadly respected even among non-Christian classicists.

A fairness most defenders skip: Honest defenders must concede where the record genuinely strains. There is no direct Egyptian record of a mass Israelite departure, and a maximal, literal-numbers Exodus is precisely the version the archaeological silence pressures. Kenyon’s Jericho dating places its great destruction well before any plausible date for Joshua, Wood’s challenge is a minority view, and the picture at several “conquest” sites is gradual settlement. The scale of David and Solomon’s kingdom is exactly what Finkelstein’s chronology calls into question, and Darius the Mede has not been confirmed. On some of these the burden of evidence does not currently favor a maximal reading. But the reply grants the observation in Finkelstein’s point, not his inference: silence is not refutation, and an unconfirmed narrative is not thereby an invented one — and the track record, from the Hittites to the five-portico pool, counsels humility before declaring the unconfirmed to be refuted.

The bounded verdict: Archaeology establishes, with as much confidence as the discipline allows, that the biblical writers were generally well-informed reporters of their world: they named real persons — some once dismissed as legendary — gave officials their correct and changing titles, and located real pools and tunnels and cities. That dismantles the picture of the Bible as a free-floating late legend and earns the texts a serious historical hearing. It does not prove the Bible’s theology or its miracles, and it does not resolve the disputes over the Exodus, the conquest, or the scale of David and Solomon’s kingdom — but neither has archaeology disproved them: they are unconfirmed and disputed, not refuted. A reliable setting is a foundation, not a finished house; how faithfully the text was transmitted, how early it was written, and whether what its authors wrote is true — which turns above all on the resurrection — are argued on their own merits. The right conclusion is neither “archaeology proves the Bible” nor “archaeology refutes it”: the biblical authors knew their world, and a source that reliable deserves to be weighed, not waved away.

    JV
    Jerusalem visitor
    "The Church of the Holy Sepulchre stands well inside Jerusalem’s Old City, but the New Testament puts the crucifixion and burial outside the city — “near the city” (John 19:20), “outside the gate” (Hebrews 13:12). So the traditional tomb cannot be the place; it was chosen centuries later on pious assumption."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    The wall that encloses the site today is not the wall of Jesus’ day. On the reconstruction most archaeologists accept, the site lay outside the northern city wall of about AD 30 — in an old limestone quarry reused for rock-cut tombs — and was brought within the city only about a decade later, when Herod Agrippa I began Jerusalem’s outer “third” wall in the 40s AD (the exact line of the earlier “second” wall remains debated). Excavation has exposed first-century quarrying and rock-cut tombs of just the type the burial narrative describes, and the 2016–17 restoration uncovered the original rock-cut burial bench of the Second Temple type (its own dating confirmed the fourth-century enshrinement, not the tomb itself). Nor was the site simply picked centuries later: when Hadrian rebuilt Jerusalem as Aelia Capitolina (AD 135), he raised a pagan temple over the spot, which had the incidental effect of marking it, so that on the mainstream reconstruction Constantine’s builders were excavating a place already marked out — though the strength of that memory-chain is itself debated. None of this identifies the grave of Jesus with certainty; that lies beyond what a trowel can settle. But it dissolves the one objection commonly thought to rule the traditional site out, so the objection does not hold: the identification rests on evidence, not on pious assumption alone. (Which sites the churches venerate, and how, is a matter of devotion on which the traditions differ; nothing here adjudicates it — the only question is whether the location is archaeologically credible as a first-century tomb outside the AD-30 wall, and it is.)
