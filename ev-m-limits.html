Does Science Disprove God?

The deep dive tells you what the argument is. This track makes you able to use it — reconstruct it from memory, answer its hardest objections, and hold the line in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

“Science has buried God” is one of the most confident slogans of the age — delivered less as an argument than as a weather report. The picture behind it is familiar: once people reached for God to explain the thunder and the plague, and one by one the gaps where God used to live have been filled in. This argument asks whether that verdict is earned. Its thesis is deliberately modest, and worth stating plainly so no one mistakes it for a bigger claim: science neither does nor can disprove God. It is not the claim that science points to God, still less that science disproves atheism — those are separate arguments made on their own evidence.

The modest thesis is shown in three moves. First, the slogan’s engine is scientism — the claim that science is the only route to knowledge — and that claim refutes itself: the statement “only science delivers knowledge” is not itself a scientific finding, so by its own standard it fails to qualify as knowledge (the same flaw that sank the logical positivists’ verification principle). Second, the slogan slides without argument from a fruitful method (methodological naturalism — look for natural causes) to an unproven metaphysics (nature is all there is); the geneticist Richard Lewontin candidly described materialism as a “prior commitment” that decides in advance what will count. Third, explaining the mechanism of a thing never removes the agent behind it: that we understand the engine does not show Henry Ford never designed the car.

The honest limits matter as much as the point. This argument removes a roadblock; it does not complete a journey. It does not prove that God exists, does not disprove atheism, and does not even establish that the universe points to a mind. The positive case is argued separately — from the beginning and fine-tuning of the universe (where atheist physicists concede the remarkable data while vigorously contesting the inference to design), from the moral law, and above all from the resurrection of Jesus. And even a Creator or Designer reached by argument is not yet the Father, Son and Holy Spirit; identifying that being with the God revealed in Christ is the work of revelation and, above all, the historical evidence for the resurrection (see The Minimal Facts Argument).

Before you start · key terms

Eight words this argument leans on

Learn these first — the objections below turn on them, and most weak versions of the argument come from blurring one of them (above all, treating a method as a metaphysics, or letting the modest thesis quietly inflate into “science proves God”).

The modest thesisThe whole claim, stated plainly: science neither does nor can disprove God. It is not that science points to God, and not that science disproves atheism — those are separate, positive arguments made on their own evidence. Keeping the thesis modest is what keeps it true; a case that claims too much will not hold.
ScientismNot science itself, but a philosophy about science that promotes it from one powerful way of knowing into the only one. Bertrand Russell put it bluntly: “what science cannot discover, mankind cannot know.” Scientism is the engine of the whole slogan — and the slogan rarely states it out loud.
The self-refutation“Only science yields knowledge” is not itself a scientific finding — no experiment could establish it — so by its own standard it is not knowledge. It saws off the branch it sits on. The same fate overtook the logical positivists’ verification principle, which was itself neither empirically verifiable nor true by definition, and so meaningless by its own terms.
Methodological naturalismA working rule, not a creed: when investigating nature, look for natural causes and do not write “and then a miracle occurred” in a research paper. An enormously fruitful rule — and one a Christian biologist can use all day precisely because she believes God made an orderly, intelligible world.
Metaphysical naturalismA worldview, not a method: the philosophical claim that nature is all there is, that the physical universe is a sealed box with nothing outside it. The slogan trades on sliding, without argument, from the method to this worldview — assuming that because science looks only for natural causes, only natural causes exist.
The slideThe unargued leap from “science studies natural causes” to “only natural causes are real.” No experiment performs it; it is performed by the philosopher hiding inside whoever makes the leap. Lewontin named it with unusual candor: materialism is a “prior commitment” that decides in advance what evidence is allowed to count — “we cannot allow a Divine Foot in the door.”
The McMullin fenceThe honest other half, and not a small concession: it would be an overreach to conclude that methodological naturalism is covert atheism. The Catholic philosopher Ernan McMullin argued it “does not restrict our study of nature; it just lays down which sort of study qualifies as scientific.” So the verdict is bounded: the method can carry a metaphysics (in Lewontin’s case openly does), but it need not, and for many scientists it does not.
Mechanism vs. agency (how vs. why)A scientific account of how a thing works does not compete with a personal account of who is responsible for it; treating them as rivals is a category mistake. Understanding the combustion engine does not show Henry Ford never designed the car; knowing the chemistry of ink does not show Hamlet had no author. Laplace’s “I had no need of that hypothesis” answered a narrow technical question, not the question of whether the law-governed system has an Author.
The argument, formally

Three lines. Each clears away one mistake behind “science has buried God.”

P1

The claims said to show science has buried God — “science is the only path to truth” and “nature is all there is” — are philosophical commitments, not scientific findings (scientism refutes itself; metaphysical naturalism is a method mistaken for a metaphysics).

P2

Explaining the mechanism of a thing never removes the agent behind it; a scientific “how” and a personal “why” answer different questions, and treating them as rivals is a category mistake.

∴ C

Therefore science neither does nor can disprove God — a roadblock removed. It does not prove God exists, does not prove design, and does not reach the Triune God of the Nicene Creed.

This argument removes a roadblock rather than completing a journey, so it is not a positive proof — and must never be presented as one. Objections tend to target P1 (“no careful person holds exceptionless scientism”) or to confuse the mechanism of P2 with the absence of an agent.

Then the step most people miss — how far it reaches

The conclusion is liberating precisely because it is modest: God was never a gap in our knowledge to be crowded out, but the reason there is an orderly world to understand at all.

What follows — and what doesn’t

Don’t read the conclusion — reason to it

Working only from what the argument has established: what does each move rule out, and how far does it reach? Reason each line out, then reveal.

Does showing “science has buried God” is false prove that God exists…?
no — it removes a roadblock, it does not complete the journeyThe argument shows only that science neither does nor can disprove God. Nothing here proves God exists, disproves atheism, or even establishes that the universe points to a mind. Those are positive arguments, made on their own evidence. Clearing a false obstacle is a real service — but it is only the clearing of the ground.
Showing a mechanism and an agent are compatible — does that prove there is an Agent…?
no — it only clears away the illusion that one rules out the otherA convinced atheist can grant every word of the mechanism-vs-agency move and still hold that, as a matter of fact, there is no mind behind the universe. What the move establishes is only that he cannot reach that conclusion by pointing to the success of science at describing mechanisms. The question “is there an Author?” stays open, to be argued on its own ground.
If methodological naturalism need not be atheism, is it just religiously neutral then…?
bounded both ways — it can carry a metaphysics, but need notThe honest verdict is narrower than the slogan and sharper than a shrug. Methodological naturalism can be — and in Lewontin’s case openly is — a metaphysical commitment smuggled in under the name of a method. But it need not be, and for many working scientists it is not. The error to expose is not the method; it is the unargued leap from “science studies natural causes” to “only natural causes are real.”
Does fine-tuning let you say “scientists agree the universe is designed”…?
no — the data is conceded; design is the contested inferenceThe positive case from fine-tuning belongs to a separate argument. There, atheist physicists concede the remarkable data while vigorously contesting the inference to design. So the honest line is never “scientists agree the universe is designed” — it is that the data is granted and the design inference is argued, and contested, on its own ground.
Grant the argument works — does it get you to the God of the Bible…?
no — a Creator reached by argument is not yet the Triune GodEven if the positive arguments later succeed in pointing to a transcendent Mind, that Mind is not yet the Father of Jesus Christ. A Creator or Designer reached by argument is still a long way from the Triune God of the Nicene Creed, who is known not by inference alone but by self-revelation in Christ, and above all by the historical evidence for the resurrection.
Defending Premise 1

The claims that bury God are philosophy, not science (scientism self-refutes; a method is not a metaphysics)

Precision check: the target is not science but two philosophical claims that ride along with it — “science is the only path to truth” and “nature is all there is.” Nothing here disparages the real triumphs of physics, chemistry, and biology, or suggests a believer may wave away an inconvenient scientific result. The claim is only that these two slogans are not findings of science at all.

Scientism saws off its own branch Self-refuting by its own standard

“What science cannot discover, mankind cannot know” (Russell). Is that claim something science can discover? No experiment, measurement, or instrument could establish it — there is no telescope one can point at “science is the only road to truth” and read off its confirmation. So by its own standard it fails to qualify as knowledge: if it is true, it is not knowledge; and if it is not knowledge, there is no reason to believe it. Either way, it collapses.

The verification principle is the precedent Not a debating trick

This is not a clever trick; it is the fate that overtook a more rigorous earlier version. The logical positivists’ “verification principle” held a statement meaningful only if empirically verifiable or true by definition — until philosophers noticed the principle is itself neither, and so, by its own terms, meaningless. It quietly died. Scientism is its less careful heir, carrying the same self-destruct mechanism inside it.

A Nobel scientist marks the boundary From inside science

Sir Peter Medawar — Nobel laureate, and no enemy of science — called science “the most successful enterprise human beings have ever engaged upon,” and then, precisely as a scientist, marked its limit: there are questions “that science cannot answer,” the childlike yet inexhaustible ones — “How did everything begin? What are we all here for? What is the point of living?” To say a thermometer cannot measure justice is not an insult to thermometers; it is to know what a thermometer is for.

The slide is admitted by materialists themselves A slide, not a discovery

That the move from method to metaphysics is a slide is best shown not by a believer but by a committed materialist with his eyes open. Richard Lewontin: we take the side of science “in spite of the patent absurdity of some of its constructs … because we have a prior commitment, a commitment to materialism … we cannot allow a Divine Foot in the door.” The door is shut before the inquiry begins. The cancer researcher George Klein said his atheism was not based on science but on conviction; the Nobel biologist Christian de Duve granted his program rested on a postulate. Each concedes the very distinction the slogan needs to blur.

Objection — this topples a straw man

No careful person holds the exceptionless claim that only science yields knowledge — merely the milder thesis that science is by far our most reliable guide to how the world is. So the self-refutation defeats a position nobody defends.

Reply

The milder thesis is reasonable, and nothing here disputes it. But notice what it costs the slogan it was meant to rescue. A scientism modest enough to survive the self-refutation is also too modest to carry the atheist conclusion: “science is our most reliable method” simply does not entail “nature is all there is,” any more than “the microscope is the best instrument for studying cells” entails “only what a microscope can see is real.” The strong version refutes itself; the version that survives no longer disproves God.

Objection — but science looks only for natural causes, so only natural causes exist

Working scientists operate by methodological naturalism all the time; surely that is as good as discovering that nature is all there is.

Reply

That is the slide, named. Methodological naturalism is a method; metaphysical naturalism is a worldview, and the one does not entail the other. The leap from “science studies natural causes” to “only natural causes are real” is never performed by any experiment — it needs to be argued for, in the open, not presupposed.

The room: the fairness most defenders skip is the McMullin fence, and it must be given its full weight. The Catholic philosopher of science Ernan McMullin argued that methodological naturalism “does not restrict our study of nature; it just lays down which sort of study qualifies as scientific,” and says nothing about whether nature is all there is. So McMullin and Lewontin can both be quoted: they agree the method/metaphysics distinction exists and disagree only about whether the method carries the metaphysics. The honest verdict is bounded — methodological naturalism can be a smuggled metaphysics (Lewontin), but need not be (McMullin).
Defending Premise 2

Explaining the mechanism never removes the agent (how vs. why)

Precision check: the two answers belong to different categories, and treating them as rivals is a category mistake. This move is compatibilist, not probative: it clears away the illusion that a complete “how” rules out a “who” — it does not, by itself, prove that there is in fact an Agent.

Ford and the car The plainest example

Explain how a car moves — the spark, the compressed mixture, the expanding gases, the crankshaft, the wheels — exhaustively, down to the last valve. Does that show Henry Ford had nothing to do with the car? Of course not. The engineering of the engine and the agency of the designer are not competing explanations fighting over one slot; they answer different questions. To say “we understand combustion, therefore there was no designer” is exactly as confused as “we understand the chemistry of ink and paper, therefore Hamlet had no author.”

The cake — how vs. why Two kinds of question

Give the world’s greatest scientists unlimited time with a cake: they could tell you in exhaustive detail how it is constituted — the chemistry, the physics of the baking, the biology of the wheat. But not one instrument could tell you why it was made: that it was baked for a friend’s birthday, as an act of love. For that you must ask the baker, whose answer no further chemistry could supply or overturn. The “why” of purpose is not a gap in the “how” waiting for better science; it is a different kind of question altogether.

Laplace, read correctly The most misread anecdote

When Laplace presented his mechanics of the solar system, Napoleon observed that it never mentioned the author of the universe; Laplace replied, “Sire, I had no need of that hypothesis.” Read in context, this is not science dismissing God. Laplace answered a narrow technical question — whether the equations of planetary motion require a term for divine adjustment — and the answer was no. But God was never a term inside the equation, any more than Henry Ford is a term inside a description of combustion. To find no need for God in orbital mathematics is no evidence against God’s existence.

Objection — this is a God-of-the-gaps Watch this one

You have found a gap in our understanding and stuffed God into it; one day a completed physics will close the gap, and your “explanation” will evaporate, like every gap argument before it.

Reply

This gets the argument backwards. A God-of-the-gaps argument says “science cannot yet explain X, therefore God.” This says the opposite: God was never a gap in our knowledge to be filled in, but the reason there is an orderly world to understand at all. The believer need not fear the next discovery crowding God out of the last gap, because God was never a term inside the equation, to be deleted when the equation is solved. Even with the physics completed, the question of why — of purpose, of an Author — would remain, because it was always a different kind of question.

The room: the category difference between mechanism and agency is not fringe apologetics but mainstream — as old as Aristotle’s distinction among kinds of cause. The honest fence stays set: this move does not, by itself, prove that a Creator exists. A convinced atheist can grant every word and still hold that the cosmos has a “how” and no “why.” What he cannot do is reach that conclusion merely by pointing to the success of science at describing mechanisms.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

This is what separates someone who knows the argument from someone who can defend it. Each is stated as you will actually meet it, then answered — and the hardest version of all is flagged just below.

The one that matters most: the sophisticated critic grants all three moves and still says — “fine, science hasn’t disproved God, but the self-refutation only defeats a straw man; no careful person holds exceptionless scientism, only the milder thesis that science is our most reliable guide, which is all a naturalist ever needed.” The deep dive answers it: the milder thesis is reasonable but too modest to carry “nature is all there is.” Take that with you, not just the replies below.

"Science keeps explaining what we used to credit to God — the gaps are closing, and God is being squeezed out" Common

Strongest form

Once we reached for God to explain the thunder, the harvest, the plague; now we have meteorology, agronomy, and germ theory. One by one the gaps where God used to live have been filled in — there is no reason to think the last ones won’t be too.

Reply

This confuses mechanism with agency. Discovering how something works does not remove the mind behind it, any more than understanding the internal combustion engine shows Henry Ford never designed the car. A scientific description of a mechanism and a personal explanation of a purpose answer different questions; they do not compete for the same slot. God was never a “gap” in our knowledge to be filled in, but the reason there is an orderly world to understand at all.

"Isn't science the only reliable path to truth?" Common

Strongest form

Whatever cannot be weighed, measured, or tested is at best private feeling. Science is the one method that has actually delivered — so if a claim can’t be established scientifically, there’s no good reason to believe it.

Reply

That claim — scientism — refutes itself. “Only science delivers knowledge” is not itself a scientific finding; no experiment could establish it. So if it were true, it would not count as knowledge by its own standard — the same flaw that sank the logical positivists’ verification principle. Science is a magnificent way of knowing; it is simply not the only one, as the Nobel scientist Peter Medawar freely granted.

"Nature is all there is, and science shows it" Trickier

Strongest form

Science investigates the natural world and finds only natural causes, every time. The honest inference from centuries of that success is that the physical universe is a sealed box — nature is all there is.

Reply

That slides, without argument, from a method to a metaphysics. Looking only for natural causes (methodological naturalism) is a fruitful working rule; concluding that only natural causes exist (metaphysical naturalism) is a worldview, and the one does not entail the other. The geneticist Richard Lewontin admitted as much: materialism is a “prior commitment” that decides in advance what evidence will count — “we cannot allow a Divine Foot in the door.” That is a philosophical stance in a lab coat, not a discovery.

"Methodological naturalism means scientists work as atheists — so science is godless at its core" Trickier

Strongest form

If the rule of the lab is to refuse any appeal to God, then science is built on the assumption that God makes no difference — which is functional atheism, whatever the scientist says on Sunday.

Reply

Here the honest fence must be set both ways. It would be an overreach to say methodological naturalism is covert atheism — the Catholic philosopher Ernan McMullin argued it “does not restrict our study of nature; it just lays down which sort of study qualifies as scientific.” A Christian biologist can look for natural mechanisms all day precisely because she believes God made an orderly, intelligible world. The verdict is bounded: the method can carry a metaphysics (in Lewontin’s case openly does), but it need not, and for many scientists it does not.

"Laplace told Napoleon he had no need of that hypothesis — science dismissed God outright" Hard

Strongest form

The most famous line in the whole debate: the great astronomer, asked why his work never mentions God, says he had no need of the hypothesis. Science had grown up and could do without the crutch.

Reply

Read in context, it is nothing of the kind. Laplace was answering a narrow technical question — whether the equations of planetary motion require a term for divine adjustment — and the answer was no: the orbits are stable on their own mechanics. But God was never a term inside the equation, any more than Henry Ford is a term inside a description of combustion. To find no need for God in orbital mathematics is no more evidence against God than finding no mention of the baker in a chemical assay of a cake is evidence that the cake baked itself.

"You've just moved God to where science can't reach — that's God-of-the-gaps, and unfalsifiable" Hard

Strongest form

Every time science advances you retreat God one step further back, into whatever gap is left. Eventually there’s nowhere left to retreat — and a God who makes no measurable difference is indistinguishable from no God at all.

Reply

This gets it backwards. A God-of-the-gaps argument says “science cannot yet explain X, therefore God.” This says the opposite: God was never a gap to be filled, but the reason there is an orderly world to understand at all — never a term inside the equation, to be deleted when the equation is solved. Even with the physics completed, the question of why reality is this way would remain, because it was always a different kind of question. And note the honest limit on the other side: this clears a false obstacle; the positive case that there is Someone there is argued separately, on evidence, above all the resurrection.

The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

The relationship of science and religion is a large and genuinely contested scholarly field. The old “conflict thesis” — that science and religion have been locked in perpetual warfare — is now regarded by most professional historians of science as a nineteenth-century myth, popularized by polemicists such as Draper and White and quietly abandoned by serious historiography. But the collapse of the conflict thesis has not ended the philosophical dispute, and it should not be oversold: confident defenders of scientism remain — Atkins, and the debates around the “new atheism,” keep the stronger claim in play. The three moves above are not fringe apologetics but mainstream distinctions: the self-refutation of a strong scientism is widely treated in philosophy as a serious problem for it; the method/metaphysics distinction is standard (which is exactly why McMullin and Lewontin can both be quoted); and the mechanism/agency difference is as old as Aristotle.

A fairness most defenders skip: the McMullin fence must be honored. It would be a serious overreach to conclude that methodological naturalism is covert atheism, or that every scientist who uses it is a dogmatist smuggling in a worldview. The honest verdict is bounded both ways: the method can be — in Lewontin’s case openly is — a metaphysical commitment smuggled in under a respectable name, but it need not be, and for many working scientists it is not.

The bounded verdict: science does not and cannot disprove God — the slogan to the contrary rests on a scientism that fails by its own standard, a slide from a fruitful method to an unproven metaphysics, and a confusion of mechanism with agency. But nothing here proves God exists, disproves atheism, or even shows the universe points to a mind; the responsible apologist should never claim it does. The positive case is argued separately — from the beginning and fine-tuning of the universe (atheist physicists concede the data; design is the contested inference, so never “scientists agree the universe is designed”), from the moral law, and above all from the resurrection of Jesus. And even a Creator reached by argument is not yet the Triune God of the Nicene Creed, known by self-revelation in Christ. Clearing the ground of a false obstacle is a real service — but it is only the clearing of the ground.

    "Say you’re right that science hasn’t buried God. All you’ve done is clear the ground. That gets you, at most, some vague Creator or cosmic principle — it’s a long way from there to a personal God, and further still to the Father, Son and Holy Spirit of your creed."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    This is conceded, not resisted — and the essay concedes it plainly. The argument removes a roadblock; it does not complete a journey. It proves nothing about God’s existence, and even if the positive arguments later succeed in pointing to a transcendent Mind, that Mind is not yet the Father of Jesus Christ. A Creator or Designer reached by argument is still a long way from the Triune God of the Nicene Creed, who is known not by inference alone but by self-revelation in Christ — and above all by the historical evidence for the resurrection (see The Minimal Facts Argument). But a boundary is not a refutation: the objection succeeds as a boundary and fails as a defeater. It marks where this argument stops, not that it has gone wrong — and clearing the ground of a false obstacle is a real service, even though it is only the clearing of the ground.
