The Early Creed of 1 Corinthians 15

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and stay clear and calm in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

In 1 Corinthians 15:3-7, Paul writes: "For I delivered to you ... what I also received: that Christ died for our sins ... that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day ... and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve," and later to more than five hundred at once. Scholars across the spectrum recognize this as a pre-formed creed Paul is quoting, not composing: the Greek words for "delivered" and "received" are the technical language of handing on fixed tradition, and the passage carries wording in part not Paul's own and a balanced, memorizable structure.

The dating is what makes it powerful. Paul was converted within a few years of the crucifixion and, three years later, went up to Jerusalem to get acquainted with Cephas, and also saw James the Lord's brother (Galatians 1:18-19) — the very figures named in the creed. Since the tradition itself must predate his receipt of it, this places the creed's content within roughly two to five years of the crucifixion, a remarkably early date that critical scholars widely accept, including skeptics such as Gerd Lüdemann, who himself did not believe in the resurrection. The proclamation of Jesus's death, burial, and appearances is therefore earliest-stratum testimony, not the product of decades of legendary growth.

Honest scope: an early, sincere belief that Jesus appeared is not the same as proof that he rose — that conclusion requires weighing rival explanations (hallucination, fraud, and so on). What the creed does decisively is remove the "late legend" theory: the resurrection claim, with named witnesses, was being recited within a few years of the events, while many of those witnesses were still alive. And this is what we hold to be true: Jesus of Nazareth was bodily raised from the dead, as this creed confesses, and the wider case — the empty tomb, the failure of the alternatives, the conversions of James and Paul — shows it.

The argument, formally

From a received formula to a date the legend theory cannot survive.

P1

In 1 Corinthians 15:3-7 Paul transmits a formula he says he received — marked by rabbinic delivery language, wording in part not Paul's own, and parallel structure — a creed older than his letter.

P2

Tracing the chain — Paul's conversion within a year or three of the cross, his Jerusalem visit three years later to get acquainted with Cephas, when he also saw James the Lord's brother (Galatians 1:18-19); the tradition itself must predate his receipt of it — dates the creed's content to within two to five years of the crucifixion, a dating held across the spectrum.

∴ C

Therefore the resurrection proclamation — died, buried, raised, appeared, with named witnesses — is earliest-stratum testimony, not accumulated legend.

This argument is the engine inside several others — minimal facts, appearances, multiple attestation all draw their early dating from here. Master it once, use it everywhere.

Defending Premise 1

In 1 Corinthians 15:3-7 Paul transmits a formula he says he received — marked by rabbinic delivery language, wording in part not Paul’s own, and parallel structure — a creed older than his letter.

Precision check: That 1 Corinthians 15:3-5 (with the list extending through verse 7) is a pre-Pauline creed is, as the critical commentator Hans Conzelmann put it in his standard commentary on 1 Corinthians, broadly recognized in the scholarship. Several converging features support it. The pre-Pauline character of the formula does not stand or fall on the Aramaic question; it rests on the cumulative weight of the receiving/delivering language, the non-Pauline diction, and the creedal structure.

The introductory verbs: “delivered” and “received” Strong

Paul says he “delivered” (Greek paredōka) what he “received” (parelabon) — the precise idiom used in Judaism for the transmission of authoritative tradition, the equivalent of the rabbinic masar and qibbel. Paul is not narrating his own experience here; he is handing on something handed to him.

Language in part not Paul’s own Strong

The phrase “for our sins,” the verb “he has been raised” in a particular perfect form, the expression “the twelve,” and “according to the Scriptures” cluster non-Pauline vocabulary into a few lines. ‘The Twelve’ is a title Paul never otherwise uses.

Honest limit: The creed also names Peter by his Aramaic name, Kēphas (Cephas), rather than the Greek Petros — though Paul himself usually calls him Cephas, so the name points to a Semitic-speaking milieu rather than proving non-Pauline wording.

A liturgical structure Strong

The lines fall into balanced clauses, each introduced by the Greek hoti (“that”), in a fourfold rhythm — died, was buried, was raised, appeared — that reads as a confession to be memorized rather than prose to be composed on the fly.

A shared, public formula Supporting

Paul reminds the Corinthians this is what he preached and what all the apostles preach (15:11) — a shared, public formula, not private theology.

An Aramaic substratum? Contested

Several scholars detect an Aramaic substratum beneath the Greek. Joachim Jeremias, who called this passage “the earliest tradition of all,” argued that its Semitic flavor and the Aramaic “Cephas” point back to the Aramaic-speaking church in Jerusalem.

Honest limit: The claim of an Aramaic original is contested. Some scholars, including those who fully accept the formula’s antiquity, think it was composed in Greek among Greek-speaking Jewish Christians, and that “Cephas” alone does not prove an Aramaic Vorlage.

Objection — a single formula, or a patchwork?

The passage shows seams. The tight, rhythmic core — died, buried, raised, “appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve” (vv. 3b-5) — reads as a single fixed unit; but the appearances that follow (to more than five hundred, to James, to all the apostles, vv. 6-7) are looser, and the aside that most of the five hundred “are still alive” is plainly Paul’s own comment, not liturgy. If Paul has enlarged a received core with material of his own, then “the creed” is partly Paul’s composition, and its unity — and so its evidential weight — has been oversold. Sharper still is an apparent seam of fact: the formula says Jesus appeared “to the twelve,” yet by then Judas was dead and Matthias not yet chosen (Acts 1:15-26), leaving only eleven.

Reply

First, the “inconsistency” is a category mistake. “The Twelve” is not a running head-count; it is the fixed name of the apostolic body, and the New Testament uses it that way even where it cannot mean twelve people: John calls Thomas “one of the Twelve” (John 20:24) at a point where Judas is already gone. And the Gospels are perfectly able to count when counting is the point — Matthew and Luke say “the eleven” (Matthew 28:16; Luke 24:33) precisely when eleven is meant. That the resurrection tradition still says “the Twelve” is therefore a deliberate institutional label, not a slip of the tally — the mainstream reading, though a determined skeptic may still call the title idealized rather than reportorial. Second, and decisively, conceding — as some scholars place vv. 6-7 with Paul — that he may have expanded the list beyond the core costs the early-dating argument little, because that argument leans on Cephas, a recipient who sits inside the four-line core (v. 5), not on the appended roll. Whether Paul added the five hundred and James from his own knowledge or received them too, the four-line core he “delivered…as of first importance” was handed to him. A composite text with an early core is still an early core — and the early core is all the argument needs.

The room: Hans Conzelmann defends that core as a genuine pre-Pauline unity, and the standard commentaries generally recognize the formula. Joachim Jeremias called it “the earliest tradition of all.” John S. Kloppenborg wrote a dedicated study of the formula’s pre-Pauline, formulaic character, though he partly dissents on a specifically Aramaic or Jerusalem origin and on the very earliest dating. Even the atheist who presses hardest on the cause of the appearances, Gerd Lüdemann, still dates the core’s elements to within a few years of the cross.
Defending Premise 2

Tracing the chain — Paul’s conversion within a year or three of the cross, his Jerusalem visit three years later to get acquainted with Cephas, when he also saw James the Lord’s brother (Galatians 1:18-19); the tradition itself must predate his receipt of it — dates the creed’s content to within two to five years of the crucifixion, a dating held across the spectrum.

Precision check: The early creed establishes early, sincere, eyewitness-anchored belief in Jesus’ death, burial, and post-mortem appearances. It does not, by itself, prove the resurrection. That the first disciples were convinced they had seen the risen Jesus — and convinced very soon — is a historical datum the evidence supports strongly. That their conviction was true is a further inference, argued on separate grounds. The creed is the indispensable first premise of the resurrection case, not the whole of it.

Counting backward through Paul’s biography Strong

Paul says that “after three years” he went up to Jerusalem “to get acquainted with” Cephas, staying fifteen days, and that he also saw James the Lord’s brother (Galatians 1:18-19). The verb he uses, historēsai, occurs only here in the New Testament and connotes inquiry — getting to know a person and, plausibly, his account. Most historians place the crucifixion around AD 30-33 and Paul’s conversion within a year or three of it, around AD 33-35. His Jerusalem visit, “after three years,” falls around AD 36-38 — and the two men he names there, Cephas and James, are precisely two of the named recipients of appearances in the creed.

Whether Paul first received the formula at his conversion (from the Damascus or Antioch community) or had it confirmed in that face-to-face fortnight in Jerusalem, the tradition itself must predate his receipt of it. That places its formation earlier still — within roughly two to five years of the crucifixion.

The Gallio anchor Firm external peg

These dates are not free-floating estimates: the firmest external anchor for Pauline chronology is the Gallio inscription, a fragmentary rescript of the emperor Claudius discovered at Delphi, which fixes Gallio’s proconsulship of Achaia to about AD 51-52. Because Acts 18:12 has Paul brought before Gallio’s tribunal at Corinth, the inscription dates Gallio and thereby anchors Paul’s stay in Corinth, and the earlier stages of his career — including the Jerusalem visit above — are reckoned backward from that fixed point.

Honest limit: It dates Gallio, and so anchors Paul; it does not by itself date the creed.

Held across the spectrum Hostile concession

This is not a conclusion driven by apologetic motive; it is held across the spectrum. The atheist New Testament scholar Gerd Lüdemann judged that “the elements in the tradition are to be dated to the first two years after the crucifixion of Jesus… not later than three years,” placing the formation of the appearance traditions in the years 30-33. And James D. G. Dunn — a believing but non-apologetic historian, characteristically cautious about how much the earliest evidence can prove — writes of the pre-Pauline formula that it “was formulated as tradition within months of Jesus’ death.”

Honest limit: Dunn’s is the confident outer edge of the few-years window on which the field broadly agrees, not a lone claim beyond it — and his judgment concerns the dating of the formula, not the resurrection itself.

What the early date forecloses The payoff

The standard skeptical move against the resurrection has long been to treat it as legend — a story that grew by accretion over decades, the original facts blurred by time and distance. The creed cuts against that. It shows that the four-part claim — Christ died, was buried, was raised, and appeared — was not a late development but the bedrock confession of the movement from its earliest traceable moment, recited by the very people (Peter, James, “the twelve”) who claimed to be its witnesses. The window is too narrow for legendary embellishment to have done its slow work, and the named, living witnesses functioned as a check.

Honest limit: It proves that the belief is early and the witnesses sincere; it hands the deeper question — what best explains their experiences and the empty tomb — to the wider argument. To claim more is to overplay the hand.

Objection — Ehrman: early belief, but only visions Hardest

Bart Ehrman grants a great deal. He affirms the historical core that the disciples came to believe they had seen Jesus alive, and he himself stresses that for Paul the resurrection was bodily, not merely spiritual. But in How Jesus Became God he argues that “it was visions, and nothing else, that led the first disciples to believe” — perhaps as few as a handful of followers (Peter, Paul, and Mary Magdalene among them) had visionary experiences akin to the well-documented “bereavement visions” in which the grieving perceive a lost loved one, and these were retold and embellished until the tradition spoke of mass appearances.

Reply

Bereavement visions are real and common; one should concede the point: the bare report “they saw him” is, on its face, compatible with hallucination. The reply turns on the details the vision hypothesis must explain away. Hallucinations are, as a rule, private and individual; the creed reports group appearances (“the twelve,” “more than five hundred”), and a collective hallucination of the kind the vision hypothesis needs here — many people independently converging on the same seen-and-heard risen Jesus — has no established clinical basis. (The five hundred are reported only by Paul, not in the Gospels; but Paul’s closeness to the tradition — he had met its named witnesses — is precisely the point, not a weakness.) The hypothesis must also account for the conversion of James, a skeptic during Jesus’ ministry, and of Paul, an active persecutor with no grief-driven longing to see Jesus restored — neither fits the bereavement profile. And it must explain the empty tomb, which many scholars publishing on the question accept as historical (though it is more contested than the appearances), and which a purely visionary belief leaves unaddressed. Ehrman’s account leaves the group appearances, the hostile-witness conversions of James and Paul, and the empty tomb unexplained — which is precisely where it fails as the best explanation of the full set of facts.

Objection — Lüdemann: the same date, a psychological cause

Lüdemann presses the vision reading harder and from inside the early date he himself defends. He concedes — indeed insists — that Peter and the disciples had real experiences in which Jesus “appeared” to them, and that these go back to the earliest period. But he interprets Peter’s experience as a vision triggered by guilt over his denial, and Paul’s as the resolution of a repressed inner conflict.

Reply

The philosopher William Lane Craig, in a detailed critical assessment, argues that Lüdemann’s reconstruction smuggles in a speculative psychoanalysis of figures we cannot put on the couch, and that the “guilt complex” and “Christ complex” he posits are read into the texts rather than out of them. The honest concession is that Lüdemann is right about the date and right that the disciples had experiences they took to be appearances; the dispute is entirely over their cause, and there the psychological mechanism is conjecture, not evidence.

The room: N. T. Wright places this primitive tradition in the mid-30s, reaching behind all our written Gospels. On the point that the creed is early, Jeremias, Conzelmann, Wright, Lüdemann, and Ehrman — believers and atheists alike — agree the creed is genuinely early. Pinchas Lapide, an Orthodox Jewish scholar, accepted the early, pre-Pauline formulaic character of the tradition, and judged the resurrection itself a historical event — while continuing to hold that Jesus was not the Messiah or Lord. Caution: Habermas’s survey of several thousand academic sources on the resurrection is his own running compilation of the published literature, not a randomized poll of scholars, and critics have questioned how the sample was assembled; what is not seriously disputed is the narrower point that the creed is genuinely early.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: Ehrman’s — grant the early date, and an early belief in a vision is still a vision. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"Early belief is not an early event — people believe fast and wrong" Common

Strongest form

Cults form overnight. A creed from year two proves Christians believed quickly — sincerity at speed, not a resurrection.

Reply

Exactly right about what it proves — and that is the argument's job: it forecloses the easy theory that the resurrection was a legend grown ripe over generations. What remains must explain the group appearances, the conversions of James, a skeptic during Jesus' ministry, and of Paul, an active persecutor, and the empty tomb. Fast and wrong has to clear all of that.

"Paul never mentions the empty tomb" Common

Strongest form

The creed says died, buried, raised — no tomb, no women. The empty tomb story came later; Paul's resurrection may have been spiritual.

Reply

'Buried, then raised' in Jewish categories IS an emptied grave — what goes down is what comes up; a 'resurrection' leaving the body in place was a contradiction in Jewish terms. Paul argues the dead are raised bodily for an entire chapter, and his 'spiritual body' (soma pneumatikon) means Spirit-empowered body, not ghost — the same word-pair distinguishes natural and spiritual people in 1 Cor 2, both embodied.

"Received means received by revelation, not from people" Trickier

Strongest form

Paul says in Galatians he received his gospel not from man but through revelation. So the creed came from his vision, and your human chain to Jerusalem collapses.

Reply

Galatians defends the divine authorisation of his gospel-commission; 1 Corinthians 15 uses the formal tradition-transfer pair (received/delivered) for a formula whose content — Cephas, the Twelve, James — is communal data no private vision supplies. And Galatians itself records him spending fifteen days with Cephas, historeo — to inquire of — three years in. The chain is in the same letter the objection quotes.

"The 500 are unverifiable — an apologetic flourish" Trickier

Strongest form

Five hundred anonymous witnesses no one can interview, mentioned nowhere else — it reads like an inflated talking point, which casts doubt on the whole formula.

Reply

Note what Paul adds: 'most of whom are still alive' — in a letter to a church in contact with Jerusalem, that is an invitation to check, the opposite of insulation. Ancient writers did not footnote crowds with names. And the argument never leaned on the 500: the early-dating argument leans on Cephas, a recipient who sits inside the four-line core (v. 5), not on the appended roll.

"According to the Scriptures shows theology drove the story" Hard

Strongest form

The creed says he rose 'according to the Scriptures' — the events were generated from texts like Hosea 6:2, prophecy historicised rather than history remembered.

Reply

Then the inventors chose badly: no clear pre-Christian text predicts a messiah crucified and individually raised on the third day — which is why the disciples are portrayed as not expecting it. Texts-first creates expected events; the resurrection was category-breaking, and the scriptural framing reads as the community scrambling to interpret what had happened — history seeking texts, not texts manufacturing history.

"A creed proves preaching, not facts — garbage in, garbage out" Hard

Strongest form

All you have dated is the church's slogan. Early packaging of a false claim is still a false claim.

Reply

Dating the slogan to year two changes everything about explaining it: the claim is now made in Jerusalem, where the tomb could be visited, by people naming themselves, within living memory of every participant — and it converts the movement's chief persecutor. Late legends are cheap; immediate public claims carry costs — costs these men paid in full. The creed is not the conclusion of the case; it is the timestamp that forces every alternative theory to work in daylight.

The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

The early date of the creed is about as close to a consensus as ancient history offers. What is not seriously disputed is the narrower point: that the creed is genuinely early — on that, believers and atheists alike agree. What is contested is an Aramaic original, and a dedicated study of the formula partly dissents on a specifically Aramaic or Jerusalem origin and on the very earliest dating.

A fairness most defenders skip: The broader survey figure on the resurrection data should be cited carefully: it is Gary Habermas’s own running compilation of the published literature, not a randomized poll of scholars, and critics have questioned how the sample was assembled. And James D. G. Dunn’s “formulated as tradition within months of Jesus’ death” is the confident outer edge of the few-years window on which the field broadly agrees, not a lone claim beyond it.

The bounded verdict: The creed establishes, with a confidence rare in the study of antiquity, that within a few years of the crucifixion the earliest Christians — including named men who claimed to have seen the risen Jesus — were already confessing his death, burial, resurrection, and appearances. It forecloses the easy theory that the resurrection was a legend grown ripe over generations. It does not, on its own, prove that Jesus rose; it proves that the belief is early and the witnesses sincere, and it hands the deeper question — what best explains their experiences and the empty tomb — to the wider argument. And this is what we hold to be true: Jesus of Nazareth was bodily raised from the dead, as this creed confesses, and the wider case — the empty tomb, the failure of the alternatives, the conversions of James and Paul — shows it.

    SK
    Skeptic
    "Every religion's founding miracles grow in the dark between event and writing. Forty years from cross to Gospel is an eternity — plenty of room for legend."
    One strong way to answer — in a 1 Peter 3:15 tone

    "Agreed — legends need dark to grow in. Now watch the lights come on: Paul, writing 25 years out, quotes a formula he received — rabbinic transfer language, wording in part not Paul's own, structured for recitation. He had it, at the latest, when he went up to Jerusalem three years after his conversion to get acquainted with Cephas, and also saw James the Lord's brother. The tradition itself must predate his receipt of it, so the resurrection proclamation — died, buried, raised, appeared, witnesses named — is fixed within two to five years of the cross. The atheist scholar Lüdemann says two. There is no forty-year dark room. The claim was public, in the execution city, while everyone involved could be cross-examined — and the man reciting it used to arrest people for it."

    Gold gate · the unseen objection

    One you weren’t coached on

    Everything above rehearsed objections the page showed you. Here is the real test of mastery: an objection we deliberately did not drill. Reason it out cold from what the argument established, then reveal how it actually handles it. An honest “nailed it” is the first half of Gold.

    OM
    Online mythicist
    "Fine, the creed is early. But its “appearances” were revelatory visions of a celestial being who was never on earth. The verb “appeared” (ōphthē) is the language of revelation, and the creed mentions no one seeing Jesus before his death."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    Answer it on the texts, not with a label — and it is the texts that defeat it. The same Paul who recites the creed names Jesus’ brother James as a man he met — “James the Lord’s brother” (Galatians 1:19) — and speaks of Jesus as “born of woman, born under the law” (Galatians 4:4). A flesh-and-blood brother whom Paul personally met, and a birth from a woman under the law, are exactly what a celestial-only Jesus cannot accommodate. Nor can “brother” be the generic title for any believer: Paul lists “the other apostles and the brothers of the Lord and Cephas” (1 Corinthians 9:5), setting the Lord’s brothers apart as a distinct group. As for ōphthē: it is indeed the standard Septuagint verb for divine appearances, but it is also used of ordinary appearing (Acts 7:26, of Moses appearing to two quarreling Israelites), and Paul marks his own experience as the exception rather than the pattern — “last of all, as to one untimely born” (15:8), closing the list rather than typifying it.
