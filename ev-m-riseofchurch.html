How the Church Took Over the Empire

The deep dive tells you what the argument is. This track makes you able to use it — reconstruct it from memory, answer its hardest objections, and hold the line in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

In the year 30 the followers of a crucified Galilean numbered perhaps a few hundred, in one corner of one province, with no wealth, no standing, and no army. Two and a half centuries later, with the faith still illegal and two imperial attempts to suppress it already spent, the state gave up and legalised them. That is a strange thing to have happened, and both the pious and the sceptical accounts of it say more than the evidence permits.

The popular sceptical answer is one word — Constantine — and it is false as an account of the period that matters. The church became too large to suppress before the state had any part in it: a Roman governor describes a spreading movement around AD 112, a hostile satirist its organised charity around 165, a bishop’s relief roll of 251 lists over 1,500 dependants, and two persecutions failed with the machinery of empire engaged. The mechanism the sources describe was unglamorous — care of the sick at cost, refusal to expose infants, mutual aid, conversion along household networks.

But the scope must be kept honest, and this track keeps it honest throughout. Growth is not truth. A movement can spread because it met real needs and still be mistaken — Stark himself notes that his assumed rate is close to the documented twentieth-century growth of the Latter-day Saints, so an exponential curve of this shape is not unique to Christianity, nor by itself evidence of anything supernatural. So this argument establishes that the imperial-power story is false and that the growth was real and costly; it does not establish that Christianity is true. That case rests on the resurrection and is made elsewhere in the Library.

Before you start · key terms

Eight words this argument leans on

Learn these first — the objections below turn on them, and most weak versions of the argument come from blurring one of them.

The compound-growth model (Stark)The sociologist Rodney Stark’s way of defusing the apparent miracle in the numbers: assume the church grew at a constant compound rate, anchor it to a starting figure and an endpoint historians roughly accept, and see what the exponential curve produces. Begin with about a thousand Christians in the year 40 and let the movement grow at 40 per cent per decade — a little under three and a half per cent a year — and you reach something over a tenth of a sixty-million empire by 300. Forty per cent a decade is not a stampede; it is what you get if, over a decade, roughly two or three believers together bring in one new person — no mass conversions, no coercion. Stark said plainly it is a model, not a census; every figure in every book is an estimate built from fragments.
Hostile-witness attestationThe best evidence that the church was growing comes not from Christians, who had every reason to advertise their success, but from officials and critics who found the movement irritating, contemptible, or alarming — and described it anyway. Pliny the Younger (c. 112) reports the “contagion of this superstition” spreading through cities, villages, and countryside; Lucian of Samosata (c. 165), who thought Christians credulous fools, describes their organised, costly support for a jailed leader. The contempt is what makes the testimony valuable: a hostile source is not building a case for the church when it confirms the church was organised, generous, and spreading.
The Constantine objectionThe standard sceptical reply, stated at its strongest because part of it is simply accurate: the real cause of Christianity’s success is not the mechanisms of the movement but a single political event. In 312 Constantine won at the Milvian Bridge and credited the Christian God; in 313 the settlement loosely called the Edict of Milan ended persecution; imperial patronage followed; and in 380 Theodosius made Nicene Christianity official, with the coercive power of the state behind it. On this telling, Christianity did not win the Roman world — it was handed it. The reply is not to deny it but to date it: it explains the fourth century, and reverses cause and effect for the stretch before 312.
Galerius’s edict of toleration (April 311)The decisive fact about timing. The state’s own attempts to destroy the church had already failed with the full machinery of empire engaged — Decius’s universal sacrifice order of 250, Valerian’s edicts of 257 and 258, and Diocletian’s persecution from 303 were all spent, and the last was formally abandoned by Galerius in the edict of toleration of April 311: a full year before the Milvian Bridge (though Maximinus Daia soon renewed it in the eastern provinces he ruled until his defeat in 313). The state conceded first. By the time Constantine acted, the church already reached into the army and the palace and numbered in the millions.
Network conversionStark’s most durable contribution, sociological rather than statistical: new religious movements grow overwhelmingly along existing relationships — family, household, trade, friendship — rather than through public preaching to strangers. The book of Acts shows exactly this, converting whole households at a stroke (Cornelius, Lydia, the Philippian jailer, Crispus) alongside the public preaching it also records. It is an unglamorous mechanism, and it is precisely what produces the steady compounding the arithmetic describes: not a wave, but a web, thickening decade by decade — the faith spreading the way trust spreads, one relationship at a time.
A wider door than anyone else offeredLeaving an unwanted newborn outdoors to die or be taken was legal and unremarkable in the Greco-Roman world; Christian texts forbid it from their earliest layer (the Didache bans it in the same breath as murder; the Epistle to Diognetus singles out Christians who “do not destroy their offspring”). The same communities admitted, without a bar of status, the categories the surrounding cults ranked and rationed — slaves, the poor, women, and non-Greeks. The claim must be kept bounded: careful specialists such as Lynn Cohick show Greco-Roman women had wider scope than the old caricature allowed, so the defensible point is the narrower, sturdier one — the communities were unusually mixed, and membership was not priced by rank.
Growth is not truth (the firewall)The load-bearing honest limit, kept distinct from everything else. The rapid, illegal, person-to-person spread of the church shows that the faith was profoundly attractive and that its people were utterly convinced — convinced enough to nurse plague victims at the cost of their lives and to keep confessing the name when a pinch of incense would have bought their safety. But it is not, by itself, proof that the gospel is true, and the argument does not offer it as one: movements can spread quickly and still be mistaken; popularity is not a truth-detector. Stark himself notes his assumed rate is close to the twentieth-century growth of the Latter-day Saints, so a curve of this shape is not unique to Christianity. The case that Jesus actually rose rests on different evidence — the early eyewitness creed of 1 Corinthians 15:3–7 — made elsewhere in the Library.
Julian’s unwilling tribute (AD 362)The sharpest testimony to what had driven the growth, from the one man with the most reason to deny it — and its date must be stated, not blurred, because it is not evidence from the illegal centuries. It comes fifty years after Constantine. In 362 the emperor Julian, raised Christian and the only emperor after Constantine to abandon the faith, set out to restore the old gods, and diagnosed that the Christians were winning because they out-behaved the pagans: when the pagan priesthood neglected the poor, “the impious Galilaeans” devoted themselves to philanthropy and so “led very many into atheism,” and his remedy was to urge his own priests to copy the charity he had set out to defeat. The quotation is grounded only in the undisputed Fragment of a Letter to a Priest; the more famous Letter to Arsacius, whose genuineness Van Nuffelen has challenged, is not relied on. Julian witnesses what the church’s enemy judged he had to beat — not why it grew in the second and third centuries.
The argument, formally

Three lines. The whole weight rests on the two premises.

P1

The church grew from a few hundred to a substantial minority of the empire within three centuries, while the faith was illegal and carried no political advantage — and the charity at the heart of that growth is attested even by hostile witnesses.

P2

This growth is far better explained by the movement’s own resources — its care of strangers, its networks, the conviction behind it — than by imperial power, which arrived only after the church was already too large to suppress.

∴ C

Therefore the claim that Christianity owes its position to the state is false for the period that matters — though this establishes that the growth was real and unassisted by the state, not that the faith is true.

This is an inference to the best explanation, and its conclusion is deliberately bounded: it answers the “Constantine did it” story for the stretch before 312, and it stops there. It is not an argument that Christianity is true — growth never shows that.

Then the honest step — what growth does and does not show

A religion’s success shows it met needs and held loyalty; it never shows its claims are true. Keep the two apart.

Defending Premise 1

The church grew from a few hundred to a substantial minority of the empire within three centuries, while the faith was illegal and carried no political advantage — and the charity at the heart of that growth is attested even by hostile witnesses

Precision check: the claim is about the shape of the growth, not a census. Nobody counted, so every figure is an estimate built from fragments; Stark’s figure is a model, not a census, and Stark said so plainly. What is not in dispute is the shape: a standing start and steep, sustained growth, achieved while the faith was illegal. That is astonishing enough without inflating it.

Pliny, c. AD 112 — an administrator’s alarm Strong · external

Pliny the Younger, governing Bithynia-Pontus, wrote to the emperor Trajan for guidance on prosecuting Christians. His letter is the earliest surviving administrative description of the church, and his alarm is that of a manager, not a theologian: the “contagion of this superstition” has spread not only through the cities but through the villages and countryside, reaching people of every age and rank and both sexes, so that the temples had stood nearly deserted before his crackdown began.

Honest limit: A governor has an interest in magnifying the problem he is solving. But the letter is a private request for guidance, not a victory bulletin, and the reason Pliny gives for troubling the emperor is the sheer number of people at risk; that is why the numbers he saw are best taken as real.

Lucian, c. AD 165 — a satirist who thought them credulous fools Strong · external

Lucian of Samosata described Christians swarming to support a jailed leader: bribing the guards to sit with him, sending delegates at collective expense from the cities of Asia, sparing no trouble and no money — which is precisely why, he sneers, any competent charlatan could grow rich among them. The contempt is what makes the testimony valuable. Lucian is not praising the behaviour but explaining how exploitable it made them — and in the process he confirms, from outside and a century and a half before Constantine, that the movement was organised, generous, and spreading.

Cornelius of Rome, c. AD 251 — a relief roll from an illegal church Strong · an internal record, not a boast

Writing about 251, Cornelius, bishop of Rome, listed his church’s establishment for a colleague: forty-six presbyters, seven deacons, and more than fifteen hundred widows and persons in distress supported by the congregation. That is a relief roll, not a boast — a single city’s illegal church already carrying fifteen hundred dependants. Whatever else the church was by the mid-third century, it was large, structured, and pouring its resources into the poor.

The state’s own attempts to destroy it had already failed Strong

Pliny’s letter and Lucian’s satire describe a spreading, organised movement in the second century, when no emperor favoured it. Cornelius’s relief roll is an administrative document from an illegal church in 251. And — this is decisive — the state’s own attempts to destroy the church had already failed with the full machinery of empire engaged. Decius’s universal sacrifice order of 250, Valerian’s edicts of 257 and 258, and Diocletian’s persecution from 303 were all spent, and the last was formally abandoned by Galerius in the edict of toleration of April 311 — a full year before the Milvian Bridge, though Maximinus Daia soon renewed it in the eastern provinces he ruled until his defeat in 313.

The arithmetic that defuses the miracle Model · not a census

The numbers do not require anything miraculous, forced, or even unusual. They require only steady, ordinary, person-to-person growth — kept up for a very long time. Stark’s method is deliberately plain: assume a constant compound rate of 40 per cent per decade, anchor it to a starting figure and an endpoint historians roughly accept, and see what the exponential curve produces. Forty per cent a decade is not a stampede; it is what you get if, over a decade, roughly two or three believers together bring in one new person. There is no need for mass conversions or coercion.

Honest limit: Estimates for AD 300 run from Ehrman’s ~2.5–3 million to Stark’s ~6.3 million — about four to ten per cent of the empire. Stark’s figure is a model, not a count, and Hopkins (often cited as independent) actually adopts Stark’s arithmetic. Ehrman also warns that the rate cannot simply be extended, since running it on to the year 400 yields more Christians than the empire had inhabitants. The range is the honest measure; the shape — a steep, sustained rise while illegal — is not in dispute.

Objection — “Constantine made Christianity the state religion — that is why it won”

In 312 Constantine won at the Milvian Bridge and credited the Christian God; in 313 he and Licinius agreed the settlement conventionally called the Edict of Milan, ending persecution; imperial patronage followed; and in 380 Theodosius made Nicene Christianity the empire’s official religion, with the coercive power of the state behind it. On this telling, Christianity did not win the Roman world — it was handed it.

Reply

The concession owed here is substantial. Everything after 312 is entangled with state power, and the church’s growth after 380 with the legal suppression of rival worship. The steepest part of the curve in raw numbers is the fourth century; anyone who points to the Christian majority of AD 400 as proof of the gospel’s persuasive force is trading on a period when the state’s thumb was on the scale. But the objection explains the wrong stretch of the curve. By the time Constantine acted, the church already reached into the army and the palace and numbered in the millions; his patronage recognised and accelerated a movement already too big to suppress. The Constantine story does not explain the growth before 312 — for that stretch it reverses cause and effect.

Objection — “Nobody counted: the numbers are just a model”

There was never any count of Christians in the empire, and every figure in every book is an estimate built from fragments. The scholarly agreement is thinner than it can look: Keith Hopkins, often cited as independent confirmation, is not — he takes Stark’s arithmetic as his starting point.

Reply

Granted, and the track says so: the honest summary is a range — roughly two and a half to six million by 300, unevenly spread and concentrated in the cities. What is not in dispute is the shape: a standing start and steep, sustained growth, achieved while the faith was illegal. That is astonishing enough without inflating it.

The room: Rodney Stark models the growth and says plainly it is a model; Keith Hopkins adopts Stark’s arithmetic rather than independently confirming it; Bart Ehrman puts the year-300 figure closer to two and a half or three million — less than half Stark’s — and warns the rate cannot simply be extended. Pliny and Lucian are hostile witnesses; Cornelius’s list is an internal administrative record — ‘a relief roll, not a boast.’
Defending Premise 2

This growth is far better explained by the movement’s own resources — its care of strangers, its networks, the conviction behind it — than by imperial power, which arrived only after the church was already too large to suppress

Precision check: an inference to the best explanation, deliberately bounded. No single cause explains three centuries across a whole empire, and none of these mechanisms is unique in kind — the claim is about their combination and cost. It answers the “Constantine did it” story for the stretch before 312, and it stops there. It is not an argument that Christianity is true — growth never shows that.

Care for the sick, at real cost Well attested · the mortality model contested

Twice the empire was gutted by epidemics — the Antonine plague from about 165, and the plague named for Cyprian from about 249 — and ancient cities had no capacity for mass nursing. Bishop Dionysius of Alexandria, writing of the second outbreak in a letter preserved by the historian Eusebius, describes Christians staying with the dying, taking their care upon themselves, and perishing in numbers as a result, while frightened neighbours abandoned their own.

Honest limit: It is a Christian source describing Christian conduct, and should be weighed as such — though flight from plague was ordinary enough that pagan sources record it too, so the contrast is not invented. Stark’s much-quoted claim is that this was not merely admirable but demographically consequential; that model rests on modern estimates applied to diseases we cannot securely identify, so it is a plausible argument rather than a proven result. But the behaviour is well attested, and it is the sort of thing that changes how a neighbour thinks.

A wider door than anyone else offered Bounded claim

Leaving an unwanted newborn outdoors to die or be taken was legal and unremarkable in the Greco-Roman world. Christian texts forbid it from their earliest layer: the Didache bans abortion and the killing of a newborn in the same breath as murder, and the Epistle to Diognetus singles out Christians as people who “marry, as do all… but they do not destroy their offspring.” The same communities admitted, without a bar of status, the categories the surrounding cults ranked and rationed — slaves, the poor, women, and non-Greeks — so that a slave and a matron could belong to the same congregation on the same terms.

Honest limit: This point is easy to overstate, and careful specialists such as Lynn Cohick have shown that Greco-Roman women had wider social and economic scope than the older caricature allowed. So the defensible claim is the narrower, sturdier one: Christian communities were unusually mixed, and membership was not priced by rank.

Household and trade networks Stark’s most durable contribution · a sociological model

New religious movements grow overwhelmingly along existing relationships — family, household, trade, friendship — rather than through public preaching to strangers. The book of Acts shows exactly this, converting whole households at a stroke — Cornelius, Lydia, the Philippian jailer, Crispus — alongside the public preaching it also records. It is an unglamorous mechanism, and it is precisely what produces the steady compounding the arithmetic describes: not a wave, but a web, thickening decade by decade.

The witness of a hostile emperor — AD 362, not evidence for the earlier growth Late · a tribute, not a cause

In 362 the emperor Julian — raised Christian, and the only emperor after Constantine to abandon the faith — holding the whole apparatus of the state and trying to restore paganism, blamed pagan decline on the priesthood’s neglect of the poor and on Christian care for strangers, and urged his own priests to imitate it (the undisputed Fragment of a Letter to a Priest; the often-quoted line that Christians fed the pagans’ poor as well as their own belongs to the disputed Letter to Arsacius and is not relied on here).

Honest limit: Julian is therefore not a witness to why the church grew in the second and third centuries — he is far too late for that, and it must not be pretended otherwise. He is a hostile emperor judging Christian charity the thing he had to beat, and setting out to copy it.

Objection — MacMullen: power, not charity

The leading dissenting historian, Ramsay MacMullen, argued that conversion in this period turned less on charity than on public demonstrations of power — healings and exorcisms a pagan could read as a straight contest between gods.

Reply

His is a rival account of the mechanism — one that discounts charity in favour of displays of power — and it is answered here only in part. The two accounts are not exclusive, since the charity is independently attested (by Lucian’s sneer and Cornelius’s relief roll, and by Dionysius), whatever weight one gives the exorcisms. And MacMullen’s account is no help to the Constantine objection, because whatever exorcism was, it was not imperial power.

Objection — “It grew fast, so it must be true” Hardest

A movement that spreads this quickly, against this much resistance, must have had the truth on its side — why else would people risk everything for it? (The sceptic’s own best move runs the other way: other movements grew fast too, so “it spread” is no evidence of truth.)

Reply

This one cuts against us, and we grant it: growth is not truth. People risk much for beliefs that turn out false, and fast growth is not unique to Christianity — Stark observed that his rate is close to the documented twentieth-century growth of the Latter-day Saints, an exponential curve of the same shape — a plausibility check on a rate already chosen, not an input to it. The rise of the church shows the movement was compelling and costly to join; whether its central claim is true rests on the resurrection, argued elsewhere.

The room: Stark argues for networks and care and models the growth; Ramsay MacMullen presses power over charity and is the leading dissenting historian — his is a rival mechanism, not a rival to the thesis, since whatever else it was, it was not imperial power. Lynn Cohick keeps the “wider door” claim bounded. Julian, a hostile emperor, is a late witness to what he had to beat, not to what caused the earlier growth.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

This is what separates someone who knows how the early church grew from someone who can defend it. Each gets its best form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged below.

The one that matters most: the sceptic’s best move is not to deny the growth but to say it proves nothing — other movements grew fast too, so “it spread” is no evidence of truth. That is correct, and this track concedes it in our own voice. The argument’s job is narrower: to refute the claim that the state did it.

“Constantine made Christianity the state religion — that is why it won” Common

Strongest form

The state gave up first.

“The numbers are just an apologist’s guess” Trickier

Strongest form

The six-million figure is a model built on an assumed growth rate, not a census. You can make it say whatever you assume.

Reply

True, and the track says so: Stark’s table is a model, its author said so, and it cannot even be extended past 350 without exceeding the empire’s population. That is exactly why the honest answer is a range — roughly 2.5 to 6.3 million — and why the case does not rest on the numbers at all, but on the datable, hostile, pre-Constantinian evidence that a large organised movement already existed.

“Christianity spread by the sword, like any other power” Trickier

Strongest form

Religions grow by coercion. Once Christianity had the state, it forced conversions — so its spread tells us nothing admirable.

Reply

For the fourth century onward there is real force to indict, and this track does not whitewash it. But for the period in question — the first three centuries, when the church became too large to suppress — Christians held no sword and no legal advantage; they were the ones being prosecuted. Coercion is a story that begins in the fourth century, and it is the church’s legacy, not its rise, that has to answer for it.

The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

What this argument establishes is bounded, and the track keeps it honest throughout. It establishes two things, and no more: that the popular “Constantine did it” story is false as an account of the period that matters — the church became too large to suppress before the state had any part in it — and that the growth was real and costly, run on care of the sick, refusal to expose infants, mutual aid, and conversion along household networks, with the charity at its heart attested even by hostile witnesses. The numbers themselves stay a range: estimates for AD 300 run from Ehrman’s two and a half or three million to Stark’s six-point-three million, a model and not a census. What the argument does not establish is that Christianity is true; growth never shows that.

A fairness most defenders skip: The scope is kept honest by naming the critics who keep it so. Bart Ehrman puts the year-300 figure at less than half Stark’s and warns the constant rate cannot simply be extended, since running it to 400 yields more Christians than the empire had inhabitants. Keith Hopkins, often cited as independent confirmation, is not — he adopts Stark’s endpoints and arithmetic, so he corroborates the method, not the conclusion. Ramsay MacMullen, the leading dissenting historian, argues conversion turned less on charity than on displays of power (healings and exorcisms); his is a rival account of the mechanism, answered here only in part, and no help to the Constantine objection, since whatever exorcism was, it was not imperial power. Lynn Cohick keeps the “wider door” claim bounded. Stark’s most contested element — that nursing was demographically consequential — rests on modern estimates applied to diseases we cannot securely identify, and is weighed as an argument, not a demonstrated result.

The bounded verdict: So the honest takeaway is not a boast but a recognition. A tiny, powerless, illegal movement outlasted the empire that tried to kill it — not by force, which in those centuries it never had, but by costly behaviour its enemies could see and its first opponents set out to copy. The claim that Christianity owes its position to the state is false for the period that matters: the state conceded first, and Constantine recognised a movement already too big to suppress. That is astonishing enough without inflating it — and it is deliberately not the claim that the faith is true. The behaviours that carried the church across the empire were what Jesus had told his followers to do, done at cost by people who mostly left no names, because they believed he had been raised. The growth is exactly what you would expect if that resurrection claim were true; it is not a substitute for the argument that it is, which rests on the resurrection and is made elsewhere in the Library.

    SK
    Skeptic
    "Your own best evidence sinks you. Dionysius says the Christians who nursed the dying perished in numbers as a result. So if anything, plague should have thinned the Christians faster than the pagans who fled — the care-for-the-sick mechanism is self-defeating, and it cannot have fuelled the growth at all."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    The behaviour is what matters, and it is well attested; the demographic claim built on top of it is exactly the part the track marks as contested, not load-bearing. Stark’s much-quoted move is that costly nursing was not merely admirable but demographically consequential: even elementary nursing can cut mortality sharply, so tended communities lost fewer of their own and won the loyalty of the strangers they had nursed — a net effect that holds even though some carers died. But that model rests on modern estimates applied to diseases we cannot securely identify, and Dionysius’s own report that the carers died illustrates the tension, so it should be weighed as a plausible argument rather than a demonstrated result. Crucially, the argument does not depend on it. What is securely attested is the conduct — Christians staying with the dying while frightened neighbours abandoned their own — and it is the sort of thing that changes how a neighbour thinks. Even if the differential-mortality mechanism is set aside entirely, the case still stands on the datable, hostile, pre-Constantinian evidence of a large organised movement; and the costly behaviour itself is what the church’s own enemies — Lucian, and later Julian — could see and judged they had to reckon with.
