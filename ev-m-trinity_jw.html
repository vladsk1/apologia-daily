Answering the Watchtower

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and stay clear and calm in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

Jehovah's Witnesses (the Watchtower Bible and Tract Society) reject the Trinity. They teach that only the Father, Jehovah, is truly God, that Jesus is God's first and greatest creation (identified with the archangel Michael), and that the Holy Spirit is God's impersonal active force rather than a person. Their New World Translation reflects this, rendering John 1:1 as "the Word was a god." Witnesses hold this sincerely, believing they are defending God's uniqueness, and the conversation should honour that earnestness.

The historic Christian response engages the key texts directly and respectfully. In John 1:1, the grammar (a predicate noun preceding the verb) indicates that the Word is fully divine in nature, not "a god" among others; reputable Greek scholarship overwhelmingly reads it as a statement of the Word's deity. Other passages weigh in too: Thomas calls the risen Jesus "my Lord and my God" (John 20:28) without rebuke, and Colossians 1:16-17 says all things were created "through him and for him," which is hard to square with his being a creature himself.

The aim is not to win an argument but to think carefully together. The conclusion should be stated plainly: the texts the Watchtower cites point the other way — the Son is the uncreated Creator, not the first creature. That is one load-bearing piece of the historic Trinity — one God, eternally Father, Son and Holy Spirit; the personhood of the Spirit is a separate case. Pursue it patiently, text by text.

Defending Premise 1

Jehovah’s Witnesses teach that Jesus is Jehovah’s first creation (in his prehuman life, Michael the archangel) — resting chiefly on the New World Translation’s John 1:1 (‘a god’) and on ‘firstborn of all creation’ (Col 1:15).

Precision check: this premise is a description, and a fair hearing means giving the argument its best form before answering it. Jehovah’s Witnesses are not vaguely “non-Trinitarian.” They hold a specific and structured Christology. The question between us is therefore precise. Not “is Jesus important?” but “is Jesus a creature or the Creator?”

What the Watchtower actually teaches Their case, stated fairly

Only the Father, whom they call by the name Jehovah, is God in the full sense. The Son is God’s first and greatest creation — the one being Jehovah made directly, who then served as the agent through whom everything else was made. Before his earthly life this Son was, they teach, the archangel Michael; he became the man Jesus, died, and was raised as a spirit creature. The Holy Spirit is not a person at all but God’s impersonal “active force.”

Honest limit: Historically this is a form of Arianism — the view, named for the fourth-century Alexandrian presbyter Arius, that the Son is the highest of creatures but a creature nonetheless, so that, in the slogan the Council of Nicaea condemned in 325, “there was when he was not.” Witnesses do not embrace the label, and there are differences in detail; but the structural claim is the same one Athanasius and the Nicene fathers contested. Against it the historic church confesses that the Son is homoousios — of the same nature as the Father — coequal, coeternal, and consubstantial: one God in three persons.

John 1:1 — “a god” Their flagship text

The flagship is the New World Translation’s rendering of John 1:1: “the Word was a god.” The reasoning is grammatical, not arbitrary. In the third clause of John 1:1, the Greek word theos (“God”) lacks the definite article, whereas the second clause — “the Word was with God” — has it (ton theon). Greek can use the article to mark definiteness; so, the argument runs, the anarthrous theos in clause three should be read indefinitely, “a god,” preserving the distinction between the Father (ho theos) and the Word, who is divine in a lesser, derived sense.

Honest limit: The absence of the article is a real feature of the text, and it is doing real work. The article-less theos does safeguard a distinction — the Word is not the person of the Father. A Witness who insists that clause three cannot mean “the Word is identical to the Father” is correct, and historic orthodoxy agrees: that error has its own ancient name, modalism.

“Firstborn,” “beginning,” and Wisdom Their case, stated fairly

The second pillar is Colossians 1:15, which calls Christ “the firstborn of all creation.” On the plain reading, the firstborn of a group belongs to that group: the firstborn of the flock is a sheep, the firstborn of Egypt is an Egyptian. So the firstborn of all creation is the first and chief created thing — exactly the Watchtower’s Christ. Revelation 3:14 is read as confirmation: Jesus is “the beginning of the creation by God” (NWT), where the Greek archē is taken to mean that he is the first thing God brought into being. Proverbs 8, where personified Wisdom says “the LORD created me as the beginning of his ways” (8:22, in the Greek Septuagint), is read as a portrait of the pre-incarnate Christ, and so as further evidence that the Son was created.

Honest limit: Colossians 1:15 looks, at first glance, like the Watchtower’s strongest verse, and its plain-sense force should be granted: “firstborn of X” can mean the first member of the group X. But Greek prōtotokos, like the Hebrew bekhor behind it, carries a second and very common meaning — preeminence, the rank and inheritance rights of the firstborn heir — and which sense is in play is settled by context, not by the bare phrase.

“The Father is greater than I,” and the Michael inference Their case, stated fairly

Third comes Jesus’s own statement in John 14:28: “the Father is greater than I.” To this the Witness adds the whole texture of Jesus’s dependence — he prays, he obeys, he is sent, he confesses he does not know the day or the hour (Mark 13:32), he calls the Father “my God” (John 20:17). Finally there is the identification of Jesus with Michael the archangel, inferred from 1 Thessalonians 4:16 (the Lord descends “with an archangel’s voice”) and Revelation 12:7 (Michael leads the armies of heaven, as the returning Christ does). Knit together, these strands yield the Watchtower’s picture: an exalted, pre-existent, mighty Son who is nonetheless Jehovah’s creature and servant, not Jehovah himself. It is this picture — not a caricature of it — that a good answer must engage.

Objection — “Witnesses aren’t Arians”

Witnesses do not embrace the label, and there are differences in detail.

Reply

There are differences in detail; but the structural claim is the same one Athanasius and the Nicene fathers contested: the Son is the highest of creatures but a creature nonetheless. Against it the historic church confesses that the Son is homoousios — of the same nature as the Father — coequal, coeternal, and consubstantial: one God in three persons.

The room: The Witness at your door is, in most cases, sincere, biblically literate by the standards of the average churchgoer, and genuinely trying to honor the oneness of God. Historically the structural claim is the one Arius advanced and Nicaea condemned in 325. The essay’s own statement of the Watchtower case draws on the 1989 brochure Should You Believe in the Trinity? and on Reasoning from the Scriptures.
Defending Premise 2

The position fails three independent tests: grammar (the construction’s normal force is qualitative, not indefinite — the Word is everything God is), logic (John 1:3 — ALL created things came through the Word, so the Word is uncreated; Col 1:16-17 makes the same move), and worship (Jesus receives the worship Revelation’s angels explicitly refuse, and Thomas’s ‘my Lord and my God’ is blessed, not corrected).

Precision check: these arguments establish something narrower than “the Trinity, proved.” They defend one load-bearing piece of that structure — that the Son is the uncreated Creator, not the first creature — against the specific texts the Watchtower marshals against it. The full doctrine of the Trinity is built from the whole of Scripture: the deity of the Son, the deity and personhood of the Spirit, the distinction of the three, and the oneness of God, held together.

Test 1 — grammar

The construction is qualitative Strong

The problem is the inference from “no article” to “therefore indefinite, therefore ‘a god.’” That inference does not hold in Greek. The decisive work was done by Philip B. Harner, in a 1973 article in the Journal of Biblical Literature. Harner examined anarthrous predicate nouns that precede the verb — exactly the construction in John 1:1c — and argued that their primary force is usually qualitative: they describe the nature of the subject rather than identifying or counting it. On his analysis the clause means that “the logos… had the nature of theos,” and he judged the qualitative force here “so prominent that the noun cannot be regarded as definite.” Daniel B. Wallace, in the standard intermediate grammar, reaches the same verdict: in John 1:1c theos is neither definite nor indefinite but qualitative — the Word possesses the full nature of deity that the Father possesses, while remaining personally distinct from him.

This is why “a god” is the one reading the construction will not bear. An indefinite “a god” makes the Word one divine being among a possible plurality — polytheism — from the pen of an author whose entire framework is Jewish monotheism. The qualitative reading, which affirms the Word’s full deity, is the settled judgment of the standard reference grammars, and even were one to retreat to a purely qualitative paraphrase — “the Word was fully divine in nature” — the created-angel Christology has already lost. One who shares the very nature of God is not a creature.

Honest limit: Colwell’s rule does not run in reverse. The 1933 study by Ernest Cadman Colwell is often invoked here — he observed that definite predicate nouns preceding the verb usually lack the article — but it is essential not to overstate it, as some apologists have. Colwell’s rule describes what happens to nouns already known to be definite; it does not run in reverse to prove that an anarthrous pre-verbal noun must be definite. Leaning on Colwell to prove “the Word was the God” is a genuine overreach, and an attentive Witness will catch it.

The NWT’s own inconsistency Limited · theology-driven, not rule-driven

Be precise here, because the popular version of this point is wrong and a trained Witness will say so: John 1:6, 1:12 and 1:13 are anarthrous genitives, not preverbal predicate nominatives, so they are not the identical construction. What is true, and enough, is that the NWT renders article-less theos as ‘God’ at 1:6, 1:12 and 1:13, and reaches for ‘a god’ only at 1:1c. That is not a grammatical rule applied consistently; it is a rendering chosen where doctrine requires it. At 1:18 the NWT prints ‘the only-begotten god,’ lower case — say so before the Witness does. The Greek text the NWT translates prints monogenēs theos — theos again without the article, though not in the construction of 1:1c — and the NWT renders it “the only-begotten god,” lower case, in the same verse in which it has just printed “God” of the Father, where the Greek is equally without the article.

Honest limit: Witnesses rightly reply that context, not a mechanical rule, governs each verse; grant it. So this point decides no single verse; its force is that ‘a god’ is theology-driven rather than rule-driven. What rules it out is John’s monotheism and verse 3. The weight of the case is carried by the monotheistic context of John 1:1 — saturated in creation and one-God language — which is what rules “a god” out.

Test 2 — logic

“Firstborn” means rank — and “for by him all things were created” Strong

Greek prōtotokos, like the Hebrew bekhor behind it, carries a second and very common meaning — preeminence, the rank and inheritance rights of the firstborn heir — and which sense is in play is settled by context, not by the bare phrase. In Psalm 89:27 God says of David — who was the youngest, the last-born, of Jesse’s sons — “I will make him the firstborn, the highest of the kings of the earth.” Here “firstborn” cannot mean “born first”; it means supreme in rank. Likewise Israel, a nation that did not exist at the dawn of creation, is called God’s “firstborn” (Exodus 4:22). The word is a title of status.

Paul answers it himself in the next breath, and this is where the Watchtower reading breaks. Colossians 1:16–17 gives the reason for the title, opening with “for” (Greek hoti): “for by him all things were created, in heaven and on earth… all things were created through him and for him. And he is before all things, and in him all things hold together.” The firstborn is the one through whom, and for whom, all created things exist — and a being for whom all things were made, and who holds all things together, is on the Creator’s side of the line, not among the things created.

The tell: To make Christ a creature while keeping “all things were created through him” coherent, the New World Translation inserts the word “other” in verses 16–20 — “all other things” — so that Christ creates everything except himself. But “other” (Greek alla) is not in the Greek text at those points, as the Watchtower’s own Kingdom Interlinear shows in its word-for-word column. The inserted word is the tell: the unaltered text places Christ outside the category of created things, and a word had to be added to put him back in.

Revelation 3:14 and Proverbs 8 Supporting · resist over-reading

Revelation 3:14 calls Christ “the beginning of God’s creation” (Greek hē archē tēs ktiseōs tou theou). The Watchtower reads archē as “the first created thing.” But archē ordinarily means “origin, source, ruler, first cause” — it is the root of English words like “architect” and “monarch.” Most translators and lexicographers take it actively here: Christ is the source or origin of creation, its active beginning, not its first item. This fits the rest of Revelation, where the same Christ is “the Alpha and the Omega, the first and the last, the beginning and the end” (Revelation 22:13) — and “the first and the last” is a title Isaiah reserves for Yahweh alone (Isaiah 44:6). One does not call a creature the Alpha.

Honest limit: Proverbs 8 is subtler, and here the honest answer is to resist over-reading in either direction. The Hebrew verb in 8:22 (qanah) is disputed and is often rendered “possessed” or “acquired” rather than “created.” More important, Proverbs 8 is a poem personifying an attribute of God — his wisdom in ordering the world — and most modern interpreters, across the theological spectrum, doubt that it was intended as a literal description of a pre-incarnate person at all. The New Testament nowhere cites Proverbs 8:22 to teach that the Son was created; that move is the reader’s, not the text’s.

Is Jesus Michael the archangel? Strong

The identification of Jesus with Michael is an inference, and the inference is weaker than it first appears. That the Lord descends “with the voice of an archangel” (1 Thessalonians 4:16) no more makes him an archangel than his descending in the same verse “with the sound of the trumpet of God” makes him a trumpet; the preposition signals accompaniment, not identity. In Jude 9, the archangel Michael, disputing with the devil over the body of Moses, “did not presume to pronounce a blasphemous judgment, but said, ‘The Lord rebuke you’” — Michael cannot rebuke Satan on his own authority; he must appeal to the Lord. Jesus, by contrast, rebukes Satan directly and on his own word (Matthew 4:10; Mark 8:33). Above all, the entire first chapter of Hebrews is a sustained argument that the Son is categorically higher than the angels: “to which of the angels did God ever say, ‘You are my Son’?” (Hebrews 1:5), and — quoting a text of worship — “Let all God’s angels worship him” (Hebrews 1:6). A chapter written to prove the Son is not an angel is difficult to reconcile with the claim that he is the chief one.

Honest limit: Revelation 12 shows Michael leading heaven’s armies, but so do many texts show angelic commanders; leadership in battle does not establish that two figures are one.

Test 3 — worship

The worship the Son receives Strong

This opens onto the deepest problem for the whole Watchtower system: worship. In Revelation 22:8–9 the apostle John falls down to worship an angel and is sharply refused: “You must not do that!… Worship God.” Faithful angels do not accept worship. Yet the risen Jesus accepts exactly that from Thomas, who says to him, “My Lord and my God!” (John 20:28) — a confession Jesus blesses rather than corrects, and which the New World Translation leaves unaltered.

The divine name their own translation cannot place Corroborating

The New Testament writers keep taking Old Testament texts about Yahweh and applying them to Jesus. The sharpest case is Isaiah 45:23, where Yahweh himself swears, “to me every knee shall bow.” The New Testament reaches for it twice — quoted directly in Romans 14:11, and echoed and applied to Jesus in Philippians 2:10–11 (“at the name of Jesus every knee should bow… and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord”). In Romans 14:11 the New World Translation prints “Jehovah”; in Philippians it renders Kyrios “Lord.” The honor Isaiah reserves for Yahweh alone is the very thing being paid in both places; the rule simply ensures the name can never be printed where that honor is paid to Christ. The same hymn says God gave Jesus “the name that is above every name” (Philippians 2:9), the name at which every knee bows — which in Isaiah is the name of Yahweh. So the Watchtower’s own handling of the divine name points the other way: the apostles were content to take the name and the honors that Isaiah reserves for Yahweh and give them to Christ.

Honest limit: The Watchtower will answer that its rule is text-critical, not theological: it restores “Jehovah” only where Kyrios stands for the divine name in an Old Testament citation, and in Philippians 2:11 Kyrios is the confession’s own title for Jesus, not a quotation of Isaiah’s divine name. Grant the rule — and notice what it guarantees. A criterion that always shields the Son from the divine name is not a neutral one.

Objection — the New World Translation renders the worship given Jesus “obeisance”

The Witness will answer that the New World Translation renders the worship given Jesus “obeisance.”

Reply

In Revelation 5 every creature in heaven and earth ascribes worship to the Lamb together with the One on the throne, and the elders fall down and worship (5:13–14); the text does not divide the honor between them. If Jesus were a faithful angel, that worship was his to refuse, as his colleague refused it; he received it instead. The created-angel Christology cannot explain why the Son accepts in heaven what every other faithful creature refuses.

Objection — “The Father is greater than I”: the cumulative subordination pattern Hardest

John 14:28 is the Watchtower’s best card, and it should be met at full strength rather than dodged. The strongest form is not merely the one verse; it is the cumulative weight of Jesus’s subordination throughout the Gospels — he prays, learns, obeys, suffers, dies, and confesses ignorance of the hour. A single proof-text can be parried; this is a pattern. As the analytic philosopher and unitarian critic of the Trinity Dale Tuggy presses the point, the New Testament repeatedly distinguishes Jesus from “the one true God,” the Father, in ways that a strict identification struggles to absorb.

Reply

The classical reply turns on a distinction the Witnesses tend to collapse: between what is true of the Son ontologically (in his eternal nature) and what is true of him economically — in the role he freely took on in the incarnation. Historic Christianity has never claimed that the incarnate Son was, in his human life, functioning as the Father’s equal in glory and office. The opposite is the heart of the gospel: he “emptied himself, by taking the form of a servant” (Philippians 2:7) and was made “lower than the angels” for a time (Hebrews 2:9). When Jesus says “the Father is greater than I,” the word is one of position, not of kind: the sender is greater than the sent, the Father in unveiled glory greater than the Son veiled in flesh. This is not to say the Son is a lesser or created being (the Watchtower’s Arian claim): “greater” is the language of position, not of kind, and in the same Gospel Jesus is “making himself equal with God” (John 5:18) and says “I and the Father are one” (John 10:30). In John 5:18 Jesus’s opponents seek to kill him because he was “calling God his own Father, making himself equal with God” — and Jesus does not retract it. In John 17:5 he prays to be restored to “the glory that I had with you before the world existed.” The church has read the two together in two ways — of the Son’s incarnate station, and, with Athanasius and Gregory of Nazianzus, of the Father as the unbegotten source from whom the Son is eternally begotten (Against the Arians 1.58; Oration 30.7) — and neither makes “greater” mean a higher order of being: a relation of origin is never an inequality. As for Mark 13:32, the orthodox tradition has long held that the Son’s not knowing the hour belongs to the genuine limits of his human consciousness — the same self-emptying — not to a deficiency in the divine nature he never ceased to possess. One may find that distinction subtle; it is not ad hoc, for it is the very logic of an incarnation.

Objection — “Colwell is disputed; an indefinite reading is defensible”

The construction is debated, so “a god” is defensible; a sincere Witness who has read about Colwell will say that apologists overstate the grammar.

Reply

Leaning on Colwell to prove “the Word was the God” is a genuine overreach, and an attentive Witness will catch it; grant that. But the honest conclusion is not that “a god” is one defensible option among several; it is that the qualitative reading, which affirms the Word’s full deity, is the settled judgment of the standard reference grammars, and that even were one to retreat to a purely qualitative paraphrase the created-angel Christology has already lost. One who shares the very nature of God is not a creature.

Objection — “The glory you gave me”: John 17:22 shares the glory with creatures

If the Son’s sharing the Father’s glory shows his deity, what of the same prayer a few verses later, where Jesus says of his disciples, “The glory that you have given me I have given to them” (John 17:22)? If shared glory made the Son divine, it would make them divine too — and glory, the objection concludes, cannot be the mark of deity at all.

Reply

The answer turns on a distinction the objection passes over: what the Son has and what the Son is given. In verse 5 Jesus asks to be restored to “the glory that I had with you before the world existed” — a glory he already possessed, with the Father. Of that glory the God of Isaiah says, “My glory I give to no other” (Isaiah 42:8; cf. 48:11): the honor of being God is shared with no creature. The glory of verse 22 is different in kind, and the text names it: it is the glory of unity — “that they may be one even as we are one” (John 17:21, 23–26). One is the glory of being God, which God shares with no creature; the other is the glory of being joined to God in love, a gift the Son can give precisely because he became man to give it. The Son is prayed back into a glory he held with the Father; the disciples are handed a glory of communion they never held at all. A creature is handed a glory that was never its own; the Son is named the holder of the glory that is God’s alone.

The room: On the grammar of John 1:1, the field is not evenly divided: the qualitative reading affirming the Word’s full deity is the settled judgment of the standard reference grammars and of specialists like Harner and Wallace; the textual scholar Bruce Metzger called “a god” “a frightful mistranslation”; and “a god” survives almost entirely outside the academic mainstream. That is not a reason to dismiss Witnesses — but it is an honest statement of the scholarly weather. The genuinely live debate is elsewhere: critics such as Dale Tuggy and, on historical grounds, Bart Ehrman argue that the New Testament’s own Christology is more diverse — and in places more subordinationist — than the later Nicene synthesis allows, and that the full doctrine of consubstantiality is a fourth-century development rather than a first-century datum. They are right that the doctrine’s formulation was refined over centuries, and right that the texts must be read without smuggling Nicaea into every verse. The Trinitarian reply, developed by scholars like Richard Bauckham and Larry Hurtado, is that the raw materials — the worship of Jesus, his inclusion within the divine identity, his role in creation — are present astonishingly early, well before any creed. But the academic critics of Nicene orthodoxy named here do not defend “the Word was a god,” the partitive reading of Colossians 1, or the equation of Jesus with Michael. Jason BeDuhn (Truth in Translation, 2003) stands nearly alone among published scholars in defending the NWT’s “a god” — and he reads it as a lesser divine being, so he takes the Watchtower’s side and should be answered, not enlisted. On those specific Watchtower exegeses, the answer is firm.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged below.

The one that matters most: the cumulative subordination pattern, not any single verse. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"Colwell is disputed — even your scholars allow an indefinite reading of John 1:1" Common

Strongest form

Apologists overstate the grammar. Qualitative, definite, indefinite — the construction is debated, so 'a god' is defensible.

Reply

Grant the first half: Colwell’s rule does not run in reverse, and leaning on it to prove ‘the Word was the God’ is an overreach. Take the reading most Greek grammarians now follow — qualitative — and the Watchtower still loses: 'the Word was fully divine in nature' is the death of created-angel Christology, not its rescue. 'A god' is the one option the context forbids (John's monotheism) and verse 3 refutes (the maker of all is not a made thing). And the grammar the Watchtower leans on does not deliver an indefinite reading in the first place: an anarthrous preverbal predicate nominative is normally qualitative (Harner, Wallace), only rarely indefinite. The one published scholar who defends ‘a god,’ Jason BeDuhn, reads it as a lesser divine being — answer him, do not enlist him.

"Firstborn of creation is a partitive — firstborn OF the group is IN the group" Common

Strongest form

Firstborn of the flock is a sheep. Firstborn of all creation is, by plain grammar, part of creation — the first one.

Reply

Hebrew-Greek 'firstborn of X' often marks rank OVER X — David the firstborn over kings he was not born among (Ps 89:27). Context decides, and Paul supplies it immediately: FOR (hoti) by him ALL things were created. The firstborn is the reason creation exists, not its first member — which is exactly why the Watchtower had to add 'other' four times to make the partitive reading survive its own next verse.

"Proskuneo just means obeisance — bowing to kings, not worship" Trickier

Strongest form

The word you translate worship is used of bowing to human kings. Honouring Jesus with proskuneo proves court etiquette, not deity.

Reply

Context again — and the contexts are merciless: the SAME word is refused by Revelation's angel ('worship GOD') and by Peter from Cornelius, yet accepted by the risen Jesus (Matthew 28:9, 17), who also blesses Thomas’s ‘my Lord and my God,’ and commanded of ALL God's angels toward the Son in Hebrews 1:6 — a quotation of a worship-of-YHWH text. Obeisance to kings exists; what no created-angel Christology explains is why the Son accepts in heaven what every other faithful creature refuses.

"Jesus is Michael — the archangel’s voice of 1 Thess 4:16 and the commander of Rev 12" Trickier

Strongest form

The Lord descends WITH an archangel's voice; Michael leads heaven's armies as Jesus does. The identification is biblical inference, not invention.

Reply

Descending 'with an archangel's voice' no more makes him an archangel than descending 'with God's trumpet' in the same verse makes him a trumpet. And Scripture draws the contrast itself: Michael 'did not dare' pronounce judgment on Satan (Jude 9) — while Jesus rebukes Satan on his own authority; Hebrews 1 exists to argue the Son is BETTER than angels, asked 'to which of the angels did God EVER say, You are my Son?' The expected answer is none — the Watchtower answers 'one.'

"The Father is greater than I — Jesus himself ranks below Jehovah" Hard

Strongest form

John 14:28 from Jesus own lips. Plus he prays, learns, dies, and does not know the hour. Your own texts give us a lesser being.

Reply

All true of the Son INCARNATE — and the doctrine teaches exactly that: he 'emptied himself,' made 'lower than the angels FOR A LITTLE WHILE' (Heb 2:9). Not knowing the hour (Mark 13:32) belongs to the limits of his human mind, not to the divine nature he never ceased to possess. 'Greater' is position — the sender than the sent, the Father in glory than the Son in flesh — in the same Gospel as 'I and the Father are one' and 17:5's shared pre-creation glory. The Watchtower must make 'greater' mean 'a different kind of being'; John's own context will not let it.

"Worship of Jesus is RELATIVE — honouring the agent honours the sender; no rival to Jehovah" Hard

Strongest form

We honour Jesus as Jehovah's appointed king — worship passing THROUGH him to God, like saluting an ambassador. Sophisticated Witnesses are not idolaters.

Reply

Scripture knows the through-pattern — and breaks it deliberately for the Son: Revelation 5 has every creature worship the Lamb AND the One on the throne, jointly, terminally; John 5:23 demands all honour the Son JUST AS they honour the Father; Stephen's dying prayer goes TO Jesus, not through him. And the relative-worship theory faces the angel test it cannot pass: John offered an angel exactly that relative honour and was refused — if creatures must refuse it, the one who accepts it is no creature.
