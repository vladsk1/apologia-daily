The Philippians 2 Hymn

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and stay clear and calm in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

Philippians 2:6-11 has long been read as an early Christian hymn that Paul quotes rather than composes — an identification challenged in recent work, though the argument below survives either way — its rhythm, structure, and vocabulary mark it as a pre-existing piece. Philippians was written in the mid-50s or early 60s, depending on where Paul was imprisoned; and if Paul is indeed quoting earlier material — the majority view — the hymn is older still, giving us a window onto the worship of the very first Christians, within perhaps two decades of the crucifixion.

The hymn says Christ existed "in the form of God" (Greek morphe theou) before his birth, yet did not cling to that status — he "emptied himself," taking "the form of a servant," and humbled himself to death on a cross. God then exalts him and gives him "the name that is above every name," so that "every knee should bow ... and every tongue confess that Jesus Christ is Lord." That climax quotes Isaiah 45:23 — the chapter where YHWH declares "I am the LORD, and there is no other" and swears that to him alone every knee will bow. The hymn lifts God's exclusive oath and centers it on Jesus.

Honest scope: the precise meaning of individual phrases (such as "form of God" and "emptied himself") is debated among scholars and the focus of careful theological discussion. The argument's main force is chronological: it shows that the worship of Jesus as Lord, sharing the divine name, was not a late legend but was already being confessed by monotheistic believers — if Paul is indeed quoting earlier material, the majority view — within perhaps two decades of the crucifixion. And the hymn holds both natures together: the one who was in the form of God took the form of a servant and was born in human likeness. He emptied himself BY taking, not by ceasing to be — fully God and fully man, and he did not lay the deity down.

Before you start · key terms

Eight words this argument leans on

Learn these first — the objections below turn on them, and most weak versions of the argument come from blurring one of them.

Pre-Pauline (the Carmen Christi)If Paul is quoting an existing piece — a hymn, a confession, a creedal fragment — then its theology is older than the letter that contains it. That Paul is quoting is the majority view, though the pre-Pauline origin is itself debated: Gordon Fee argues Paul composed the hymn, and some have questioned whether “hymn” is the right genre label.
“In the form of God” (morphē theou)The word morphē does not mean mere outward shape, as if Christ only looked divine. A few words later the same noun describes what Christ became — the “form of a servant” (v. 7) — and by the logic of the parallel, the “form of God” he started in must be equally real.
HarpagmosThe rare noun behind “a thing to be grasped” (v. 6), among the most contested words in the New Testament. On one reading equality with God is a thing not yet possessed that Christ refused to seize; on the other, a thing already possessed that he refused to exploit. The word remains the subject of live philological dispute.
Kenosis (“emptied himself”)It does not say Christ emptied himself of anything — the Greek has no genitive “of his deity.” Grammatically, the sentence tells us how he emptied himself: “by taking the form of a servant.” The mystery is not subtraction but addition and concealment; he did not set aside, lose, or diminish his deity.

The name above every nameAlmost certainly the divine name itself, rendered Kyrios (“Lord”) — the Greek Old Testament’s standard substitute for YHWH. The confession “Jesus Christ is Lord” thus places Jesus on the divine side of the line that Jewish monotheism drew most sharply.
Christology of divine identityBauckham’s reading: Second Temple Jews defined the one God by who he is — the sole Creator, the sole sovereign, the only one worthy of worship — and the hymn includes Jesus within that unique identity. Wright grants the genuine Adam echoes but argues they are woven into a frame the Adam reading cannot carry: the sequence “in the form of God… taking the form of a servant” describes a transition Adam never made.
The argument, formally

A pre-Pauline hymn that gives Jesus the name above every name.

P1

Philippians 2:6-11 preserves an early Christian hymn — its rhythm, vocabulary, and structure mark it as composed before Paul's letter (mid-50s or early 60s), within perhaps two decades of the crucifixion.

P2

The hymn says Christ existed in the form of God before his birth, emptied himself to take a servant's form — and that every knee will bow and every tongue confess him as Lord, quoting Isaiah 45:23, where YHWH swears those words apply to himself alone.

∴ C

Therefore full deity-worship of Jesus is not a later legend — it was already being confessed by monotheistic Jews within perhaps two decades of the crucifixion.

The argument's force is chronological: it removes the time legend needs. The highest Christology turns out to be among the earliest evidence we possess. We hold that this worship is rightly placed — that the one they sang to is, as Nicaea confessed, true God from true God, one God with the Father and the Spirit. The gospel needs him to be, and joyfully confesses him to be, fully God and fully man.

Then the dagger — what Paul did with Isaiah 45.

Isaiah 45 is the most insistently monotheistic chapter in Isaiah — 'I am the LORD and there is no other' — and verse 23 is YHWH's oath that every knee will bow to him. The hymn lifts that oath and centers it on Jesus. A devout Pharisee did not do that carelessly.

Defending Premise 1

Philippians 2:6-11 preserves an early Christian hymn — its rhythm, vocabulary, and structure mark it as composed before Paul’s letter (mid-50s or early 60s), within perhaps two decades of the crucifixion.

Precision check: the dating is only as secure as the pre-Pauline premise itself. If Paul is indeed quoting prior material, that material predates the letter — plausibly reaching back into the 40s, within a couple of decades of the crucifixion. That Paul is quoting pre-existing material is the majority view, though the pre-Pauline origin is itself debated.

Form: the marks of quoted liturgy The core warrant

The passage shifts into rhythmic, structured, exalted Greek with rare vocabulary Paul uses nowhere else — the marks of quoted liturgy — long the majority reading in critical scholarship. Even in English the passage reads differently from the prose around it — balanced, rhythmic, almost liturgical. In 1928 the German scholar Ernst Lohmeyer argued in Kyrios Jesus that the lines fall into a deliberate strophic structure, and that Paul is not composing here but quoting. Most twentieth-century scholarship followed.

Honest limit: the hymn theory has been challenged in recent work (see the objection below: the case survives either way). Some, including Hurtado himself, have questioned whether “hymn” is the right genre label and whether the strophic reconstructions are secure.

Function: Paul cites it as shared ground Supporting

Paul cites it as something the Philippians already know, to settle an ethics dispute — you cite shared songs, not new compositions, to settle disputes.

Why the date matters: a compressed timeline Consequence

This is the linchpin of what scholars call the early high Christology debate. The older liberal model assumed that a “high” view of Jesus — pre-existent, divine — evolved slowly, drifting upward as the church moved from Jewish Palestine into the Greek world. But if a hymn this exalted was already sung in the church’s first generation, the timeline for legendary development is badly compressed. As Martin Hengel argued, more happened in Christology in the years between the crucifixion and Paul’s letters than in the seven centuries of doctrinal development that followed.

Objection — “Scholars dispute that it is a hymn at all”

Recent scholarship questions the pre-Pauline hymn theory — maybe Paul wrote it himself. Then your early date collapses to the date of the letter.

Reply

Take the worst case: Paul composed it for this letter, in the mid-50s or early 60s. The passage is still a Pharisee, trained under Gamaliel, applying YHWH’s oath to a crucified man within three decades — and presenting it to a congregation as uncontroversial common ground. As Gordon Fee observed, Paul nowhere sets out to prove that Christ pre-existed; across more than a decade of letters he everywhere assumes it, in the offhand way a writer treats settled common ground. An assumption that pervasive is harder to explain away than any explicit proof would be. The same descent surfaces in letters no one reads as a hymn: “though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor” (2 Corinthians 8:9) reads most naturally—though a metaphor of earthly poverty is possible—as a transition from a prior state. So even if the Philippians hymn were set aside entirely, the pre-existence it sings would still stand in Paul.

The room: Lohmeyer (1928) and the twentieth-century majority hold that Paul is quoting; Gordon Fee argues Paul composed the hymn; Hurtado questions the genre label. Several things are widely (not universally) agreed, and several remain contested — a careful reader should label the strict “hymn” classification as contested.
Defending Premise 2

The hymn says Christ existed in the form of God before his birth, emptied himself to take a servant’s form — and that every knee will bow and every tongue confess him as Lord, quoting Isaiah 45:23, where YHWH swears those words apply to himself alone.

Precision check: the hymn does not, by itself, deliver the full doctrine of the Trinity or the two-natures formula of Chalcedon; those took centuries of reflection to articulate. It is poetry, not the creed of Chalcedon, and it leaves much unspoken about how the divine and human are related. It does not say Christ emptied himself of anything — the Greek has no genitive “of his deity.” Grammatically, the sentence tells us how he emptied himself: “by taking the form of a servant.”

“In the form of God” (morphē theou) Strong

The word morphē does not mean mere outward shape, as if Christ only looked divine. The decisive clue is internal: a few words later the same noun describes what Christ became — the “form of a servant” (morphē doulou, v. 7). His servanthood was not a costume; he genuinely became a servant. By the logic of the parallel, the “form of God” he started in must be equally real. The phrase points to Christ’s sharing in the very condition and glory of God.

The borrowed text: Isaiah 45:23 Strong

The climax (vv. 10–11) quotes Isaiah 45:23: “every knee should bow... and every tongue confess.” In its original context this is one of the most fiercely monotheistic passages in all of Scripture. In Isaiah, YHWH declares, “I am God, and there is no other,” and swears that to him alone “every knee shall bow, every tongue shall swear.” It is a text about the worship owed to the one God of Israel and to no one else. The hymn takes that text and applies it to Jesus.

The name above every name Strong · almost certain

The “name above every name” that Jesus is given is almost certainly the divine name itself, rendered Kyrios (“Lord”) — the Greek Old Testament’s standard substitute for YHWH. The confession “Jesus Christ is Lord” thus places Jesus on the divine side of the line that Jewish monotheism drew most sharply.

Divine identity, and early devotion Widely influential · category debated

Richard Bauckham calls it a “Christology of divine identity.” His point is that Second Temple Jews defined the one God not by an abstract essence but by who he is: the sole Creator, the sole sovereign, the only one worthy of worship. The Philippians hymn, drawing on the most insistently monotheistic chapter in Isaiah, includes Jesus within that unique identity — he receives the worship and the name that belong to YHWH alone, “to the glory of God the Father.” Larry Hurtado approached the same evidence from the angle of practice: the earliest Jewish believers underwent a startling “mutation” in their devotional life: while firmly maintaining one God, they began to reverence Jesus alongside the Father.

Honest limit: Bauckham’s divine-identity reading is widely influential, though the category itself is debated (James F. McGrath disputes it).

The hymn guards the Father’s glory Safeguard

The universal confession of Jesus as Lord does not rival the Father or divide the Godhead — it redounds to the Father’s glory (v. 11). That the Son’s worship glorifies the Father is exactly why the earliest Jewish believers could bow to Jesus without supposing they had gained a second god.

Objection — Dunn: “Form of God just means image, like Adam” Hardest

James D. G. Dunn argued that the hymn is best read as Adam Christology, not as a story of a pre-existent being descending to earth. Adam, made in the image of God, grasped at equality with God (Genesis 3) and was condemned to death; Christ, the second Adam, faced the same archetypal choice, refused to grasp, accepted obedience and death, and was exalted. On this reading every line maps onto a contrast between two men, and the hymn need not assert that Christ personally existed before Bethlehem at all.

Reply

Paul genuinely uses Adam–Christ typology, and it is one of his richest ways of asserting Christ’s true humanity. But the typology does not reduce Christ to a merely human second Adam. Wright grants the genuine Adam echoes but argues they are woven into a frame the Adam reading cannot carry: a merely human second Adam was never “in the form of God” before “taking the form of a servant” and being “born in the likeness of men.” That sequence — being in God’s form, then becoming human — describes a transition Adam never made. Fee adds that the parallel morphē theou / morphē doulou resists being flattened into two human postures. And no Adam typology explains giving Jesus YHWH’s own Isaiah 45 oath.

Objection — Harpagmos: “he did NOT grasp at equality, so he lacked it”

The Greek says he did not consider equality with God something to be grasped — seized. You do not seize what you already own; the verse denies equality. The harpagmos debate is genuinely unsettled; the word is so rare that confident lexical certainty is impossible, and critics argue that the Adam reading fits a “grasping at what one does not have” sense.

Reply

A widely cited study by Roy W. Hoover (1971) examined the idiom harpagmon hēgeisthai across Greek literature and argued it consistently meant treating something already at one’s disposal as a prize to be used for one’s own advantage. N. T. Wright built on Hoover, arguing that the verse is not about Christ refusing to grab deity but about his refusing to exploit a divine equality he already had. On harpagmos, the honest concession is that the word is contested; but the Hoover–Wright reading does not depend on it alone — it is reinforced by the morphē language and the Isaiah 45 climax, neither of which fits a purely human Jesus comfortably.

Objection — “Exaltation means he was not God before” (Ehrman: an angel promoted)

God “highly exalted him and gave him the name” — a reward. Some read the hymn as an exaltation Christology, rather than the round-trip descent and return of an incarnate divine person. The agnostic scholar Bart Ehrman grants that the hymn teaches pre-existence — and argues that the pre-existent one was an angel, so that equality with God is the prize at the end of the story, not its starting point.

Reply

Everything Ehrman places at the end, the hymn names at the beginning. “In the form of God” and “equality with God” both stand in verse 6 — before the incarnation, before the cross, and before the “therefore” of verse 9. The exaltation restores and publicly vindicates a status already possessed, and confers a name — a public station — not a divine nature the exalted one had previously lacked. And the one exalted is the one who took the form of a servant: he is exalted as truly man, without ever ceasing to be what he was. The decisive difficulty is the text the hymn quotes: Isaiah surrounds those words with “I am God, and there is no other” (45:22) and “my glory I give to no other” (42:8). An angel lifted into that oath is precisely the “other” the oath excludes. Grant, for argument’s sake, that God’s name and authority could be entrusted to a supreme agent; the worship still could not be. Hurtado found no evidence of angels receiving worship in any known Jewish circles of Paul’s day. So the honest answer is no: the Philippians hymn does not fit an angel Christology. The verdict is bounded: the hymn shows that the one it sings is no angel, not that it states the whole doctrine of Nicaea by itself.

The room: Wright, Fee, Bauckham and Hurtado hold the pre-existence reading; Dunn presses the Adam reading; Ehrman presses the angel reading; McGrath disputes Bauckham’s category. The pre-existence question is a real and ongoing scholarly debate, not a settled rout — though the weight of recent scholarship has moved toward reading genuine pre-existence in the text. The one who was “in the form of God” took the form of a servant and became truly man, and it is he — fully God and fully man — at whose name every knee, in heaven and on earth, will bow.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: Dunn’s Adam reading, and the genuinely contested meaning of harpagmos. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"Form of God just means image, like Adam" Common

Strongest form

"Kyrios was just a polite title — like sir" Hard

Strongest form

Kyrios meant lord in the everyday sense — masters, officials, even Caesar. Calling Jesus kyrios proves respect, not deity.

That is not courtroom politeness; it is the divine prerogative of the chapter that says 'there is no God but me.' The hymn does not call Jesus sir; it calls him by the Name.

"This contradicts the Trinity — God exalts him, so two beings" Hard

Strongest form

If God highly exalted Jesus, then Jesus is not God — the exalter and the exalted cannot be the same being. The hymn is binitarian confusion, not Trinitarian theology.

Reply

The hymn distinguishes persons — exactly what Trinitarian doctrine requires. The Father exalts the incarnate Son; the confession 'Jesus is Lord' lands 'to the glory of God the Father.' One divine nature, distinct persons, no rivalry — the hymn is not confusion about the Trinity; it is the raw material the doctrine was minted from.

The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

Several things are widely (not universally) agreed, and several remain contested. That Paul is quoting pre-existing material is the majority view, though the pre-Pauline origin is itself debated. The “exploited / used to advantage” reading of harpagmos is now common in major commentaries, but the word remains the subject of live philological dispute. And the pre-existence question — Dunn versus Wright, Fee, Bauckham, and Hurtado — is a real and ongoing scholarly debate, not a settled rout, though the weight of recent scholarship has moved toward reading genuine pre-existence in the text.

A fairness most defenders skip: The rival reading should be stated at full strength: Dunn argues from the text that the hymn is Adam Christology, in which every line maps onto a contrast between two men and the hymn need not assert that Christ personally existed before Bethlehem at all; in his judgment the clearest statement of personal pre-existence in the New Testament is in John’s Gospel, and he nuanced his position in response to critics without abandoning the Adam reading. Gordon Fee argues Paul composed the hymn; Hurtado himself questioned whether “hymn” is the right genre label; and Bauckham’s divine-identity category is widely influential but debated — James F. McGrath disputes it.

The bounded verdict: The Carmen Christi does not, by itself, deliver the full doctrine of the Trinity or the two-natures formula of Chalcedon. But it shows that, if Paul is indeed quoting earlier material — the majority view — then within perhaps two decades of the crucifixion, Jewish followers of Jesus were confessing him as one who shared the “form of God,” who was given the divine name, and to whom they applied a text Isaiah had reserved for YHWH alone. Whatever the unresolved questions about a single Greek word, the worship of Jesus as God is not a late accretion. We hold that this worship is rightly placed — that the one they sang to is, as Nicaea confessed, true God from true God, fully God and fully man.

    "Paul tells the Galatians they received him “as an angel of God, as Christ Jesus” (Galatians 4:14). Ehrman argues that when Paul uses that construction he is not contrasting two things but saying they are the same thing.     How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    That verse cannot bear the weight laid on it. The grammar allows that reading; it does not require it. Hurtado noted that even Charles Gieschen, whose work Ehrman follows here, offers it only as a “possibility,” and that most scholars read the verse as “a kind of stair-step statement,” with “angel of God” and “Christ Jesus” as ascending categories: you welcomed me as you would an angel — indeed, as you would Christ himself. The verse implies only that Paul was welcomed with an honour fit for an angel — indeed for Christ himself — not that Christ is an angel. Paul’s plain statements, meanwhile, put Christ on the other side of the line. Angels appear among the powers that cannot “separate us from the love of God in Christ Jesus our Lord” (Romans 8:38–39); and Christ is the “one Lord” “through whom are all things” (1 Corinthians 8:6) — and the one by whom every created thing was made cannot himself be one of the created things. One disputed verse, read against its most natural sense, cannot outweigh all of that.
