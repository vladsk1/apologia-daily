Was Jesus wrong about the end of the world?

✓Fact-checked & reviewed for Nicene orthodoxyHow we review →

No — the texts do not show that Jesus promised the final end within his hearers' lifetime and was proved wrong. The charge is usually pressed from two sayings in Mark: "there are some standing here who will not taste death until they see the kingdom of God after it has come with power" (Mark 9:1) and "this generation will not pass away until all these things take place" (Mark 13:30), the second spoken just after the discourse has described the Son of Man "coming in clouds" (13:26).

Read them where they stand. The prophets had always spoken this way — "The day of the LORD is near" (Isaiah 13:6; Joel 1:15) — and "near" summons the hearer to readiness now; it need not mean that the whole of God's purpose will be finished within a set number of years. The same discourse sets its own limits: no one knows the day or hour, "nor the Son, but only the Father" (Mark 13:32), a not-knowing that belongs to the incarnate Son's true human nature and is no deficiency in his deity; he remains fully God. And before the end "the gospel must first be proclaimed to all nations" (Mark 13:10). To disclaim knowledge of the day is not to be wrong about it.

The discourse begins with the Temple (Mark 13:2) and the disciples' question about "all these things" (13:4). Many interpreters, France among them, read "all these things" in 13:30 as picking up that question; on that reading the generation saw what the verse promised, and the fall of the Temple came true within it. (For those who read 13:24–27 of the return, 13:29 marks the difference: it calls "these things" signs that "he is near," not the coming itself.) Mark 9:1 is followed at once by the Transfiguration, which many readers from the early church on have taken as its fulfilment; others read it of the resurrection and Pentecost, or the judgment on Jerusalem. A critic replies that "some … will not taste death" is odd for an event six days off; on the Transfiguration reading the scene is a foretaste of the kingdom's power, not the whole of it. Only the reading that makes "come with power" the final return turns the saying into a failed prediction. Christians differ on how much of Mark 13 concerns AD 70 and how much the final return; the answer does not depend on settling that.

Many critical scholars, Dale Allison, Bart Ehrman and E. P. Sanders among them, conclude that Jesus expected God's decisive intervention, the end of the age, within his own generation; the texts do not require that conclusion. The hardest texts (Mark 13:24; Matthew 24:29; Matthew 10:23) are where the debate is closest; interpreters across traditions read each of them in ways that do not make it a failed prediction, though others judge those readings strained. Allison's case rests less on any one saying than on the pattern of the tradition as a whole; that pattern also includes sayings that look past an immediate end (Mark 13:10; Luke 19:11–12), which he reads as the movement adjusting to delay and we read as part of the pattern from the start. We hold that he was not mistaken: what he said would happen within that generation did, and the day he said no one knows is still to come. The church confesses in the Nicene Creed that he "shall come again, with glory, to judge the quick and the dead." The full case is in the essay.
