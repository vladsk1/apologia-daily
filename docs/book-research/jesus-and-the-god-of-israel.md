## Thesis / spine (from the Introduction — read in full, 2026-10-05)
In our own words (not Bauckham's prose): the New Testament's Christology is, from its earliest
post-Easter beginnings, a **Christology of divine identity** — the first Christians included Jesus
**within the unique identity of the one God of Israel**, not alongside God as a second or lesser being.
Bauckham's key move is to make **identity** (*who* God is) the governing category, rather than
**nature/essence** (*what* divinity is); he argues this reframes and gets "beyond" the old
**"functional vs. ontic"** debate, a distinction he says does not fit early Jewish thinking and has
distorted the reading of NT Christology. Second Temple Judaism, he argues, marked out the one God's
unique identity by clear, consistent identifiers (to be confirmed/filled from chs. 1, 3: **sole Creator
of all things; sole sovereign Ruler of all things**; the only one **worshipped**; bearer of the divine
**name** YHWH). To include Jesus in these identifiers is to include him in God's identity while keeping
monotheism intact. His slogan: **"the earliest Christology was already the highest Christology."** He
also insists the divine-identity Christology is **not a mere stage on the way to** the later patristic
"ontological" Christology — it is already a full inclusion of Jesus in God's identity. Chapter 1 is his
1998 *God Crucified: Monotheism and Christology in the New Testament* (Didsbury Lectures 1996); the
other chapters develop aspects of the proposal.

## Introduction — mined (read in full, p-0009…p-0016)
In our own words. Bauckham frames the whole collection: ch. 1 is his 1998 *God Crucified: Monotheism
and Christology in the New Testament* (a version of the 1996 Didsbury Lectures; pub. Carlisle:
Paternoster / Grand Rapids: Eerdmans, 1998 — **fn 1**); the other chapters are self-contained essays
(from conferences/multi-authored volumes, some revised) that develop it, and are "working papers on the
way to" a fuller promised study (provisional title *Jesus and the Identity of God: Early Jewish
Monotheism and New Testament Christology*). Two halves of the *God Crucified* argument: (a) early
Christians **included Jesus in the unique divine identity** (who God is), reframing the old
**functional-vs-ontic** debate — "the earliest Christology was already the highest Christology," and it
is **not a mere stage** toward later patristic "ontological"/essence-and-nature Christology; (b) the
**corollary** — because it is the crucified, humiliated, earthly Jesus (not only the pre-existent/exalted
Jesus) who belongs to God's identity, **God's own identity is revealed in Jesus' human life and cross**,
in humiliation as well as exaltation (a *theology of the cross*; Bauckham notes Nicene theology was
"less successful in appropriating this corollary" and credits the Luther-adumbrated theologia crucis).
ch. 8 (on Jesus' death in Mark) develops this second half. **No primary-source leads in the Introduction
beyond fn 1.**

## Chapter 1 — God Crucified (IN PROGRESS, from p-0017)
Internal structure (cite by section, stable across editions): **§1 Understanding Early Jewish
Monotheism** → **§1.1 Early Jewish monotheism and New Testament Christology in recent discussion**.
- **§1.1 Early Jewish monotheism and NT Christology in recent discussion (p-0017…p-0026):** sets up the
  debate. The book's key question = the relation of Second Temple **Jewish monotheism** to **NT
  Christology**; what is new is a live scholarly debate about the **nature of Jewish monotheism itself**.
  Two approaches Bauckham critiques: **(1)** Judaism was a **"strict" monotheism** making it impossible to
  attribute real divinity to any figure but the one God — so (some argue) Jesus could be really divine
  only by a **radical break** with Jewish monotheism (**fn 2**). **(2) Revisionist** views that **deny**
  the strictly monotheistic character, pointing to **intermediary figures** (principal angels, exalted
  humans, personified divine attributes/functions) in a subordinate "semi-divine" status, so the
  God–creation line was "by no means absolute," and hunting **Jewish precedents/parallels** for early
  Christology (**fn 3**). **Bauckham's own position:** he sides with (1) that the monotheism **was**
  strict, **against** (2)'s revisionism — intermediary figures are **not** blurred semi-divinities; they
  are **either** aspects of the one God's own reality **or** unambiguously **creatures** the literature
  "takes pains to distinguish" from God (so they are NOT the key to early Christology). **Yet** (against
  (1)'s conclusion) Christology was possible within strict monotheism **"not by applying to Jesus a Jewish
  category of semi-divine intermediary status, but by identifying Jesus directly with the one God of
  Israel, including Jesus in the unique identity of this one God"** (p-0023; a heavily-highlighted
  passage). Two methodological criticisms: (a) the question "what counts as *divine*?" is rarely faced
  with clarity; (b) the thin, debatable intermediary-figure evidence has been allowed to dominate — study
  the broader evidence of God's uniqueness first. **⭐ IMPROVEMENT LEAD (p-0025):** Bauckham warns that
  grounding Christology in "semi-divine intermediary" precedent, far from supporting orthodoxy, "often
  produce[s] something much more like an **Arian Christ, a demigod who is neither truly divine nor truly
  human.**" → candidate addition to `worship-of-jesus.html` / `jesus_as_god_nt.html`, which refute the
  agency route but may not make this sharp "the intermediary route yields an Arian demigod, not Nicene
  Christ" point. **Verify against the essays in the cross-check.** This maps onto the agency/shaliach
  objection already handled in `shema.html` + `worship-of-jesus.html`.
- **§1.2 Second Temple Judaism as self-consciously monotheistic (p-0026…p-0028):** Jews knew who their
  God was and defined their distinctive way by worshipping only him. Best evidence = **two texts** known
  universally: the **Shema** (Deut 6:4–6) and the **Decalogue's** first two commandments (**Exod 20:2–6;
  Deut 5:6–10**), both read as asserting YHWH's absolute uniqueness; the Shema recited twice daily (with
  the Decalogue in this period). Monotheism was belief **and praxis**: **monolatry** (worship of only the
  one God) as the corollary of monotheism — a point he returns to. **Primaries:** Deut 6:4–6; Exod 20:2–6;
  Deut 5:6–10. (All already in `shema.html` → corroboration.)
- **§1.3 The unique identity of God in Jewish monotheism (p-0028…p-0030+):** the governing category is
  the **identity** of God (**fn 5**) — the God of Israel has a **name and a character**, **acts, speaks,
  relates, can be addressed and known**; so the analogy of **personal identity** ("who God is") is the
  right synthesizing category, over against the Greek abstraction of **essence/nature** ("what God /
  divinity is"). Bauckham's own definitional line (heavily highlighted, p-0030): **"Identity concerns
  *who* God is; nature concerns *what* God is or what divinity is."** ⬜ NEXT: the specific identity
  criteria (expected: YHWH as **sole Creator of all things** and **sole sovereign Ruler of all things**,
  plus worship / the divine name) — to capture from §1.3 onward. **This is the exact Bauckham attribution
  our `shema.html` (fn 8) and `worship-of-jesus.html` make → corroboration + source-of-record for that
  attribution.**
- **§1.4 Characterizing the unique identity of God (p-0032…p-0040):** ⭐ the core taxonomy. Bauckham
  distinguishes **two categories of identifying features** of the God of Israel: **(A) in relation to
  Israel** — the **name YHWH**, revealed in the exodus and his self-revealed **character** ("YHWH, YHWH, a
  God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in steadfast love and faithfulness," **Exod 34:6**,
  **fn 7**; acts in history: **Exod 20:2; Deut 4:32–39; Isa 43:15–17**); and **(B) in relation to all
  reality** — "most especially, that he is **sole Creator of all things** (**fn 8**) and **sole sovereign
  Ruler of all things** (**fn 9**)." These two are "not *sufficient* to identify God (they say nothing of
  his goodness or justice), but the features which most readily distinguish God absolutely from all other
  reality… every Jew in every synagogue in the late Second Temple period would certainly have known."
  Grounded esp. in **Deutero-Isaiah** (Isa 40–55), the most important source outside the Torah (**fn 10**).
  ⭐⭐ **THE LOAD-BEARING DISTINCTION (p-0038–p-0040):** **creation vs. rule differ.** In **creation God
  acted utterly ALONE** — "I alone stretched out the heavens… by myself spread out the earth" (**Isa
  44:24**); "God had **no helper, assistant or servant** to assist or implement his work of creation
  (**fn 11, 12**); God alone created, no one else had any part — this is **axiomatic** for Second Temple
  Judaism." In **rule**, God uses servants (angels), **but** angels are "**invariably servants… they do
  not share in his rule; they serve. While God sits on his throne, the angels, even the greatest, stand**"
  (**fn 13, 14**) — "excluding any possibility of interpreting their role as that of **co-rulers**." →
  This is the exact basis our cluster uses: because creation admits **no** helper, the NT's "all things
  made **through** him" (1 Cor 8:6; Col 1:16; John 1:3; Heb 1:2) puts Jesus on the **Creator** side; and
  "sitting" on the throne (vs. angels standing) = sharing God's **rule** (seeds ch. 5). **Primaries:**
  Exod 34:6; Exod 20:2; Deut 4:32–39; Isa 43:15–17; **Isa 44:24**; Deutero-Isaiah (Isa 40–55).
  **Cross-check: corroborates `shema.html` fn 8/11 (Bauckham 7–13, 9–11) and `worship-of-jesus.html`
  exactly; this is the source-of-record for those attributions.**
- **§1.5 Exclusive worship of YHWH as recognition of his unique identity (p-0040…p-0045):** the **third**
  identity-marker — **monolatry** (exclusive worship of the one God), "the factor which, in religious
  practice, **most clearly signalled the distinction between God and all other reality**" (**fn 16**).
  Jewish scruples about any practice construable as worship of others (**fn 18**); their monolatrous
  practice sharply distinguished them from non-Jews who worshipped a high god **and** lesser divinities
  (**fn 19**). ⭐ **A key intramural nuance (p-0041):** against the view (**fn 20**, = Hurtado-type) that
  **worship is THE defining criterion**, Bauckham argues worship is **"a recognition of and response to"**
  God's unique identity (Creator + Ruler), not the primary definer — "it is God's unique identity which
  *requires* worship of him alone." Pagan worship was a matter of **degree** because divinity was a matter
  of degree (a spectrum/hierarchy from the one God down); Jewish worship marks an **absolute** line
  (**fn 21, 22**). **Cross-check: corroborates `worship-of-jesus.html` exactly, AND is the source-of-record

- **§1.6 Jewish monotheism and 'intermediary' figures (p-0045…p-0047):** the method — for each
  intermediary figure, ask: by the Jews' **own** identity criteria, does it **belong to** God's unique
  identity or **fall outside** it (intrinsic to God, or creature/servant however exalted)? "In almost
  every case the question is readily answerable."

## Chapter 1 §2 — Christological monotheism in the NT (IN PROGRESS, from p-0055)
- **§2.1 Introduction: divine identity Christology (p-0055…p-0060+):** applies the framework to the NT. The
  Second-Temple divine-identity analysis is the **"hermeneutical key"** to how the NT relates Jesus to the
  one God. ⭐ **The thesis, in Bauckham's own voice:** "the intention of New Testament Christology,
  **throughout the texts, is to include Jesus in the unique divine identity**… deliberately and
  comprehensively by using precisely those characteristics of the divine identity on which Jewish monotheism
  focused" — divine **sovereignty** over all things, **creation**, and the **worship** that recognizes the
  identity. This yields a **"christological monotheism"** "fully continuous with early Jewish monotheism,
  but distinctive in… see[ing] Jesus Christ himself as intrinsic to the identity of the unique God." ⭐⭐
  **The "surprising thesis" (p-0059, our "early = high" cornerstone):** "the **highest possible Christology
  — the inclusion of Jesus in the unique divine identity — was central to the faith of the early church even
  before any of the New Testament writings were written, since it occurs in all of them.**" Development was
  in *understanding*; the decisive step was made "at the beginning of Christology." The idea that **Jewish
  monotheism and high Christology were in tension is "one of the prevalent illusions"** the texts dispel; the
  NT saw the inclusion of Jesus as the **fulfilment of eschatological monotheism** (the one God universally
  acknowledged in his universal rule). **Cross-check: directly corroborates the "early, not late" spine of
  `worship-of-jesus.html` + the whole deity cluster; source-of-record for "earliest Christology = highest
  Christology."**
- **§2.1 continued (p-0061–p-0062) — two sharp argument-structure points (improvement-lead territory):**
  (a) the common idea that semi-divine intermediaries "made room" for high Christology is "**almost the
  exact opposite of the truth**" — Jewish monotheism **could not** accommodate semi-divine/subordinate
  deities, but **could** include Jesus **directly** in the one identity (reinforces the **"Arian Christ"**
  improvement lead from §1.1); (b) the decisive step is **binary / *de novo*** — it "does **not** become
  any more intelligible by being placed at the end of a long process of christological development,"
  against gradualist/exaltation models (**Ehrman**-type). Best explained as taken **very early**.
- **§2.2 Jesus and the unique sovereignty of God (p-0063–p-0064):** the **earliest** move — Jesus
  **exalted to be seated on God's throne**, "seated with God on God's throne," exercising/participating in
  "God's unique sovereignty over the whole cosmos"; angels **stand/serve**, Jesus **sits** → shares rule =
  inclusion in the divine identity. A human participating in the unique divine sovereignty is
  **unprecedented** (angels/patriarchs give no precedent).
- **§2.3 Psalm 110:1 in early Christology (p-0064–p-0068):** ⭐ **Ps 110:1 (LXX 109:1)** is the **most-cited
  OT text in the NT — 21 quotations/allusions** across most NT writings (Johannine lit. the one exception)
  (**fn 39**; likely D. M. Hay, *Glory at the Right Hand*). The verse ("The LORD said to my Lord, 'Sit at
  my right hand…'", **fn 40**) is itself ambiguous, but early Christians read it as Jesus **on** the divine
  throne, often combined with **Ps 8:6** ("all things under his feet," **fn 42**). ⭐ **Striking datum:**
  Ps 110:1 is barely used in Second Temple Judaism — **only one probable allusion, *Testament of Job* 33:3**
  (**fn 43**), and nothing applies it to a figure sharing God's cosmic throne — so the Christian
  throne-sharing reading is a **distinctive novelty** (the Jewish Messiah ruled *on earth*, never the
  cosmos from heaven). **Primaries:** Ps 110:1; Ps 8:6; Testament of Job 33:3. **Cross-check: load-bearing
  for `jesus_as_god_nt.html`, `hands.html`, ch. 5 preview; the "21 allusions" figure + the T. Job-only
  datum are citable and may be sharper than what we carry → check in the cross-check.**
- **§2.4 Jesus' sovereignty over 'all things' (p-0068–p-0069) — aspect 1 of "four further aspects":** the
  NT's "**all things**" is the **standard rhetoric of Jewish monotheism** (the whole of created reality
  from which God is distinguished as Creator/Ruler, **fn 44**). God's servants rule *some* things; only God
  rules over *all* things — so applying "all things" to Jesus is a **divine-identity marker** (Phil 3:21;
  1 Cor 15:27–28; Eph 1:22; Col 1:16–17; Heb 1:3; etc. — to pin exact refs as they appear).
- **§2.5 Jesus shares God's exaltation above all the angelic powers (p-0069–p-0070+) — aspect 2:** texts

  ("seated… far above all rule and authority… put all things under his feet," = Ps 110:1 + Ps 8:6) and
  **Hebrews 1**'s catena of **seven** OT quotations concluding with Ps 110:1, proving Jesus' superiority
  over **all** angels (**fn 46**). Jesus is **not** slotted into an angelic position; he is on God's side of
  the line. **Cross-check: supports the anti-JW "Jesus is above all angels, not an angel" point
  (`trinity_jw`, `jesus_as_god_nt`, ch. 7 Hebrews preview).** (Heb 1:7–9, 13–14 — angels serve, Jesus sits.)
- **§2.6 Jesus given the divine name (p-0072–p-0073) — aspect 3:** the exalted Jesus is given the divine

  (Jesus "became as much superior to the angels as the name he has inherited is more excellent") = the divine
  name, as is "the name that is above every name" bestowed in **Phil 2:9**. And "**call on the name of the
  Lord**" (**Joel 2:32**; fn 48–49) — an OT phrase for invoking **YHWH** — is applied to **Jesus** (**Rom
  10:13; Acts 2:21; 1 Cor 1:2**): invoking Jesus as the divine Lord who bears the divine name. **Cross-check:

- **§2.7 Worship of Jesus as recognition of his exercise of the unique divine sovereignty (p-0073–p-0074) —
  aspect 4:** worship recognizes the unique divine identity (Creator/Ruler), so worship of Jesus = recognizing
  his participation in it. Key texts (treated in detail next): **Phil 2:9–11** and **Revelation 5** (universal
  worship evoked precisely by Christ's exaltation to the divine throne); also **Matt 28:17** ("all authority
  in heaven and on earth," fn 50). **Cross-check: `worship-of-jesus.html`.**
- **§2.8 The pre-existent Christ participates in God's unique activity of creation (p-0074–p-0082+):** ⭐⭐ the
  most decisive move. Early Christians included Jesus in the divine sovereignty not only eschatologically but
  **protologically** — from the beginning, in **creation**. Since (per §1.4) "**creation, axiomatically, was
  the sole work of God alone**" (no servant helped), including Jesus in creation is "**the most unequivocal way
  of excluding any threat to monotheism — as though Jesus were a subordinate demigod** — while redefining the
  unique identity of God in a way that includes Jesus." Found in **1 Cor 8:6, Hebrews [1:2], John [1:3]** (Col
  1:16 the fuller Pauline form), each using creation-Christology "**precisely to express Jewish monotheism in
  christological terms**." ⭐ **The 1 Cor 8:6 analysis (the `shema.html` source-of-record, p-0078–p-0082):**
  v.6 is structured a (one God the Father) / b (from whom all things) / c (one Lord Jesus Christ) / d (through
  whom all things); it is **composed from two sources** — the **Shema** (Deut 6:4; fn 53) and a **creation
  formula** — with Paul reproducing **all** the Shema's words but **rearranging** them to affirm "one God the
  Father" **and** "one Lord Jesus Christ." ⭐⭐ **The decisive rebuttal of the "alongside / addition" reading
  (= McGrath):** if Paul merely **added** a Lord to the Shema's God, that would be "**outright ditheism**…
  flatly contradict[ing] the uniqueness" of the one God; the **only** monotheistic reading is that Paul
  **identifies Jesus as the 'Lord' whom the Shema affirms to be one** — the unique identity "**consists of**
  the one God, the Father, **and** the one Lord, his Messiah." **Primaries:** 1 Cor 8:4–6; Deut 6:4; Col 1:16;
  John 1:3; Heb 1:2. **Cross-check: exact source-of-record for `shema.html`'s core argument (fn 7–9) and its
  answer to McGrath's "alongside, not inside" (shema.html already runs the ditheism-or-inclusion dilemma) →
  corroboration + source-of-record.**
- **§2.8 continued — the "through whom" / second source (p-0083–p-0085):** the b/d lines are a **creation
  formula**: **Rom 11:36** ("from him and through him and to him are all things") is said of **God alone**,
  but in 1 Cor 8:6 Paul **divides** it — "from/to" to the Father, "**through**" to Christ (**fn 54** = Jewish
  parallels for the from/through/to formula). ⭐ **The prepositions mark no rank difference** (efficient /
  final / instrumental cause are all God's unique creative relationship), so "through him" puts Christ **on
  the Creator side**. Christ is implicitly identified with God's **Word/Wisdom** — but these are the *vehicle*,
  not the *driver*, of the Christology. **Cross-check: exact source-of-record for `shema.html` fn 11's Rom
  11:36 move (prepositions ≠ rank) → corroboration.**
- **§2.9 Conclusion — NT Christology is a Christology of divine identity, beyond "functional"/"ontic"
  (p-0086–p-0091):** "A higher Christology than Paul already expresses in 1 Cor 8:6 is scarcely possible." The
  **"functional vs ontic" contrast is "fundamentally misleading"** — sovereignty is not a delegable *function*
  but part of *who God is*; so the NT's lack of concern with Christ's "nature" is **not** a merely functional
  Christology — "**the Christology of divine identity common to the whole New Testament is the highest
  Christology of all**." (Source-of-record for the Introduction thesis; `jesus_as_god_nt.html` / general.)

- **§3.2 Christological monotheism: the early Christian reading of Isaiah 40–55 (p-0095–p-0104):** ⭐⭐ the
  exegetical engine of "God Crucified," and a strong **improvement-lead candidate** (likely sharper than our

  demonstration of his deity to the nations through the **Servant**. ⭐ **The core move (p-0101–p-0103):** the

  "exalted, lifted up, very high") to **Isa 6:1** (God on his throne) and **Isa 57:15** (God himself) — a
  "striking verbal coincidence" early Christians exploited by the Jewish exegetical principle **gezêrâ šāvâ**

  **John 12:38–41** brings Isa 53 + Isa 6 together and says Isaiah "saw Jesus' glory" (= the glory of YHWH in

  texts combine **Ps 110:1 + Isa 52:13** (**Acts 2:33; 5:31**) and **Ps 110:1 + Isa 57:15** (**Heb 1:3**) — so

## Chapter 2 — "Biblical Theology and the Problems of Monotheism" (IN PROGRESS, from dense q-0025)
⚠ **This is Bauckham's METHODOLOGY chapter, not a fresh deity-of-Christ argument.** Its job is to defend the
*category* "monotheism" (rightly defined) against the charge that it is an anachronistic, Enlightenment-loaded
idea — i.e. to secure the ground the divine-identity argument of Chapter 1 stands on. Mostly academic
background for us; the apologetic payoff is indirect (it answers the "exclusive monotheism / high
Christology developed late" move at the OT end, mirroring Chapter 1 §3.7 at the NT end).

- **§1 Introduction — "the problems of monotheism."** "Monotheism" is contested at *both* testament ends:
  historical-critical work questions how far pre-exilic Israel was "monotheistic," and parallel debates
  question how far Second Temple Judaism was "monotheistic" — which bears directly on how unique early
  Christology was. Bauckham puts "monotheism" in scare-quotes throughout because the term itself is modern and
  can smuggle in an Enlightenment picture of religion. He wants "monotheism as a truly pan-biblical issue."
- **§2 "Monotheism as a misleading category."** Engages **Nathan MacDonald, *Deuteronomy and the Meaning of
  'Monotheism'*** (FAT II/1; Mohr Siebeck, 2003) [scholar lead — verify], who argues that "monotheism" (like
  "polytheism") is a 17th-c./Enlightenment coinage (Cambridge Platonists; the "intellectualization" of
  religion), so that reading Deuteronomy as asserting an *objective, rationally-knowable metaphysical fact*
  ("there is exactly one God") is anachronistic; Deuteronomy's "oneness of YHWH" is instead a **call to
  exclusive, whole-hearted love and obedience** (the Shema, Deut 6:4–5), relational not propositional.
  **Bauckham's response (the key move):** he grants that the term needs care and that Deuteronomy's monotheism
  is deeply relational/devotional — BUT he denies MacDonald's reduction. Deuteronomy *does* assert YHWH's
  **objective, transcendent uniqueness**, not merely that YHWH is Israel's elected national god. Primary
  backbone (all canonical OT — safe to cite directly once verified): **Deut 4:32–40** (esp. the two climactic
  "there is no other" statements, **4:35** and **4:39**: "YHWH is God [*hā-'ĕlōhîm*] in heaven above and on the
  earth beneath; there is no other [*'ên 'ôd*]"), **Deut 10:14** (heaven and the heaven of heavens belong to
  YHWH), **10:17** ("God of gods and Lord of lords, the great God"), **32:39** (Song of Moses: "See now that I,
  even I, am he; there is no god besides me; I kill and I make alive…"), with **Josh 2:11** and **1 Kgs 8:60**
  (Solomon's dedication echoing Deut 4) as parallels. The recurring **"there is no other" / *'ên 'ôd* /
  *'ên 'ôd mil-baddô* formula** (also Isa 45:5–6, 18, 21–22) asserts YHWH's unique cosmic deity — "God with
  supreme power over heaven and earth," the gods of the nations being impotent non-entities (Deut 32:21,
  *lō' 'ēl* "no-god"). **Cross-check: this is the OT foundation of the whole divine-identity framework** — the
  "YHWH alone" exclusivity our `shema.html` / `jesus-is-yahweh.html` rest on. Possible P3 improvement lead: do
  our essays ground OT exclusive monotheism in the *Deuteronomy* "there is no other" texts (not just the Shema
  and Isa 44–45)? If not, Deut 4:35/39 + 32:39 + the *'ên 'ôd* formula is a sourceable strengthening.
- **§3 "The quest of the historical monotheism."** The methodological parallel Bauckham draws explicitly:
  **just as NT theology faces "the quest of the historical Jesus," OT theology faces the historical
  reconstruction of the rise of monotheism.** He surveys the reconstructions — **J. C. de Moor, *The Rise of
  Yahwism*; W. H. Schmidt** (the 1968 *Das erste Gebot* / *The First Commandment*, "double process of
  recognition and rejection"); **Robert Gnuse, *No Other Gods: Emergent Monotheism in Israel*** (1997);
  **Mark S. Smith** ("convergence" and "differentiation," *The Early History of God* / *The Origins of Biblical
  Monotheism*) [all scholar leads — verify]. Bauckham prefers **Schmidt's model of an early distinctive "core"
  of exclusive Yahwism** over **Gnuse's late/"revolutionary" emergence model**, noting that archaeology
  actually favours *early* Yahwistic aniconism, and he is pointedly critical of Gnuse's **evolutionary** framing
  (Gnuse slots Israelite monotheism among the "Axial Age" intellectual breakthroughs — Greece, Persia, India,
  China — and even uses a biological "Punctuated Equilibria" analogy). **This mirrors Chapter 1 §3.7's verdict
  that the *developmental* model of Christology is "seriously flawed":** Bauckham resists an evolutionary account
  at BOTH ends — exclusive monotheism is early and "core," not a late metaphysical achievement. ⚠ Academic
  reconstruction detail; little direct apologetic use beyond the "it was early, not a late development" point.

- **§4 "The Old Testament — a monotheistic book?"** (dense q-0030, q-0031). Bauckham's constructive move: the
  **final-form canon IS exclusive-monotheistic**, read that way by early Judaism. His preferred terms:
  **"exclusive monotheism" / "Jewish monotheism"** (= the strict identity-based exclusive monotheism of Second
  Temple Judaism, "the theology of the canon," which the NT presupposes) — pointedly NOT "Enlightenment
  monotheism" (the intellectualist abstraction). Engages **James A. Sanders** ("the Bible as *monotheizing
  literature*") and **E. P. Sanders** (both scholar leads). Two sub-threads that are strong OT groundwork for
  our divine-identity case:
    - **The heavenly retinue / "divine council."** The "gods" beside YHWH in the Hebrew Bible are either (a)
      YHWH's **created servants/retinue** — "host of heaven," "sons of the Most High," "holy ones," "watchers" —
      who serve and worship him and do NOT advise him (the "divine council" does not deliberate: Neh 9:6; Job
      1:9–12; 2:4–7; 1 Kgs 22:19–22; Job 15:8; Jer 23:18,22; Ps 82), or (b) **impotent non-entities** / "non-gods"
      (Ps 96:5 "all the gods of the nations are *'ĕlîm* [idols], but YHWH made the heavens"; Deut 32:21
      *lō' 'ēl*). ⭐ **This is the OT foundation of Chapter 1 §1's claim that angels are servants, not sharers in
      the divine identity** — i.e. the backbone of the "Jesus is not merely an exalted angel/agent" argument.
    - **Two OT idioms of uniqueness** (Bauckham, following **John F. A. Sawyer** [scholar lead]): the
      **"there is no other / none besides YHWH" formula** (*'ên 'ôd* / *'ên 'ôd mil-baddô*: 2 Sam 7:22; Deut
      4:35,39; 1 Kgs 8:60; Isa 45:5,6,18,21–22; Joel 2:27) and the **"incomparability" texts** ("Who is like
      YHWH?": Exod 15:11; 1 Sam 2:2; Isa 44:7; Jer 49:19; Ps 86:8; 89:6). Even the **chaos-conflict** imagery
      (Rahab/Leviathan; Ps 74:13–14; Isa 27:1) is re-functioned to assert YHWH's transcendent supremacy (engages
      **Jon Levenson** and **Herbert Niehr** [scholar leads]). In Second Temple Judaism the word "gods" was
      almost dropped for any being that serves YHWH (except with care at Qumran / in Philo).
- **§5 "The Shema in the New Testament"** (dense q-0032, q-0033) — ⭐ **the apologetically load-bearing section
  and the main cross-check target for `shema.html`.** Two framing moves then the texts:
    - **Rejects the "two gods in Israel" thesis.** Against **Bernhard Lang** and esp. **Margaret Barker** (who
      argue an older Israelite tradition of two gods — El and YHWH, father and son — survived into early Judaism
      and that Christians "identified Jesus with YHWH" on that basis), Bauckham holds there is **no evidence**
      non-monotheistic Israelite religion survived through the Second Temple period; "the literature of early
      Judaism is uniformly monotheistic." ⭐ **Useful against the skeptical "high Christology just revived a
      suppressed Israelite polytheism / a second god" move** (a cousin of the rabbinic "two powers in heaven"
      argument). The NT writers **presuppose** exclusive Jewish monotheism and innovate christologically **on
      that basis, not by departing from it.**
    - **§5.1 Romans 3:28–30** — "God is one" grounds **Jew + Gentile** unity (the universalism of the one God),
      echoing **Zechariah 14:9** ("YHWH will be king over all the earth; on that day YHWH will be one and his
      name one"); **not** christological. (Scholar lead: **Mark Nanos** on Romans.)
    - **§5.2 1 Corinthians 8:1–6** — the centrepiece (also mined in Ch. 1 §2): the two monotheistic formulae
      ("no idol has real existence" + "there is no God but one"), idols-as-demons (Deut 32:17 LXX; 1 Cor
      10:19–21), and the **reformulated Shema** that splits BOTH the Shema's words AND the creation of "all
      things" between "one God the Father" and "one Lord Jesus Christ" (Rom 11:36; distinguished from the
      Ps-Aristotle *De Mundo* 6 / Marcus Aurelius *Medit.* 4.3 "all things" prepositional parallels). Including
      Jesus in the Shema = including him in the unique divine identity, *within* (not against) monotheism.
    - **Other NT Shema echoes:** **John 17:11,22** (the "that they may be one as we are one" prayer), **Ephesians
      4:4–6** (the "one…one…one" creedal list incl. "one God"), the Jewish "one God → one people / one temple /
      one altar" topos (Josephus *A.J.* 4.201; *C. Ap.* 2.193; *2 Bar.* 85:14), **Ezekiel 37:15–28** ("one
      king/one shepherd") → **John 10:16** ("one flock, one shepherd"); cross-reference to **Mark 12:32** (the
      scribe: "he is one, and besides him there is no other").

## Chapter 3 — "The 'Most High' God and the Nature of Early Jewish Monotheism" (IN PROGRESS, from dense q-0035)
A lexical/thematic study of the divine title **"the Most High"** (Hebrew *'Elyon*, Greek *Hypsistos*) across early
Jewish literature, deployed to **defend "exclusive monotheism" against William Horbury's "inclusive monotheism."**
- **§1 Introduction.** Engages **William Horbury, "Jewish and Christian Monotheism in the Herodian Age"** [scholar
  lead], who argues that reading Second Temple Judaism as *exclusive* monotheism (denying other divine beings) does
  "less than justice" to its **messianic tendencies**, which were bound up with an **"inclusive" monotheism** (a
  supreme deity *in association with* other spirits/powers). ⭐ **Bauckham rejects the framing:** every
  monotheistic tradition (Jewish, Christian, Islamic) has always accepted hosts of supernatural beings (angels,
  demons) — but these are **created by and subject to God**, no more a qualification of monotheism than earthly
  creatures are. So "rigorous/exclusive monotheism" is still the right description. ⭐ **This is the direct answer
  to the "there was already room for a second divine figure, so Jesus' divinity didn't break monotheism by
  lowering the bar" move** — Bauckham's point is the opposite: the bar stayed absolute (divine identity), and Jesus
  was included *within* it, not slotted into a pre-existing vacancy.
- **§2–§3 "The 'Most High' in early Jewish literature."** The title clusters in three fields of association:
  **§3.1 Temple, cult and prayer** (the God to whom one has access in worship/sacrifice/thanksgiving/blessing —
  Sir 50:1–21; Melchizedek, Levi and the Hasmoneans as "priests of the Most High God," cf. Gen 14:18–20);
  **§3.2 God's sovereign rule over all things** (the earthly holy of holies mirrors God's heavenly throne-room;
  the Most High as universal Ruler and Judge); **§3.3 Use by or to Gentiles** (the title appropriate when speaking
  of/to Gentiles — the Most High *over all nations*). The nations' "gods" (Deut 32:8–9, 17, 21; the *lō' 'ēl*
  "non-gods") exist as subordinate created beings allotted to the nations, not as rivals — Philo, Jubilees ("his
  angels/spirits/authorities," created, *Jub.* 2:2), Ben Sira ("rulers") all treat them as subject to YHWH the
  Most High who created and rules them. ⭐ **Reinforces Chapter 1's "sole Ruler" divine-identity category.**

- **Occurrence table + endnotes** (dense q-0040, q-0041): a statistical table of "Most High" occurrences in early
  Jewish literature (4 Ezra 68×, Ben Sira 47×, 2 Baruch 24×, Jubilees 23×, 1 Enoch 17×, Daniel 14×, Genesis
  Apocryphon, 4Q242 Prayer of Nabonidus, Psalm 154, Tobit, 1QS, Pseudo-Philo, Testament of Moses, 1QH, Testament
  of Levi, Ladder of Jacob…). Philo is careful to avoid the pagan "highest among many" sense of *Theos Hypsistos*;
  the epigraphic "cult of Theos Hypsistos" question is deferred. **Primaries/leads:** Daniel's Aramaic plural

## Chapter 4 — "The Worship of Jesus in Early Christianity" (COMPLETE except one page; dense q-0042…q-0052)
⭐ **A MAJOR chapter for us — the worship-as-divine-identity-criterion argument; the main cross-check target for
`worship-of-jesus.html`.** Bauckham argues the worship of Jesus is early, pervasive, and decisive for christological
development (NT → Nicaea/Chalcedon), and that it was NOT a late Hellenistic development (rejects the old Bousset
*Kyrios Christos* thesis).
- **§1 Introduction.** The worship of Jesus has been underestimated; it is the thread from the NT through ante-Nicene
  Christianity to the Nicene/Chalcedonian definitions. (Lead: the "most significant step… in the origins of
  Christianity" quote — attributed to **Johannes Weiss / W. Bousset**-era scholarship; verify the exact source.)
- **§2 Origins** — ⭐ the core early evidence: (a) **"calling on the name of the Lord Jesus"** (Rom 10:12–14; 1 Cor
  1:2; 2 Tim 2:22; Acts 9:14,21; 22:16) — drawn from **Joel 2:32**, an OT *worship-of-YHWH* text, applied to Jesus;
  (b) **confessing Jesus as Lord** (1 Cor 12:3; Phil 2:11); (c) **Maranatha** ("Our Lord, come!": 1 Cor 16:22; *Did.*
  10:6; cf. Rev 22:20) — an Aramaic acclamation proving very early Palestinian origin; (d) **Acts 13:2**
  (*leitourgountōn* — cultic-worship language); (e) petitionary prayer (**John 14:14**, reading "ask *me*"); (f)
  **proskynein in Matthew** (28:9,17; 14:33; 2:2,11; 8:2; 9:18; 15:25; 20:20 — distinguished from reverence-for-
  superiors, tied to God's presence in Jesus, Matt 18:20; 28:20). **Hurtado**'s "binitarian devotion" / "a mutation
  *within* Jewish monotheism." Leads: **Larry Hurtado** (*One God, One Lord*), **David Aune**.
- **§3 Doxologies** — christological doxologies ("Glory to…"; 2 Tim 4:18); and ⭐ **Revelation 5:13** — the doxology
  to God **and the Lamb together** (cf. 7:10), the nearest the NT comes to a coordinated Trinitarian doxology;

- **§4 Hymns** — **Martin Hengel**: hymns to Christ "as old as the community itself." Eph 5:19; ⭐ **Pliny the
  Younger, *Ep.* 10.96.7** ("*carmen Christo quasi deo*" — sang a hymn to Christ as to a god); Ignatius *Eph.*; Acts
  of Paul; and ⭐ the anonymous early-3rd-c. **anti-Artemon fragment** (preserved in Eusebius *HE* 5.28) that refutes
  the heretic Artemon's claim that Christ's deity was a recent innovation — **directly answers the modern "high
  Christology developed late" objection.**
- **§6 Worship of Jesus in Revelation** (dense q-0050) — ⭐ the **angel refuses worship** and redirects John to God
  (Rev 19:10; 22:8–9), while **the Lamb IS worshipped** (Rev 5:8–14) in the heavenly throne-room — Jesus is placed
  on the divine side of the worship boundary, not excluded with the angels. **§6.3 Missionary Christianity in the

- **§7 Conclusion** (dense q-0051) — the ante-Nicene continuity to Nicaea: **Origen** (four types of prayer/worship;

## Chapter 5 — "The Throne of God and the Worship of Jesus" (PARTIAL — core captured via Ch. 1 overlap; §1–§5.1 skipped)
Bauckham's well-known essay (first published in *The Jewish Roots of Christological Monotheism*, ed. C. C. Newman,
J. R. Davila, G. S. Lewis; Brill, 1999). Its thesis — **Jesus' sharing of the divine THRONE** (Ps 110:1) is a
divine-identity marker, since the heavenly throne is reserved to God alone — is **already fully mined in Chapter 1
§2** (the throne texts: Ps 110:1; Acts 2:33–36; 5:31; Heb 1:3; Rev 3:21; 5; 22:1,3). Captured here directly:
- **§5.2 "Novel exegesis and novel claim"** (dense q-0091): ⭐ **Psalm 110:1 is the OT verse most often applied to
  Jesus in early Christian literature**, and the christological reading — Jesus *seated on God's own throne* — is a
  **novel exegesis making a novel claim** (Second Temple Judaism did NOT read the royal psalms this way; cf.
  *Testament of Job* 33:3, which uses throne-language quite differently). ⭐ **Good apologetic point: the
  throne-sharing Christology was a bold, unprecedented claim, not something borrowed from existing Jewish
  categories.** Engages **Timo Eskola, *Messiah and Throne: Jewish Merkabah Mysticism and Early Christian Exaltation
  Discourse*** (WUNT 2/142; Mohr Siebeck, 2001) [lead].
- ⚠ **§1–§5.1 NOT captured** (the throne-of-God setup, the merkabah tradition, the exalted-Jesus-enthroned argument).
  **The core argument is held via Ch. 1 §2**, but the chapter's own development is owed — same TOC-navigation help

## Chapter 6 — "Paul's Christology of Divine Identity" (COMPLETE in map; dense q-0093…q-0098)
Applies the Chapter 1 divine-identity framework to Paul. ⭐ **Main cross-check target for `paul-divinity.html`.**
- **§1** recaps the framework: strict Second Temple monotheism; God identified by (a) covenant relationship with
  Israel (name YHWH; Exod 34:6) and (b) relationship to all reality (sole Creator, sole Ruler); Wisdom/Word =
  aspects of God's own identity, while exalted servants/angels are unambiguously creatures. High Christology =
  **identifying Jesus *with* the one God (including him in the unique identity), NOT applying an intermediary
  category.** "What was important was *who* the one God is, rather than *what* divinity is."
- **Phil 2:6–11 as a christological reading of Deutero-Isaiah** (dense q-0094): ⭐ **Isaiah 45:23** ("every knee
  shall bow… every tongue confess") applied to Jesus (Phil 2:10–11) = including Jesus in **YHWH's unique universal
  rule and worship** (Isa 45 is the great "there is no other" monotheism chapter). Rebuts the reductive "mere Adam
  Christology" reading (proskynein / the *Life of Adam and Eve* parallel); Rev 5:8–14 and Polycarp confirm the
  worship is of the one enthroned in divine sovereignty, not a restored Adam.
- **1 Cor 8:6 / creation** (dense q-0096): Christ as the one through whom God creates all things (Neil Richardson's
  "theological inclusion" of Christ in the divine identity; Rom 11:36 split between God and Christ).
- **§8 "Christological reading of scriptural YHWH texts outside Paul"** (dense q-0096): ⭐ a catalogue showing the
  practice of applying OT **YHWH texts to Jesus** was **not originated by Paul** but widespread in early
  Christianity — **the backbone of `jesus-is-yahweh.html`.** Key lead: **David B. Capes, *Old Testament Yahweh Texts
  in Paul's Christology*** (WUNT 2/47; Mohr Siebeck, 1992) — the monograph on this argument.
- **Angelic-intermediary rebuttal** (dense q-0097): the principal angel **Yahoel** (Apocalypse of Abraham) and
  **Michael** bear the divine name and have exalted functions, but are **created servants who do NOT share the
  divine throne or identity** — so they are **not real precedents** for including Jesus in the divine identity
  (⭐ the "Jesus is more than a principal angel" rebuttal; connects to Ch. 1 §1's "exception that proves the rule").
- **Endnotes** (dense q-0098): leads — **David B. Capes**, *Old Testament Yahweh Texts in Paul's Christology*;
  Martin Hengel; William Horbury, *Jewish Messianism and the Cult of Christ*.

## Chapter 7 — "The Divinity of Jesus in the Letter to the Hebrews" (mapped; dense q-0099…q-0102)
Applies the divine-identity framework to Hebrews. ⭐ **Main cross-check target for the Hebrews material in
`jesus_as_god_nt.html` (Heb 1:8 "your throne, O God").**
- **§1 "A Christology of divine identity"** (dense q-0099) — ⭐ opens with **Bauckham's crispest list of the
  divine-identity criteria**, worth quoting as a framework (port the structure, not his prose): God is (1) **sole
  Creator of all things** (all others created), (2) **sole sovereign Ruler over all** (all others subject to God),
  (3) **known through his narrative identity** (who God is in the story of his dealings with creation, the nations,
  Israel), (4) the God who **will achieve his eschatological rule** (all creatures acknowledging YHWH's sole deity),
  (5) named by **the name YHWH** in his unique identity, (6) the God who **alone may and must be worshipped** (worship
  = acknowledgement of God's sole deity), (7) **alone fully eternal** (self-existent from past to future eternity).
- **§4 "The significance of the angels in chapters 1–2"** (dense q-0100) — the Son is **superior to the angels**
  (Heb 1) yet made **lower than the angels** in the incarnation (Heb 2); angels are created beings subject to God.
  The Son shares the Father's throne and inherits **the divine name** (Heb 1:4; the "name above every name," Phil
  2:9). Against reading the Son as a supreme angel.
- **§5 "The full divinity of the Lord (1:5–14)"** (dense q-0100, q-0101) — ⭐ the **catena of seven OT texts in
  Heb 1:5–14** applied to the Son demonstrates his full divinity: he is **addressed as God** ("Your throne, O God" —
  Heb 1:8 / Ps 45:6), is **the Creator** and **eternally unchanging** (Heb 1:10–12 / **Ps 102:25–27**, an OT text
  about YHWH applied to the Son), is **worshipped by the angels** (Heb 1:6). Then **Heb 7:3** (Melchizedek
  "resembling the Son of God… remains a priest for ever") and **Ps 110:4** are read via the Son's sharing in **God's
  unique full eternity** (divine-identity criterion 7). The *Apocalypse of Abraham* divine-attribute hymn ("Eternal
  One… self-originate… unbegotten… without father, without mother…") is the Jewish backdrop for God's unique
  self-existent eternity.
- **Endnotes** (dense q-0102) — leads: **Bauckham, "Monotheism and Christology in the Letter to the Hebrews,"** in
  *Early Jewish and Christian Monotheism* (ed. L. T. Stuckenbruck & W. E. S. North; JSNTSup 263; T&T Clark, 2004),
  167–85; L. T. Stuckenbruck, *Angels and the Eschatology of Hebrews 1–2*; Timo Eskola, *Messiah and Throne* + "The
  Merkabah Tradition in Heb 7" (*Bib* 81, 2000); Jerome H. Neyrey, "Without Beginning of Days or End of Life (Heb
  7:3)" (*CBQ* 53, 1991). ⚠ The detailed §2–§3 setup and parts of §5 live in dense columns read at overview level;
  the thesis + all load-bearing primaries (the Heb 1 catena, Heb 1:8, Ps 102, Heb 7:3, the criteria list) are
  captured.

## Chapter 8 — "God's Self-Identification with the Godforsaken: Exegesis and Theology" (mapped; dense q-0103…q-0105)
The final chapter: an exegetical + theological study of Jesus' **cry of dereliction** — **"My God, my God, why have
you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34 / Matt 27:46 = Psalm 22:1).** Ties directly back to Chapter 1's "God Crucified."
- **§1 "The cry of desolation (Mark 15:34) in biblical context"** (dense q-0103) — Jesus quotes **Psalm 22:1** (in
  Aramaic, then Mark gives a literal Greek rendering, not the LXX). Bauckham sets Mark's whole Passion narrative
  against the **psalms of lament** (a Mark→Psalms table: Mk 14:18/Ps 41:10; 15:24/Ps 22:18; 15:29/Ps 22:7;
  15:32/Ps 22:6; 69:9; 15:34/Ps 22:1; 15:36/Ps 69:21, etc.).
- **§1.1.9 "New and old in Jesus' quotation of the psalm"** (dense q-0104) — ⭐ Jesus' use of Ps 22:1 gives the words
  **new meaning**: the crucified Messiah, **the one uniquely identified with God, prays the psalm not exclusively but
  inclusively — identifying himself with godforsaken people** in their godforsakenness. ⭐ **The "God Crucified"
  theme restated at the cross** (cf. Ch. 1 §3.5: the cross reveals God as self-giving love).
- **§1.2 "Mark 15:34–39 in the narrative structure of the Gospel"** (dense q-0104) — the cry as the **narrative
  climax of Mark** (the centurion's confession, 15:39; the two-part authority/passion structure hinging on Peter's
  confession, 8:29).
- **Endnotes** (dense q-0105) — leads: S. E. Balentine, *The Hidden God*; Raymond E. Brown, *The Death of the
  Messiah*; Gérard Rossé, *The Cry of Jesus on the Cross*; Jürgen Moltmann, *The Crucified God*; Jenkins & Reid,
  "God's Forsakenness." ⚠ The detailed §1.1.1–§1.1.8 psalm-by-psalm analysis is in dense columns read at overview
  level; the thesis + primaries (Ps 22; the lament-psalm matrix; Mark's narrative climax; the Moltmann engagement)
  are captured.
