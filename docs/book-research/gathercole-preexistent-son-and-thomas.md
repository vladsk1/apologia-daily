## A · *The Preexistent Son* (Eerdmans, 2006) — the ⭐ high-value half

### Thesis (our words, from Gathercole's own 2024 restatement)
Against the standard developmental picture — a "low" Synoptic Christology later raised to a high one in
John — Gathercole argues that **Matthew, Mark and Luke already presuppose Christ's preexistence**. The
central evidence is the **"I have come" + purpose** formula. In ancient Jewish literature that formula is
the characteristic self-announcement of an **angelic visitor**: *I have come [from elsewhere] to do X*.
When Jesus uses it, he is not describing local travel but **his whole mission** — and therefore, if the
idiom carries its ordinary force, he is stating that he **came from somewhere**.

### The evidence base (all primary and independently checkable — this is what makes the thesis usable)
- **Synoptic sayings** (the *ēlthon* + purpose-infinitive set): **Mark 2:17 // Matt 9:12 // Luke 5:32**;
  **Matt 5:17** (bis); **Matt 10:34–35** (ter); **Luke 12:49, 51**. Wider treatment at **pp. 83–189**.
- **Ancient Jewish angelic parallels:** **Dan 9:22–23; 10:12, 14, 20; 11:2 (OG)**; **Tob 5:5 (Sinaiticus)**;
  **4 Ezra 6:30; 7:2**; **2 Baruch 71:3** — the last three discussed at **pp. 119–124**. Now joined by
  **Ps.-Philo, *De Sampsone* 13.1** (the 2024 article's addition).
- **Standard counter to steelman:** *ēlthon* + purpose is ordinary Greek; **Mark 1:38** ("that is why I
  came out") arguably means leaving Capernaum.

### Guardrails — see the full treatment in the article note; the two that matter most
1. **⚠⚠⚠ The angel problem.** "Jesus' sayings pattern with angelic announcements" is one bad compression
   away from **Arian / Jehovah's-Witness angel-Christology**. The parallel is to the **form** of the
   utterance, never the **nature** of the speaker. Requires an **`orthonote` clarifier** on any page that
   carries it. **Dual-consensus (orthodoxy + neutrality) mandatory** — deity tier.
2. **⚠ Contested, not consensus.** A serious and well-received argument, but a minority-to-moderate
   position in Synoptic Christology. Never "scholars agree the Synoptics teach preexistence."

## B · *The Composition of the Gospel of Thomas* (CUP, 2012)

### Thesis (our words, from the publisher's own abstract)
Two linked questions: **what was Thomas's original language, and what influenced it?** Gathercole
**dismantles the Semitic-original theories** (Aramaic and Syriac) and argues that **Thomas is Greek
literature**. He then argues that the language question is bound up with the more contested one of
Thomas's relationship to the canonical Gospels — and takes a **third path**: neither "independent of
Matthew, Mark and Luke" (the usual Western-Aramaic position) nor "thoroughly dependent on the four
Gospels" (the usual Syriac position), but a **refined account of Synoptic influence**. Thomas can be seen
to refer to **Matthew as a Gospel writer**, and it **incorporates phraseology distinctive to Luke**, while
extending that special Lukan language.

  and *GTh* 25 (263)
- **Conclusion** (267); Bibliography (271); Index Locorum (303)

### Why this matters for us (in one line)
The popular claim is that **Thomas is an early, independent witness to Jesus** — sometimes "the fifth
Gospel," sometimes earlier than the canonical four. That claim's load-bearing plank is usually a
**Semitic original** (which would put Thomas early and outside the Greek Synoptic stream). Gathercole
attacks that plank directly, and then shows positive traces of **Lukan** wording inside Thomas — i.e.
Thomas is **downstream**.

### Guardrail fit
- **⚠ State the debate, don't declare it over.** Thomas's date and independence are **genuinely
  contested**. Serious scholars argue for independence and an early core (April DeConick; Stephen
  Patterson; the Jesus Seminar tradition). **Mark Goodacre** reached a similar dependence conclusion by a
  different route in *Thomas and the Gospels* (Eerdmans, 2012), and the two were reviewed together
  (*Scottish Journal of Theology*) — so this is a live argument with real weight on both sides, not a
  settled result.
- **⚠ Don't overreach from "influenced by Luke" to "worthless."** Gathercole's own position is expressly
  **neither** independence **nor** thoroughgoing dependence. Flattening him into "Thomas just copies the
  Gospels" misrepresents him and would fail the argument gate.
- **⚠ Canon-argument bound.** Even granting Gathercole in full, the conclusion is *chronological and
  literary* — Thomas is later and downstream — **not** "Thomas is heretical therefore excluded." Our
  `library/canon.html` already makes exactly this distinction ("the decisive point is chronology, not
  theology") and it is the right one. Keep it.
- Orthodoxy: nothing at stake in our own voice; the risk is overstatement, not heterodoxy.
