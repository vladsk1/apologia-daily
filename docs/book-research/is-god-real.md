## The book's spine

A cumulative case for theism, built one interview at a time, moving outward from physics to biology to
(presumably, in the unphotographed chapters) history and experience. The three chapters captured run:

1. **The cosmos requires a Creator** — the kalam cosmological argument, defended in Craig's standard
   three steps, with the twentieth-century cosmological evidence as corroboration of Premise 2.
2. **The universe needs a Fine-Tuner** — the constants and initial conditions of physics, the
   habitability of Earth, and the multiverse as the main rival explanation.
3. **Our DNA demands a Designer** — the origin of biological information as the argument for an
   intelligent cause, via Meyer's *Signature in the Cell* case.
4. **Easter showed that Jesus is God** — the pivot from natural theology to history: Licona on the
   historian's method and the five minimal facts. Strobel states the hinge explicitly at p. 69: the
   scientific findings "fall short of establishing the overall credibility of Christianity. For that, I
   knew I would need to turn to history."

Strobel's own thread is autobiographical: the ex-atheist legal-affairs journalist "running down the
suspects" and finding each naturalistic alternative wanting.

## Chapter 1 — "The Cosmos Requires a Creator" (pp. 17–34)
### Interview with William Lane Craig

**Thesis.** The universe began; whatever begins has a cause; therefore the universe has a cause — and
conceptual analysis of *that* cause yields a recognisable sketch of God.

### Argument beats (our own words)

**Framing (pp. 17–18).** Strobel opens on a *Discover* magazine cover ("Where Did Everything Come
From?") reporting that the whole universe at one second old was a sphere a few centimetres across.
Genesis 1:1 as the ancient answer. He samples the popular literature: Bill Bryson's line that the
universe's existence is itself proof that you *can* get something from nothing; Edward Milne's remark
that the first cause is "left for the reader to insert." Strobel's stated method: mathematics and
cosmological data rather than conjecture.

**Origins of the argument (pp. 19–20).** Aristotle held both God and the universe eternal, with God
ordering rather than creating — which cut against Hebrew creation *ex nihilo*, so Christians set out
to refute him. **John Philoponus of Alexandria** is named as the prominent Christian who argued the
universe had a beginning. Muslim theologians took the arguments up; **al-Ghazālī (1058–1111)** is the
famous proponent. *Kalām* = Arabic "speech"/"doctrine," and named the whole medieval movement of
Islamic theology. Al-Ghazālī's three steps, then a conceptual analysis of what it is to be the cause
of the universe.

**Craig's own route in (p. 19).** Taught as an undergraduate (graduated 1971) that the theistic
arguments were weak, he changed his mind on reading **Stuart C. Hackett, *The Resurrection of Theism*
(1957)**, and wrote his doctoral dissertation on the argument under **John Hick**.

**Step 1 — whatever begins to exist has a cause (pp. 21–24).** Craig's report of the dialectic: he
expected P1 to be uncontroversial and P2 contested, and says the accumulation of scientific evidence
for a beginning has pushed atheists to attack P1 instead. **Quentin Smith** is quoted concluding
their co-authored book with the claim that the most reasonable belief is that we came from nothing,
by nothing and for nothing. Defence of P1: metaphysical (uncaused coming-to-be is worse than magic —
the magician at least has a hat), plus the empirical record (nothing has ever been observed to come
into being uncaused). The quantum objection is met in two moves: virtual particles may be theoretical
constructs rather than entities; and in any case the **quantum vacuum is not nothing** but a
structured sea of fluctuating energy governed by laws, so the origin question simply relocates to the
vacuum itself. Sources sampled: the *Discover* article, **Edward Tryon's 1973 proposal** (an
assistant professor at Columbia, unnamed in the text) that the whole universe arose this way; **Alan
Guth's "free lunch"**; **Timothy Ferris** on the violation of classical foundations at quantum scale.

**Step 2 — the universe began (pp. 24–29).** Two pathways.
- *Mathematics/philosophy.* An actual infinite generates absurdities, so an infinite past is
  impossible. Illustrated with **infinite marbles** rather than Hilbert's Hotel: give away all of them
  and none remain; give away the odd-numbered and infinitely many remain; give away all numbered four
  and higher and exactly three remain — so ∞ − ∞ yields 0, ∞ and 3. Hence subtraction and division are
  barred in transfinite arithmetic; hence the actual infinite is conceptual only, not descriptive of
  the concrete world; hence substitute "past events" for "marbles."
- *Strobel's objection, and this is the one worth keeping (p. 26).* **"Then what about God being
  infinitely old? Doesn't your reasoning also rule out an eternal deity?"** Craig's answer: it rules
  out a God who *endured through* infinite past time, which is not the classical idea. Time and space
  are creations; beyond the beginning of time there is timeless eternity; God is timeless in his
  being. ⚠⚠ Craig then adds that once God creates, **he could enter time** — see the hazards.
- *Science.* Einstein 1915 and the non-static solutions; **Friedmann and Lemaître**; Hoyle's coinage
  of "big bang"; **Hubble 1929** and redshift; **Gamow** in the 1940s predicting a relic background of
  a few degrees; the 1965 detection; light-element abundances (deuterium and helium requiring a
  furnace hotter than stellar interiors). **Hawking** is quoted that almost everyone now believes the
  universe and time itself had a beginning at the Big Bang.

**Step 3 — therefore a cause (pp. 29–33).** **Kai Nielsen's "loud bang"** illustration is used as an
atheist's own statement of the causal intuition. **George Smith** is quoted for the "how did god
become exempt?" objection, answered by the scope reply plus a *tu quoque*: atheists have long held the
universe itself uncaused-because-eternal. "Why only one creator?" is answered with Occam's razor —
which Craig calls his opinion and Strobel calls soft — and then routed to the cumulative case (the
resurrection vindicating Jesus' monotheism). Then the properties: uncaused, beginningless, timeless,
spaceless, immaterial, personal, free, enormously powerful. **Personhood** by three routes:
(i) **Swinburne's scientific-vs-personal explanation** distinction with the **kettle** illustration —
there can be no scientific explanation of the *first* state, since there are no prior conditions to
appeal to; (ii) the **two-categories** argument — a timeless immaterial thing is either an abstract
object or a mind, and abstract objects are causally inert; (iii) the **water-freezing** analogy — a
timeless cause with eternally sufficient conditions yields a co-eternal effect, so a temporal effect
from a timeless cause requires a free agent.

**Alternatives (pp. 32–34).** The **steady state** theory (1948) is dismissed as motivated purely by
the desire to avoid a beginning and as never securing a single piece of experimental verification.
**J. Howard Sobel** (Toronto) is named as a serious atheist critic who devoted ~70 pages of his magnum
opus to the cosmological argument, chiefly the Leibnizian version; Craig replied in a journal article.
Craig closes on the reversal-of-fortunes theme: a century ago Christians held a beginning by faith
against appearances, and now the atheist holds an eternal universe by faith against the evidence.

## Chapter 2 — "The Universe Needs a Fine-Tuner" (pp. 35–51)
### Interview with Michael G. Strauss (experimental particle physicist, Univ. of Oklahoma; CERN/LHC)

**Thesis.** The constants and quantities of physics, and the conditions of our planet, sit inside
ranges so narrow that design is the most plausible explanation; the multiverse is the only serious
rival and is unsupported.

### Argument beats (our own words)

**Framing (pp. 35–37).** Epigraph from **Edward Harrison**, *Masks of the Universe*, calling the
fine-tuning *prima facie* evidence of deistic design. **Geraint F. Lewis** (Cambridge doctorate;
builds simulated universes) is quoted on what happens when you alter the laws — the periodic table
disappears, structure is replaced by black holes or thin hydrogen. Psalm 19:1. **Christopher
Hitchens** is quoted conceding that fine-tuning is the theistic argument he and other skeptics find
most intriguing. **Patrick Glynn**, *God: The Evidence*, on the shift since the 1960s. **Paul
Davies**, *The Mind of God*, on being unable to accept the universe's ingenuity as a brute fact.

**The problem of a cosmic beginning (pp. 38–39).** Against Michael Shermer's "we don't know," Strauss
argues we live by probabilities, not bare possibilities. The **hundred-dials control board**
illustration; then an unattributed scholar on ratios between constants needing tuning as well as the
constants themselves, so improbabilities multiply.

**Worked parameters (pp. 39–43).**
- **Matter density** tuned to ~1 part in 10⁶⁰ shortly after the big bang; illustrated against the
  27.6-billion-light-year diameter of the visible universe. **Davies (agnostic)** on the accuracy
  being one of cosmology's great mysteries.
- **Inflation as the proposed mechanism**, and Strauss's reply: a mechanism that produces the tuning
  is itself a tuned mechanism — the **lawn-mower funnel** — so inflation moves the design question
  back a stage rather than dissolving it.
- **Strong nuclear force**: +2% adds radioactive, life-destroying elements and strips hydrogen (so no
  water); −5% leaves only hydrogen. **Light quark mass** ±2–3% → no carbon.
- **Electromagnetic-to-gravitational force ratio** at ~1 part in 10⁴⁰, illustrated with **Hugh Ross's**
  billion-continents-of-dimes image.
- **Penrose's 1 in 10^10^123** for the initial low-entropy state, with the not-enough-particles-to-write-the-zeros
  remark. **Davies** again on the impression of careful thought.
- **Habitability**: spiral galaxy; galactic habitable zone; a Class G, middle-aged, single,
  third-generation star; a large stabilising Moon; plate tectonics as "the central requirement for
  life on a planet"; Jupiter as a comet shield. **Hugh Ross's 322 parameters → 10⁻³⁰⁴**.

**Testing alternatives (pp. 44–47).** **Barrow & Tipler's** Final Anthropic Principle (observers
eventually create the universe) and the simulation hypothesis are presented as the naturalist's best
efforts. Smolin-style **black-hole-birthed universes** (via Shermer), with **Luke Barnes** calling the
physics speculative. Chance: **Craig** on its being "plain silly." **Robin Collins**: the fifty-heads
coin-flip as the structure of the design inference; and on a Theory of Everything, that a law forcing
the dials into place would only move the improbability up a level.

**The multiverse (pp. 46–48).** **Tim Maudlin's** blurb on *A Fortunate Universe* framing the choice
as multiverse or designer. M-theory's untestability, with **John Horgan** in *Scientific American* on
its almost-infinite versions. **Polkinghorne** that a sufficient portfolio of universes could only be
generated by processes beyond what sober science endorses. **Swinburne** on a trillion-trillion
universes versus one God. ⭐ **Sabine Hossenfelder** (agnostic, quantum-gravity researcher, Frankfurt
Institute for Advanced Studies; *Existential Physics*) calling the multiverse a waste of time and
saying the press overstates how many scientists endorse it. ⭐ **Lewis & Barnes, *A Fortunate
Universe***, summarising 200+ published papers: fine-tuning has stood up well under physicists'
scrutiny, and it is *not* the invention of religious believers who hijacked physics — physics has
consolidated the understanding of it.

**Fine-tuned for life (pp. 48–49).** **John Leslie**, *Universes*, that on a single universe the
fine-tuning is genuine evidence for God. Then ⭐ Strobel's own objection — **why would a creator waste
so much space, since the universe is mostly inhospitable wasteland?** — and Strauss's reply: rocky
planets and carbon-based life need **third-generation** stars, which takes roughly nine billion years
to reach, and a universe expanding for that long is necessarily this large. The size is a consequence
of the required age, not extravagance.

**The God hypothesis (pp. 49–51).** Strauss's eight deduced attributes: transcendent, immaterial,
timeless, powerful, smart, personal, creative, **caring**. Then the identification move: one creator
rules out polytheism, being outside creation rules out pantheism, a non-cyclical universe "violates
the tenets of Eastern religions." **Hugh Ross** on Scripture anticipating cosmology (Gen 1:1; John
1:3; Col 1:15–17; Heb 11:3; Job 9:8; Ps 104:2; Isa 40:22; 45:12; Jer 10:12; Jer 33:25; Eccl 1:3–11;
Rom 8:20–22). Closes on the artist/soul-of-the-Artist image and Isaiah 55.

## Chapter 3 — "Our DNA Demands a Designer" (pp. 53–63 captured; chapter continues beyond)
### Interview with Stephen C. Meyer (Discovery Institute)

**Thesis.** The origin of the specified information in DNA is best explained by an intelligent cause.

### Argument beats (our own words)

**Framing (pp. 53–55).** Crick and Watson, 1953. The information-density framing: six feet of DNA per
cell, ~30,000 genes yielding ~20,500 protein kinds. **Michael Denton** on DNA's capacity exceeding
any other known system, and on a teaspoon of DNA holding the instructions for every organism that has
ever lived with room left over for every book ever written. **Dean Kenyon** ("once-skeptical") on
molecular genetics as where design is most evident. The human-genome announcement, with **Bill
Clinton** and **Francis Collins** quoted using theological language. Meyer's biography (Cambridge
MPhil/PhD in history and philosophy of science; Whitworth; Discovery Institute; *Signature in the
Cell*; *Return of the God Hypothesis*).

**The DNA-to-design argument (pp. 56–59).** **Bernd-Olaf Küppers**, *Information and the Origin of
Life*, that the origin-of-life problem is equivalent to the origin-of-information problem. DNA as a
four-character digital code; amino-acid sequence determining fold determining function; the snap-lock
bead demonstration. Meyer prefers **"library," not "blueprint,"** because DNA builds proteins, which
are subunits of larger informatively-arranged structures. 1,200–2,000 bases per protein. **Bill
Gates** on DNA as software. **Henry Quastler**: creation of new information is habitually associated
with conscious activity. Uniformitarian logic → intelligence as the cause of the first cell's
information. Strobel's caveat: unless a better explanation exists.

**The missing soup (pp. 60–61).** Darwin's 1871 "warm little pond" letter. Meyer's claim that there is
**no evidence the prebiotic soup ever existed** — no nitrogen-rich deposits in the earliest sediments
— citing **Jim Brooks, *Origins of Life* (1985)** and **Michael Denton (1985)**. Plus cross-reaction
problems even if it did.

**Scenario 1 — random chance (pp. 62–63).** Conceded to be out of vogue: "virtually all origin-of-life
experts have utterly rejected that approach." Minimal complexity threshold (~75+ amino acids for
tertiary structure); right bonds, homochirality, specified sequence → ~1 in 10¹²⁵ for one short
functional protein; 300–500 proteins for a minimal cell; ~100 Myr window between cooling and the first
microfossils. Chance is "a naturalistic miracle… a confession of ignorance."

**Scenario 2 — natural selection (pp. 63–65).** Dawkins on selection scaling improbable peaks; Meyer's
reply that whatever selection does in biological evolution, it cannot operate at the level of
*chemical* evolution, quoting **Dobzhansky**: "Prebiological natural selection is a contradiction in
terms" (fn 8). The circularity spelled out: selection needs a self-replicating organism; reproduction
needs cell division; cell division presupposes information-rich DNA and proteins — the very things
being explained. Illustrated by the man who falls into a hole, climbs out to fetch a ladder, and jumps
back in. Then the **RNA-world** reply, raised by Strobel and answered in two moves: an RNA molecule
would itself need information to function, so the question recurs; and a single RNA strand needs an
identical molecule nearby to replicate, which Meyer costs at a library of order 10⁴⁰ molecules (fn 9).
**Graham Cairns-Smith** is quoted that the experiments have shown the theory "highly implausible" (fn
10), and **Gerald Joyce** (Scripps) that "you have to build straw man upon straw man to get to the
point where RNA is a viable first biomolecule" (fn 11).

**Scenario 3 — chemical affinities and self-ordering (pp. 65–67).** By the early 1970s, Meyer says,
most origin-of-life scientists had become disenchanted with chance and with selection, and turned to
self-organisation: chemical attractions causing DNA's alphabet to self-assemble, or natural affinities
between amino acids linking them into proteins. **Dean Kenyon**, co-author of *Biochemical
Predestination*, is the named early advocate — and is reported to have later repudiated his own
conclusions. Then the refutation: salt crystals as the model case, Na⁺/Cl⁻ bonding
affinities produce a highly ordered *repeating* lattice. Amino acids show no comparable affinities
that correlate with the sequences found in functional proteins. **Hubert Yockey** and **Michael
Polanyi** are cited for the deeper point: if sequencing *were* explained by self-organising
properties, you would get something like a salt crystal — a repetitive sequence. Meyer's illustration:
an instruction book for building a car that reads "the-the-the-the." Information requires variability,
irregularity and unpredictability — *complexity*; self-organisation yields repetitive, redundant
structure — *simple order*; and complexity and order are categorical opposites.

**"Almost a miracle" (p. 67).** **Francis Crick**, described as "a philosophical materialist," is
quoted on the origin of life appearing "almost a miracle, so many are the conditions which would have
had to have been satisfied to get it going" (fn 14). Then an unattributed scientist on researchers
having faith that some "magic mineral" with exactly the right properties will be found (fn 15).

**The argument-from-ignorance objection, raised and answered (pp. 68–69).** ⚠ **This corrects an
earlier draft of this note**, which recorded the god-of-the-gaps objection as absent — it was absent
from pp. 53–63 and is present here. Strobel puts it directly ("scientists admit they don't know how
life started, so you conclude there must have been an intelligent designer"). Meyer: "I'm not saying
intelligent design makes sense simply because other theories fail… I'm making an inference to the best
explanation, which is how scientists reason in historical matters," assessing each hypothesis by
**causal power** — "not on the basis of what we *don't* know, but rather on the basis of what we *do*
know. That's not an argument from ignorance." Strobel then presses the **argument-from-analogy**
objection; Meyer denies he is arguing by analogy at all, holding that the coding regions of DNA have
*exactly* the same relevant properties as a computer code or a language, and citing the **Rosetta
Stone** as the case where archaeologists inferred intelligence from sequential symbols. He also grants
that nothing here is provable with 100 percent certainty and that "all scientists reason in a way
that's provisional," while maintaining that some possibilities are categorical dead ends.

**Close (p. 69).** **Fazale Rana** (biochemistry doctorate) is quoted that origin-of-life researchers
"have failed to generate any tangible progress toward a strictly materialistic explanation," and that
"the harmony between the Bible's account of the origin of life and nature's record provides powerful
evidence for the validity of the Christian faith" (fn 16). Strobel's transition to ch. 4: the
scientific chapters "fall short of establishing the overall credibility of Christianity."

## Chapter 4 — "Easter Showed That Jesus Is God" (pp. 71–85 captured; chapter continues)
### Interview with Michael Licona

**Thesis.** Working by ordinary historical method, a short list of facts that even sceptical scholars
grant is best explained by Jesus' bodily resurrection — which in turn vindicates his divine claims.

### Argument beats (our own words)

**Framing (pp. 71–73).** Epigraph from **Timothy Keller** (a Twitter post dated 5 April 2022) that
everything hangs not on whether you like Jesus' teaching but on whether he rose. Strobel's **Hugh
Hefner** interview at the Playboy Mansion: a minimal belief in God as "the beginning of it all" and
the "great unknown," Christianity's God dismissed as "a little too childlike," but real interest at
the mention of the resurrection — "if one had any real evidence that, indeed, Jesus did return from
the dead, then that is the beginning of a dropping of a series of dominoes"; and then the denial, "I
don't think that he is any more the Son of God than we are" (fn 1). 1 Cor 15:17 (fn 2).

Then a list of Jesus' divine claims (fn 3): forgiving sins (Matt 9:1–3); **the Son of Man title —
🔴 see the retired-claim flag**; divine control over nature (Matt 14:30–33); sitting at God's right
hand and exercising judgment (Mark 14:61–64); receiving worship (Matt 28:17); all authority (Matt
28:18); omnipresence (Matt 28:20); omniscience (Matt 9:4); the same honour as the Father (John
5:22–23). Then John 10:30 — the Greek *hen* is **neuter, not masculine**, so the claim is oneness of
nature or essence rather than of person (fn 5) — with the opponents' reaction as the confirming
evidence: they take up stones "because you, a mere man, claim to be God" (John 10:33, fn 6) and later
"he claimed to be the Son of God" (John 19:7, fn 7).

**Licona's credentials (p. 73).** Doctorate on the resurrection, University of Pretoria; mentored by
**Gary Habermas**; co-author of *The Case for the Resurrection of Jesus*; author of the 718-page
***The Resurrection of Jesus: A New Historiographical Approach***; **Paul Maier's** endorsement;
debates with **Shabir Ally, Dan Barker, Richard Carrier, Elaine Pagels, Bart Ehrman**; a period of
doubt at the end of his graduate studies in 1985.

**The historian's three Rs (pp. 74–75).**
- **Relevant sources** — the NT writings; the secular mentions (Josephus, Tacitus, Pliny the Younger);
  the apologists; the Gnostic writings; and the apostolic fathers (Clement of Rome believed a disciple
  of Peter; Polycarp probably a disciple of John).
- **Responsible method** — greatest weight to reports that are **early, eyewitness, enemy,
  embarrassing, and corroborated**.
- **Restrained results** — historians "should not claim more than the evidence warrants."
- On bias: "Nobody is exempt, including theists, deists, atheists" — hence checks and balances, hence
  Habermas's **minimal facts** approach, whose two criteria are (1) very strong historical evidence and
  (2) acceptance by the vast majority of current scholars on the subject, **including sceptical ones**.
  Habermas's bibliography is given as **more than 2,200 sources in French, German and English from 1975
  to the present**. The jigsaw-puzzle illustration: fit every piece without forcing any.

**Fact #1 — Jesus was killed by crucifixion (pp. 76–77).** **Crossan**: "That he was crucified is as
sure as anything historical ever can be" (fn 2). **James Tabor**: "no doubt that given Jesus'
execution by Roman crucifixion he was truly *dead*" (fn 3). **Lüdemann** (atheist) and **Ehrman**
(agnostic) both call it indisputable. ⭐ **Licona's methodological clarification, which is the whole
point of the method:** for the purposes of the argument he is "not considering the Bible to be
inerrant, inspired, or Scripture of any kind" — only a set of ancient documents subject to normal
historical scrutiny. Non-Christian corroboration: **Tacitus** ("suffered the extreme penalty during
the reign of Tiberius"), **Josephus** (Pilate "condemned him to be crucified"), **Lucian of
Samosata**, **Mara Bar-Serapion**, and the **Talmud** ("Yeshu was hanged") — with the note that
*Yeshu* is *Joshua* / *Jesus* and that hanging on a tree commonly denoted crucifixion (fn 4).
Executioner competence; death by asphyxiation as unfakeable. The **JAMA** analysis: "the weight of the
historical and medical evidence indicates that Jesus was dead [even] before the wound to his side was
inflicted" (fn 5).

**Fact #2 — the disciples believed he rose and appeared to them (pp. 78–83).** Three strands.
- *Paul's testimony.* He knew Peter, James and John personally (fn 6), and 1 Cor 15:11 — "whether it
  was I or they, this is what we preach" — reports that the apostles claimed the same thing he did.
- *Oral tradition.* Creeds, hymns and sermon summations embedded in the NT, which must predate the
  documents that contain them. **1 Cor 15:3–7** quoted in full, the letter dated about **AD 55** (fn
  7); many scholars hold Paul received the creed from Peter and James in Jerusalem three years after
  his conversion (fn 8) — within five years of the crucifixion. An unattributed expert: "the sort of
  data that historians of antiquity drool over" (fn 9). **James D. G. Dunn** (Durham, FBA): the
  tradition "was formulated as tradition within months of Jesus' death." Licona's own guard: early
  dating "does not totally rule out the possibility of invention or deceit," only legendary
  development. Sermon summaries in Acts as enshrining early apostolic teaching (fn 11).
- *Written sources.* Matthew, Mark, Luke, John (fn 12) — four biographies within seventy years, on the
  generous estimate Licona deliberately adopts ("an excellent case can be made for dating the Gospels
  earlier, but let's go with the more generous estimations"). Strobel's tighter chain: Acts omits the
  events of the 60s → before 62 → Luke earlier → Mark earlier still → a maximum gap of about thirty
  years (fn 13). Comparison: the two best sources on **Alexander the Great** postdate him by at least
  four hundred years (fn 14). Then the apostolic fathers — **Clement of Rome** (Irenaeus: he had "the
  preaching of the apostles still echoing"; Tertullian: ordained by Peter), quoted from *1 Clement*
  (fn 15); and **Polycarp** (Irenaeus: "instructed by the apostles, and conversed with many who had
  seen Christ," "recalled their very words"; Tertullian: appointed bishop of Smyrna by John), whose
  letter to the Philippians of about AD 110 mentions the resurrection **no fewer than five times** (fn
  16). Total: "nine sources that reflect multiple, very early, and eyewitness testimonies."
- *Willingness to suffer.* At least **seven** early sources that the disciples willingly suffered for
  the belief; **eleven** if the martyrdoms of Paul and James are included (Clement, Polycarp, Ignatius,
  Tertullian, Origen). The argument: liars make poor martyrs, so they held the belief sincerely — and,
  unlike later martyrs, the disciples "were on the scene and able to ascertain for sure" whether it
  happened. ⚠⚠ **The illustration used to make that distinction is a hazard — see the flags.**
  **Paula Fredriksen**: the disciples' conviction that they had seen the risen Christ "is [part of]
  historical bedrock, facts known past doubting" (fn 17).

**Fact #3 — the conversion of the persecutor Paul (pp. 83–84).** Paul's own claim to have encountered
the risen Jesus (fn 18); six further ancient sources (Luke, Clement of Rome, Polycarp, Tertullian,
Dionysius of Corinth, Origen) that he suffered and died for it. ⭐ **The reply to "people convert all
the time" is the sharpest move in the chapter:** virtually all converts hear the message from
*secondary* sources — what others tell them — whereas Paul's conversion rests on *primary* evidence, a
claimed first-hand encounter. Plus the unlikeliness of the candidate: his mindset was to oppose a
movement he believed followed a false Messiah, and he had "nothing to gain in this world" by inventing
it.

**Fact #4 — the conversion of the sceptic James (p. 84).** Mark and John both report that none of
Jesus' brothers believed in him (fn 19) — retained under the **principle of embarrassment**, since it
would be humiliating for a first-century rabbi not to have his own family as followers (fn 20). The 1
Cor 15 creed reports an appearance to James (fn 21); he becomes leader of the Jerusalem church (Acts
and Galatians, fn 22) and dies a martyr, attested by Christian *and* non-Christian sources (fn 23).

**Fact #5 — the empty tomb (p. 85).** ⭐ **Licona flags it as the weak link himself**, and this is the
one place the book's calibration matches ours exactly: the empty tomb "doesn't enjoy the nearly
universal consensus among scholars that the first four do" (fn 24), and **Habermas's figure is about
75 percent**, not "virtually all." Three strands: the **Jerusalem factor** (public execution and burial
in the same city where the resurrection was then proclaimed — Acts 2:32, fn 25; the authorities need
only have produced the corpse), **enemy attestation**, and the **testimony of women**. **Capture ends
here.**
