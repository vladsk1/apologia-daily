## Thesis and spine (our words)

Barnett's question is the historian's: *why* did Christianity happen, and what kind of man set it going? His answer
is about **time**. Our earliest Christian documents (Paul's letters, from about AD 48–50) are only about **twenty years
or less** after the crucifixion, and they already call Jesus Christ, Son of God and Lord, and pray to him to come back.
That christology barely changes from Paul's first letter to his last, so it was settled before the letters began. It
was also *received*, not invented by Paul. Barnett therefore pushes it back into the **one-year "corridor" between
Easter and Paul's conversion**. His slogan is that **christology gave birth to Christianity, not the reverse**. He then
reconstructs AD 33–48 from two independent windows, **Galatians 1–2 and Acts 1–12**. He argues that they agree on the
sequence of events, so neither is derived from the other (Momigliano's rule: original vs derivative authorities).

## Chapter map (our words; page refs = Kindle pages)

- **Preface; ch. 1 Introduction (pp. ix, 1–6).** The earliest records come from within about twenty years and already
  carry a high christology. Survey of rival explanations: Wrede's messianic secret; Casey (a Jewish prophet became a
  Gentile God); Crossan (a Cynic-type Jesus); Bousset (Hellenization); Akenson (Saul made Jesus Messiah). Barnett's
  replies include the failed-messiah contrast (Judas the Galilean, Theudas, Simon bar Giora) and the shortness of
  the time for such reinterpretation. **Chronology:** crucifixion "30 (or 33)", Paul in Corinth AD 50; Barnett prefers
  33. ⚠ **His endnote concedes that 33 is a minority view and that most scholars opt for 30** (ch. 3 n. 10).
- **Ch. 2 Early Christianity and the Study of History (pp. ~7–22).** Method and standpoint. The sources are Paul's
  undisputed letters plus Acts. The "we" passages make Luke an "equal primary source" for Paul (⚠ hazard: see verdict
  table). Acts' known difficulties are flagged by Barnett himself: Quirinius (Luke 2:2) and Theudas (Acts 5:36–37).
  **Momigliano's rule**, with a table showing that Paul and Acts give the same sequence for Paul's "unknown years"
  (Acts omits Arabia). Jerusalem's Hellenist synagogues and the **Theodotus inscription**. Damascus. Tacitus *Annals*
  15.44: the movement was "checked" and then broke out again in Judea.
- **Ch. 3 Time Borders and Christology (pp. ~23–26).** The core chronology. **Aretas IV died AD 40**, so Paul's escape
  from Damascus (2 Cor 11:32–33) and first Jerusalem visit fall before then, and the conversion is no later than 37.
  Luke 3:1 (Tiberius's 15th year = AD 29) plus astronomy (Passover 30 or 33) gives crucifixion AD 33. Claudius's
  expulsion of the Jews (Suetonius *Claud.* 25.4; Orosius) gives AD 49. The **Gallio inscription** (published 1905)
  puts the start of Gallio's year in summer 51, so Paul reached Corinth early in AD 50. Gal 2:1's "fourteen years" is
  counted from the call. Result: **conversion AD 34; first Jerusalem visit AD 36**; letters begin c. 48–50. "The
  chronology drives us to this conclusion": christology preceded Christianity.
- **Ch. 4 World History and Christian History (pp. ~27–40).** A synchronised timeline, 33–47:
  - Pilate dismissed in 36; Aretas defeats Antipas in 36; Tiberius dies 16 March 37; Caiaphas deposed in 37.
  - Caligula's statue crisis, 39–40. Barnett suggests it shaped the wording of **Mark 13:14** (masculine participle;
    "let the reader understand") and **2 Thess 2:4**. He does **not** claim Mark was written in 40.
  - Agrippa I's persecution, 41–44 (Acts 12); the famine of c. 46–47.
  - Believers were already in Damascus when Paul arrived, within a year of the crucifixion. The church was in Antioch
    by the mid-30s. The churches of Judea arose under Peter (Gal 1:22; 1 Thess 2:14).
  - The irony: Paul's persecution is what spread the movement.
- **Ch. 5 Mission to Greece: 1 Thessalonians (pp. ~41–54).** 1 Thessalonians (c. AD 50, from Corinth; authorship and
  text secure) is "a window into Christian beliefs in the year 50". It calls Jesus Son (1:10), Lord and Christ (1:1),
  and says he "died for us" (5:10). It appeals to what readers already "know", and it presupposes the churches of
  Judea "from the mid-30s" (2:14). **1 Thess 4:14 is a pre-formed credo**: "we believe", the citation formula *hoti*,
  and a symmetrical form. Paul received his core tradition most likely in **Damascus at his baptism**; the less likely
  alternative is the first Jerusalem visit. Either way it was formulated in Jerusalem. Antioch's influence was late
  and minimal, against Bousset and Bultmann, with Hengel. A table shows Son/Lord/Christ across 1 Thess and 1–2 Cor.
  1 Cor 8:6 adapts the Shema. *Marana tha*. Bauckham's "christology of divine identity"; Hurtado's "binitarian" shape.
- **Ch. 6 Between Jesus and Paul (1): Galatians 1 (pp. ~55–64).**
  - Galatians is the most historically detailed Pauline letter. It is defensive, so its raw facts are likely true.
  - **"The faith" (Gal 1:23) already existed** and was being persecuted, so it was formulated in the months before
    Paul's conversion.
  - Gal 4:4–5 is a possible early creed. *Abba* (Mark 14:36; Rom 8:15).
  - "After three years" is counted **inclusively**: two years and some months.
  - Arabia means preaching among the Nabateans, which explains Aretas's hostility (2 Cor 11:32). Barnett's own note
    cites Murphy-O'Connor's doubt that Paul went far into Nabatea.
  - ⚠ Hazards: flat "James was his brother"; the anti-"new perspective" verdict.
- **Ch. 7 Between Jesus and Paul (2): Acts 1–9 (pp. ~65–80).**
  - Only "a year or so" passed between Easter and Damascus.
  - Hebrews and Hellenists. Stephen's critique of the Temple. Saul was probably Stephen's accuser, a young teacher in
    the Hellenist synagogues (Acts 6:9).
  - Saul's motive: under Deut 21:23 a crucified Messiah is cursed.
  - **"Christology was born… AD 33–34."** Paul received it through revelation (Gal 1:16) and tradition
    (1 Cor 15:1–3); Barnett says the weighting cannot be determined.
  - ⚠ Barnett's note concedes that **F. F. Bruce** argued the opposite: Paul received the tradition in Jerusalem on the
    first visit (*Paul: Apostle of the Heart Set Free*, 83–94).
  - "All fingers point to Peter" as the formulator.
  - A parallel table of Gal 1 against Acts 1–9.
- **Ch. 8 "Christians" in Antioch (pp. ~81–86).**
  - Two waves reached Antioch: Jews only (c. 34), then Cypriots and Cyrenians preaching to Greeks.
  - *Christianoi* was an outsiders' political label for "adherents of the Christ" (compare *Herodianoi*). It is
    Messiah-centred, so it tells against Bousset's Antiochene origin for christology.
  - The Caligula and Alexandria troubles form the background.
  - Barnabas fetched Paul from Tarsus c. 44; the famine visit was c. 47 (Barnett identifies it with Gal 2).
- **Ch. 9 Earliest "Teaching": the Influence of Peter (pp. ~86–94).**
  - Philip's preaching echoes Peter's: the Servant (*pais*, Isa 53), "the Christ".
  - **Table: 1 Cor 15:3–5 and Acts 10:39–43** share content and wording: died, the Scriptures, "on the third day"
    (same phrase, word order reversed), appeared to chosen witnesses.
  - **Table: Rom 1:3–4 and Peter's Pentecost speech**: Scripture, David, Son/Christ, resurrection, Lord, Spirit.
    Rom 1:3–4 is pre-Pauline (the archaic "Spirit of holiness") and formed among Jews, most likely in Jerusalem.
  - Rom 6:17 and 16:17 use transmission vocabulary.
  - ⚠ Barnett's Peter-as-"rock" framing is a hazard.
- **Ch. 10 Into the Land of Israel, AD 34–39 (pp. ~95–108).**
  - Churches in Judea, Galilee and Samaria, and on the coastal plain (Lydda, Joppa, Caesarea), under Peter's
    oversight.
  - Acts' 3,000 and 5,000 may include pilgrims; **"many hundreds" in Jerusalem** is the safer figure.
  - Paul and Acts complement each other on **James**: Gal 1:18–19 (c. 36); Gal 2:9 (pillar, c. 47); Acts 12:17;
    15:13; 21:18. The brothers were unsympathetic during the ministry (Luke 8:19–21), then appear with the Twelve
    (Acts 1:14); 1 Cor 15:7 "most likely supplies the clue".
  - ⚠ The **James ossuary "may be genuine"**, a 2005 judgment (n. 16: C. A. Evans, *BBR* 13.1, 2003).
  - Hebrews dated c. 49–70; a speculative readership.
  - Paul's mission inside Israel was limited (Gal 1:22 against Acts 26:20).
- **Ch. 15 Final Reflection: What Cannot Be Denied (pp. 180–~185).**
  - History applies to faith claims (Wright on the resurrection). Conjecture must be flagged as conjecture.
  - **Boundaries.** All 27 NT texts within about 70 years; Mark within about 40; Paul's first letter within about 20.
    The span from Jesus to 1 Thessalonians equals the span from the end of WWI to the start of WWII.
  - **"Things difficult to deny."** (1) Jesus was proclaimed Messiah. Evidence: *Christianoi*; "another king"
    (Acts 17:7); Claudius and *Chrestus*. The titulus "king of the Jews" is authentic (A. E. Harvey): he was crucified
    for treason, not stoned. (2) The resurrection was fundamental (Acts 2–10; 1 Cor 15:4).
  - **The failed-messiah argument**: Judas the Galilean and Simon bar Giora left more trace in Josephus, but no
    movement survived them.
  - **The Davidide argument**: Vespasian, Domitian and Trajan watched David's family (Eusebius *H.E.* 3.12, 19, 22,
    i.e. Hegesippus). There were many Davidides, but only Jesus was hailed Messiah.
  - The "twin conviction" (Christ, raised) "alone accounts for" the church.
- **App. A History and Geography in Acts (pp. ~185–~197).**
  - Tacitus *Ann.* 15.44 parallels the turn from Luke into Acts.
  - Luke-Acts is one work, and its author was Paul's companion (Plummer, Fitzmyer, Hemer, Hengel; Muratorian
    lines 2–8; Irenaeus *AH* 3.1.1, 3.14.1).
  - Luke 1:1–4: "many" had compiled *written* narratives, "handed over" (*paredosan*) to him.
  - Acts is primarily history: speeches about 30% of the book; Claudius; the conventions of Hellenistic historians.
  - Persons and topography are accurate. Selwyn: 1 Peter and Peter's Acts speeches. Aramaisms (Torrey, Wilcox).
  - **Honest concessions:**
    - **Theudas**: critics "rightly point to problems". His answers: a notorious contemporary; many uprisings; a
      short form of a common name.
    - The first-Jerusalem-visit accounts (Gal 1:18–22 against Acts 9:26–29) are "not easily resolved", though partly
      reconcilable.
    - n. 39: **both** Lukan cruxes (Luke 2:1–5 and Theudas) involve Quirinius's census, and Luke may have relied on a
      flawed source.
  - Verdicts against Acts: Haenchen, Conzelmann. For it: Mommsen, Eduard Meyer, Hengel, **Sherwin-White 1963 p. 189**,
    **Hemer 1989 p. 412**.
- **App. B Dating Galatians (pp. ~197–~203).**
  - South Galatia, written from Syrian Antioch in the late 40s. Strabo, Pliny and Ptolemy put Pisidia in Galatia, and
    the Pednelissus inscription calls it "a city of Galatia". "Phrygian Galatia" in Acts 16:6.
  - The early-date argument rests on "so quickly" (1:6), on the autobiography ending at the first Galatian mission,
    and on the collection still being at its genesis.
  - ⚠ Barnett's notes 9–11 cite "T. S. Mitchell" (*ABD* "Galatia") and a "Hagar, Ishmael, Josephus…" piece in ways
    that appear **crossed** (that article is Fergus Millar's, *JJS* 44, 1993). **Verify any Mitchell/Millar
    attribution independently**; never cite it through Barnett.
  - If Galatians is c. 48 and the crucifixion 33, the gap is about 15 years.
- **App. C Reflections on Crossan (pp. ~203–~206).** Crossan's 1998 *Birth of Christianity* says almost nothing about
  the years after the execution, despite its subtitle. Barnett's criticisms:
  - His biblical index has no Acts reference, and he ignores Hengel.
  - His socio-economic reconstruction of Galilee is conjectural.
  - His "illiterate peasant" Jesus fails to fit a rabbi with a fishing-business circle and Joanna among his followers.
  - He has no theory of Jesus' death.
  - He leans on a "Q Gospel" plus Thomas, Peter and the Didache.
  - The ethical echoes of Jesus in Paul, James and 1 Peter mark early catechesis, not a "real" Galilean Jesus.
