## Ch. 11 — Sean McDowell, "The Deaths of the Apostles" (pp. 179–199)

### The spine

The chapter **concedes the popular version of the argument and rebuilds a narrower one.** Its
opening move is to quote Bart Ehrman's dismissal (Ehrman blog, 13 Oct 2017) of the apologists' claim
that *all* the apostles were martyred, and then to **grant Ehrman the point**: we do not know how
most of the apostles died, and apologists have overstated both the evidence and its significance.

**The salvaged form** — this is the sentence the whole chapter exists to establish: because of their
sincere belief that Jesus had risen and appeared to them, the apostles **willingly suffered and were
prepared to die** for their public proclamation of the risen Christ. They did not invent the stories.

**Three limits McDowell puts on his own argument — carry all three:**
1. **It does not require that all, or even any, apostles died as martyrs.** The load-bearing claim is
   that they were *willing* to suffer. Actual martyrdoms strengthen it; the argument survives without
   them, so it survives even if Ehrman is right about the later traditions.
2. **It does not establish the truth of the resurrection, and does not defeat the hallucination
   hypothesis.** It establishes **sincerity** — that they were not liars.
3. **It does not depend on Christians being the only people willing to die for their convictions.**
   Maccabean and pagan precedents exist. The distinguishing question is *what* the martyrs died for.

### The two-part precondition (why the chapter spends its middle on persecution)

Before the martyrdom evidence, McDowell argues two things must hold: (a) the apostles had a
**resurrection faith**, and (b) **Christians really were persecuted in the first century**.

**On (a) — the centrality of the resurrection.** Early creeds; **1 Cor 15:3–7** dated roughly
twenty-five years after the crucifixion, embedding a tradition earlier than 1 Corinthians itself
(AD 54–55). **Habermas's five reasons the creedal nature is widely accepted:** the technical
transmission verbs *paradidōmi* / *paralambanō*; non-Pauline vocabulary; stylised oral form;
Semitic indications (the Aramaic **Cephas**); and the triple "and that" plus the Scripture-fulfilment
reference indicating Hebraic narration. On *when* Paul received it, McDowell **declines to pick** —
he argues only that Paul had ample opportunity and motive.

**On (b) — persecution.** ⭐ **The most useful net-new lead in the chapter:** Eckhard J. Schnabel's
survey concludes the only Roman provinces with **no reported persecution** are **Cyprus, Cilicia and
Pamphylia** (plus Spain and Egypt, where no first-century missionary activity is recorded at all).

### The martyrdom evidence, apostle by apostle

| Apostle | Strength | Key sources |
|---|---|---|
| **Peter** | Strong | **John 21:18–19** (a *first-century* reference, within ~3 decades of his death); **1 Clement 5:1–4**; *Ascension of Isaiah* 4:2–3; Ignatius, Dionysius of Corinth, Irenaeus, Tertullian |
| **Paul** | Strong | **1 Clement 5:5–7** (written *in Rome*, the traditional execution site, in a position to know); Ignatius *Eph.* 12:2; Polycarp *Phil.* 9:1–2 |
| **James son of Zebedee** | Good | **Acts 12:2** — McDowell's argument is from its *brevity*: two lines where Stephen gets two chapters, no legendary embellishment, no parallels drawn to Jesus. "It reads like an official execution." |
| **James brother of Jesus** | Good | **Josephus, *Ant.* 20.197–203** (largely undisputed, unlike the Testimonium); Hegesippus via Eusebius *H.E.* 2.23.8–18 (legendary accretion, but affirms stoning); attested by Jews, Gnostics *and* Christians |
| **All others** | ⚠ **Unknown** | Andrew and Thomas have "moderately early" traditions. **Bartholomew's skinning first appears c. AD 500.** McDowell: "we simply do not know what happened to most of them." |

⚠⚠ **Hostile-witness concessions worth more than our own assertions:** Ehrman grants that John
21:18–19 has Peter told he will be executed and that this is a martyr's death; that it was widely
known by the end of the first century that Peter had been martyred; and that Polycarp was aware of a
tradition that Paul and other apostles were martyred.

### ⭐ The "nobody recanted" argument — and why it is *not* a glib argument from silence

We already assert this on several pages. **McDowell supplies the reasoning we lack**, and it is two
independent moves:
1. When the early church argued about how to treat believers who **faltered under persecution**, a
   tradition of an apostle recanting would certainly have been deployed. None was.
2. **Early critics — Celsus, Lucian, Porphyry — would have been eager to cite such a tradition.**
   Their silence is the telling kind.

### The two objections he answers

- **"The apostles were never offered the chance to recant"** (Candida Moss). He **grants** there is no
  early record of such an offer, then denies it carries the weight: they ministered knowingly in
  threatening environments, following a leader crucified as an enemy of Rome, having seen the fates
  of John the Baptist, Stephen and Jesus, and having been warned to expect it.
- **"Others have died for their beliefs."** The reply is the **eyewitness/secondhand distinction**:
  modern martyrs die for what they sincerely believe on testimony received from others; the apostles
  claimed to have been there.
  ⚠⚠ **DO NOT PORT HIS ILLUSTRATION.** McDowell makes the point via the 9/11 attackers (his own
  fn. 61 concedes that people who kill for their faith are murderers, not martyrs). **The
  distinction is sound and we should use it; the example is inflammatory on a public page, adds
  nothing to the logic, and would fail our own tone guardrail.** Use a neutral case.

### Conclusion, in his own framing

Popular apologists have overstated both the evidence and its significance. Carefully construed, the
argument is **one piece of a larger case**: the apostles' willingness to suffer does not prove
Christianity true, but it shows **they were not liars**.

## Ch. 16 — Michael R. Licona, "The Primacy of Paul" (pp. 289–301, 310–313)

**Thesis:** Paul is our **best ancient source** for the resurrection. Licona gives five reasons;
**four are confirmed here.**

1. **Paul's letters are early.** Confining himself to the seven undisputed letters, and taking Paul's
   martyrdom as a *terminus ad quem* of AD 65: a maximum of ~35 years from the crucifixion (AD 30 or
   33), with all but Philippians and Philemon written AD 48–58 — **15 to 28 years.** He documents the
   dating range from a spread of mainstream introductions rather than asserting it.
2. **Paul had been an enemy.** Gal 1:13–14, 1:23; 1 Cor 15:9; Phil 3:5–6; Acts 7:58; 9:1–2; 22:3–5;
   26:9–12. Not a disciple, not a secret sympathiser — a man who thought the movement had to be crushed.
3. **Paul believed he was an eyewitness.** 1 Cor 9:1; 15:8; Acts 9:3–20, 22:6–16, 26:12–18. And his
   willingness to suffer and eventually be martyred shows he sincerely believed what he preached.
4. **Paul is a link to the preaching of the Jerusalem apostles.** Gal 1:18–19 (fifteen days with
   Peter, plus James); Gal 2:1–10 (Peter, James and John, "those reputed to be pillars," verifying
   his gospel); Acts 9:26–27, 11:27–30, 15:1–13, 21:18–19. **C. H. Dodd's line** — that they can
   hardly have spent that fortnight discussing the weather — is quoted via **Birger Gerhardsson**.
5. ⚠ **NOT READ** (pp. 302–309). Do not guess.

**On the nature of the resurrection (pp. 310–313):** Paul held resurrection to involve a
transformation *of the corpse*, so his view is **consistent with the Gospels**, not in tension with
them. He reads **Acts 13:32–37** (Ps 16, "did not experience decay") as implying a body that did not
decay. He also handles the objection from the *manner* of the appearance to Paul — bright light
(*periastraptō* / *perilampō*) in Acts vs. the tangible, meal-eating Jesus of the Gospels — by noting
the appearance to Paul came **after the ascension**.

**His conclusion:** we have an early source, hostile to Jesus before his experience, who suffered
continually and was martyred, and who had personal contact with the Jerusalem leadership including
Peter, John and James — who certified his message. So Paul's letters are a **tool for detecting the
content of the Jerusalem apostles' preaching**, which included bodily resurrection as a historical
event.

## ⚠️ Calibration flags (carry into any use)

1. **This chapter is a CORRECTION, not an amplification.** Its dominant contribution is telling
   apologists to claim *less*. Any content that mines it for stronger claims has inverted it.
2. **"Willing to suffer," never "all were martyred."** Only four apostolic deaths are well attested.
3. **Establishes sincerity, not truth** — and explicitly **does not defeat the hallucination
   hypothesis.** Never let it carry more.
4. **Drop the 9/11 illustration.** Keep the eyewitness/secondhand distinction; find another example.
5. **Habermas's own restraint is a model:** he does not include James son of Zebedee's martyrdom in
   his minimal facts, even while McDowell judges the evidence good.
6. Ehrman's concessions here are **specific and limited** — quote them at their actual scope.
