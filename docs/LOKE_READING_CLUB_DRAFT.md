## Session 1 — Chapter 1: Ruling out every alternative, one by one

**Summary (our words).** Most defences of the resurrection build a cumulative case from a handful of
agreed facts. Loke takes a different route. Up front he sets out *every* logically possible explanation
of the two things that need explaining — the reports that people saw Jesus alive again, and what became
of his body — and then works through the list, eliminating each natural option until one remains. For
the appearances he distinguishes six possibilities (from "the stories are a later legend" through to
"they really did see the risen Jesus"); for the fate of the body, eight. The argument is staged so that
showing people genuinely *experienced* someone they took to be the risen Jesus does not, at this first
stage, require anything supernatural; the further question of what ultimately caused it is deliberately
held back. It is his direct answer to the common objection that "you can always dream up another
alternative."

**Discussion questions.**
1. Loke argues his list of explanations is *logically exhaustive*. Why would that matter for the
   argument — and what would it take to be confident a list really leaves nothing out?
2. He keeps two questions apart: "did something happen?" and "what caused it?" Why separate them, and
   what does that protect the argument from?
3. Which natural explanation seems strongest to you going in — the one you'd most want to see answered
   as the book proceeds?

## Session 2 — Chapter 2: Was the claim even made? (ruling out legend)

**Summary (our words).** Before asking whether the appearances were real, Loke asks whether they were
even *claimed* — because if the resurrection reports were a legend that grew up generations later, there
is no early historical fact to explain. He argues the claim is early and firsthand. The list of
witnesses Paul hands on in 1 Corinthians 15 is widely dated by scholars to within a few years of the
crucifixion, and Paul points to witnesses still living — "most of whom are still alive" — people a
reader could in principle have gone and questioned. He also argues that the well-known differences
between the resurrection accounts are not the fingerprints of invention but of *independent* witnesses
describing the same events from different angles.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Why does it matter so much whether the resurrection was claimed *early* rather than told as a legend
   decades later?
2. Paul appeals to witnesses who were still alive when he wrote. How much weight should that "you could
   go and ask them" point carry?
3. When independent witnesses to any event differ on details, is that usually a mark of unreliability —
   or of independence? How should we weigh that when we read the Gospels side by side?

## Session 3 — Chapter 3: Were they lying? (ruling out a hoax)

**Summary (our words).** If people really did claim to have seen the risen Jesus, could they simply have
made it up? Loke argues not — and states the case carefully. The point is **not** "they died, therefore
it must be true," but that the first witnesses were willing to suffer for a claim they were uniquely
placed to know was either true or false, and people do not, as a rule, suffer for what they know they
invented. He is candid that scholars debate exactly how many of the apostles were actually martyred, and
he does not overstate it. He then adds a sharper point that is often missed: for devout first-century
Jews, inventing a resurrection would mean bearing false witness about God himself — "we are found to be
misrepresenting God" (1 Corinthians 15:15) — something a sincere monotheist would have dreaded.

**Discussion questions.**
1. What is the difference between "they died for it, so it's true" and "they were willing to suffer for
   what they were in a position to know firsthand"? Why does the distinction matter?
2. Loke openly grants that the number of apostolic martyrdoms is debated. Does conceding that *strengthen*
   or *weaken* the overall argument, in your view?
3. Why would "we are found to be misrepresenting God" weigh especially heavily on a first-century Jew —
   and how does that bear against the idea that the first witnesses were lying?
