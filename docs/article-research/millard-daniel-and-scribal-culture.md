## A · "Daniel in Babylon: An Accurate Record?" (2012)

### Thesis (our words)
A tightly bounded question: **not** when Daniel was written, but whether its narratives about the
Babylonian court *reflect the sixth-century world accurately*. Millard works through the standard list of
alleged blunders — the date in Daniel 1:1, Nebuchadnezzar's madness, "Chaldeans" as a class of wise men,
Belshazzar as king, "Darius the Mede," the 120 satraps, the Persian/Greek/Aramaic vocabulary — and argues
that most of them dissolve on closer inspection of the cuneiform and epigraphic record, while the
remainder (chiefly Darius the Mede) are **open questions, not demonstrated errors**.

### ⚠⚠ THE LOAD-BEARING GUARDRAIL — read this before any use
**Millard opens by conceding, in his own voice, that "almost all commentators deduce that the book could
only have been composed in the second century BC,"** precisely because the *forecasts* are so detailed and
match known history so closely. **He does not argue for a sixth-century date of the book, and neither may
we on his authority.** His claim is narrower and different in kind: the Babylonian *narratives* preserve
accurate memories of the Babylonian court. Turning "Daniel gets Belshazzar right" into "so Daniel was
written in the sixth century" would be exactly the overstatement `apologia-argument` exists to catch — and
it would also misrepresent the source. Keep it at: **an accurate record of the setting, whatever the date
of composition.** (Same family as the manuscripts/preservation-not-truth rule and the
archaeology-corroborates-the-setting rule.)

### Argument map → primaries to VERIFY
**1 · Daniel 1:1 (Jehoiakim's "third year").** Usually called an outright error, since Jehoiakim's third
year would predate Carchemish (summer 605). Millard shows a **calendrical combination that accommodates
it**: an *autumn* (Tishri) New Year plus the *accession-year* system puts Jehoiakim's third year at
Sept/Oct 606 – Sept/Oct 605. → *Primaries:* **Dan 1:1; Jer 25:1; 46:1–2; 2 Kgs 24:1, 7; Jer 52:28 vs.
2 Kgs 24:12** (the seventh/eighth-year discrepancy that reveals the two calendars); the **Babylonian
Chronicle** (Glassner, *Mesopotamian Chronicles*, SBLWAW 19 [2004], 228–31). The Assyrian analogy for
attributing a general's campaign to the king: Shalmaneser III and his *turtan* Dayyan-Ashur (Grayson,
*RIMA* 3, 69–71). ⚠ Millard's own bound: *possible*, not proved — "if the autumn New Year and the
accession-year dating are not acceptable, then there is probably no alternative to assuming an error."
Carry that.
**2 · Nebuchadnezzar's madness (Dan 4).** Babylonian records are almost silent for ~30 years of his
43-year reign, which leaves room — and Millard **himself calls this "reminiscent of a 'god of the gaps'
approach"** and says it should not be leaned on. Better leads: **Berossus** via **Josephus** (Burstein,
*The Babyloniaca of Berossus* [1978], 28) reports the king falling ill after beginning his building works;
sick kings were not necessarily deposed (cf. **Uzziah, 2 Kgs 15:5–6**; George III). On the **Qumran
"Prayer of Nabonidus" (4Q242)**: the usual claim is that it preserves a more primitive form of Daniel 4;
Millard notes the real differences (Teima not Babylon; boils not animal-like madness; no boasting about
building works) and — **importantly for honesty** — that **Frank Cross's restoration of line 3 as "I came
to be like [the beasts]" is no longer supported** (Peter W. Flint, "The Daniel Tradition at Qumran," in
Collins & Flint, eds., *The Book of Daniel* [Brill, 2001], 329–67, esp. 332–36). → *Primaries:* Collins,
"4QPrayer of Nabonidus," in *Qumran Cave 4, XVII* (Clarendon, 1996), 83–93; A. Steinmann, *RevQ* 20
(2002): 557–70, for the reverse-dependence case.
**3 · "Chaldeans" as a class of wise men (Dan 2:2, 4, 10 etc.).** Called an "undoubted anachronism"
(Porteous). Millard's reply is symmetrical and careful: there is **no evidence for *kaldāyu* as a
professional name in Babylonian texts — but also no occurrence of it as an ethnic term in the royal
inscriptions of Nebuchadnezzar, his father, or his successors**, so calling the professional sense
anachronistic is as unfounded as calling it sixth-century. Analogy: the **Magi**, one of Herodotus's six
Median tribes (*Hist.* 1.101), who became *the* name for religious functionaries (R. N. Frye, *The
Heritage of Persia* [1962], 76). The Hebrew *kaśdim* vs. Greek *Chaldaioi* difference is explained by a
known Akkadian sibilant+dental → l+dental shift — which tells **for** the Hebrew form's antiquity, not
against it. → *Primaries:* **Herodotus** 1.181–82 (Chaldeans as priests of Bel); von Soden, *GAG* §30g;
Saggs, "Ur of the Chaldees," *Iraq* 22 (1960): 200–209, at 206 n. 39.
**4 · Belshazzar (Dan 5).** The classic case. → *Primaries:* the **Nabonidus cylinders from the Ur
ziggurat** (found 1854; more in the 1960s) praying for the health of "the crown prince Belshazzar"; oath
formulas naming Nabonidus *and* the crown prince — a formulation unknown in earlier reigns; the
**Nabonidus Chronicle** ("the crown prince, his officers and his troops stayed in" Babylonia);
the **"Verse Account"** poem, which says Nabonidus "entrusted the kingship to him" (ANET 313). The
scholarly exchange to reproduce fairly: **H. H. Rowley**, *JTS* 32 (1930): 12–31 (no text titles him king,
no document is dated by his reign → Daniel errs) vs. **E. J. Young**, *A Commentary on Daniel* (1949),
115 ff. (*de facto* not *de jure*). Millard's fresh supporting datum: the **Tell Halaf (Gozan) bilingual
statue**, where the same ruler is *šākin māti* ("governor") in Assyrian and *mlk* ("king") in Aramaic —
each title fitting its audience (Millard & Bordreuil, *BA* 45 [1982]: 135–41, at 139). Cf. the thirty-two
"kings" with Ben-Hadad (**1 Kgs 20:1, 12, 16**). **"Third place in the kingdom" (Dan 5:16)** follows: the
first two ranks were taken. **Ancestry (Dan 5:11, 18 "father"):** Millard sets out the speculation that
Nabonidus married a daughter of Nebuchadnezzar and **explicitly labels it speculation** — do not present
it as fact.
**5 · ⭐ "Darius the Mede" (Dan 5:31; 6:28; 9:1) — the genuinely NEW lead for us.** Millard's preferred
solution is **D. J. Wiseman's 1957 proposal that Darius the Mede *is* Cyrus**, resting on a **waw
explicativum** reading of **Dan 6:28** ("during the reign of Darius, *that is to say*, the reign of Cyrus
the Persian"), exactly parallel to **1 Chronicles 5:26**, where "Pul king of Assyria and Tiglath-pileser
king of Assyria" are certainly one man (RSV and later versions already translate it that way).
⭐ **The updating datum:** the objection used to be that *Pulu* was never a contemporary name for
Tiglath-pileser (it survived only in a late king-list, the Bible, and Greek sources). A **Phoenician
inscription from his own reign — the Incirli trilingual — refers to him four times as p'l.** → *Primary:*
**Stephen A. Kaufman, "The Phoenician Inscription of the Incirli Trilingual," *Maarav* 14.2 (2007): 7–26,
at 18.** Supporting: Cyrus's Median ancestry and his being addressed as "king of the Medes" (**Herodotus**
1.91, 1.206; **Xenophon,** *Cyropaedia* 1.2.1); Persian throne names (Artaxerxes I/II = Arses; Darius II =
Ochus); Cyrus was ~61 when he took Babylon vs. Daniel's "about sixty-two."
⚠⚠ **Hold this LOOSELY and state the counter-evidence.** Millard himself says **"the question… remains
open,"** notes **no earlier Xerxes is attested** for Dan 9:1's "son of Xerxes," notes the Medes left no
written records to settle it, and concedes the hypothesis has been fairly criticised as **"difficult to
falsify"** — his reply is only that *the alternatives are equally unfalsifiable*, which is a tie, not a
win. Also record the alternatives on the page: **H. H. Rowley,** *Darius the Mede and the Four World
Empires* (Cardiff, 1935) — the classic case that he is fictional; **J. C. Whitcomb,** *Darius the Mede*
(Eerdmans, 1959) — the Gubaru identification; **Lester L. Grabbe,** *CBQ* 50 (1988): 198–213. Anything we
publish here must read as *"one live proposal among several, none demonstrable,"* never as *"archaeology
has solved Darius the Mede."*
**6 · The Medes, and the sequence of kingdoms.** "Medes and Persians" (Dan 6:8, 12, 15; 8:20) vs. Esther's
inverted "Persians and Medes" (Est 1:3, 14, 18, 19; contrast 10:2) tracks the real sixth→fifth-century
shift in usage, mirrored in the Greek habit of calling the Persian wars "the Median War." → *Primaries:*
**Isa 13:17; 21:2; Jer 51:11, 28; 25:25; 2 Kgs 17:6; 18:11**; the **Nabonidus Chronicle** on Cyrus of
Anshan defeating Astyages (550) and taking Ecbatana; the **Cyrus Cylinder**/ANET 126 on Medes as
Nabonidus's enemies; David Graf, "Medism," *JHS* 104 (1984): 15–30.
**7 · The 120 satraps (Dan 6:1)** vs. Herodotus's twenty satrapies (*Hist.* 3.89). Millard's reply: the
Babylonian/Aramaic administrative word actually used is *pīḫatu / pḥh*, whose range covers both vast
territories and small ones like Judah or Samaria — so the terminology was looser than Herodotus implies.
→ *Primaries:* *CAD* P, 366b; Petit, *JBL* 107 (1988): 53–67; Stolper, *JNES* 48 (1989): 283–305; Briant,
*From Cyrus to Alexander* (Eisenbrauns, 2002), 712; Kuhrt, *The Ancient Near East* (1995), 689.
**8 · The languages — answering S. R. Driver's famous verdict.** Driver (*Introduction*, 9th ed. [1913],
508) held the Greek words *demand*, the Hebrew *supports*, and the Aramaic *permits* a post-332 BC date.
Millard's update: the **Aramaic of Daniel is now widely agreed to be Persian-period** (Kitchen, "The
Aramaic of Daniel," in Wiseman et al., *Notes on Some Problems in the Book of Daniel* [Tyndale, 1956],
31–79; Kutscher; Folmer, *The Aramaic Language in the Achaemenid Period* [Peeters, 1995], 753–55); the
**Greek words are all names of musical instruments**, and Greeks are now attested living and owning
property in Nebuchadnezzar's Babylon (the Jehoiachin ration tablets — Weidner 1939; ANET 308; Mitchell,
*PEQ* 124 [1992]: 124–43, esp. 134–40); and Persian words in a Persian-period book are unremarkable.
⚠ **Note the honest half:** Millard says the **Hebrew** "might support a second-century date" and could
equally support an earlier one — the linguistic-dating debate is itself unsettled (Ian Young, ed.,
*Biblical Hebrew: Studies in Chronology and Typology* [T&T Clark, 2003]; Young & Rezetko, *Linguistic
Dating of Biblical Texts* [Equinox, 2008]). **This does not settle the date, and must not be sold as if
it did.**
**9 · The methodological close (the most portable thing in the chapter).** Lone references long treated as
suspect have repeatedly proved correct — **Belshazzar** is one; **Sargon II**, known only from **Isaiah
20:1** until his cuneiform inscriptions were deciphered in the mid-nineteenth century, is another. →
*Primary:* Steven W. Holladay, "The Quest for Sargon, Pul, and Tiglath-pileser in the Nineteenth Century,"
in Chavalas & Younger, eds., *Mesopotamia and the Bible* (Sheffield Academic, 2002), 68–87, esp. 68–71.
Millard's own rider on Kenton Sparks: a human view about the Bible being *believed* doesn't ensure its
correctness — **"nor its error."** Millard also contrasts Daniel's accuracy on these points with the
looser handling of Babylon in **Judith and Tobit** (Millard, "Judith, Tobit, Ahiqar and History," in
*New Heaven and New Earth*, VTSup 77 [Brill, 1999], 195–203).
⚠ **DENOMINATIONAL NEUTRALITY FLAG:** Judith and Tobit are **deuterocanonical/apocryphal — canonical for
Catholics and Orthodox.** Millard's comparison is a *historiographical* one about how each text handles
Babylonian detail. If we ever use it, frame it strictly that way and **never** as an argument about
canonical status. (Same flag as `book-research/in-defense-of-the-bible.md` ch. 16.)

## B · "The Knowledge of Writing in Iron Age Palestine" (*TynBul* 46.2, 1995)

### Thesis (our words)
No Hebrew books survive from Iron Age Palestine, and the epigraphic haul is thin next to Egypt's or
Mesopotamia's. Millard argues the surviving scraps nonetheless attest a **varied, everyday use of writing
that reached beyond palace and temple scribal circles**, and that — on the ancient Near Eastern analogies
— written *literature* could well have existed in the land from the tenth century BC onward. His verdict
is a **possibility argument**, deliberately: the scarcity of tenth–ninth-century inscriptions is a
Fertile-Crescent-wide phenomenon and is best explained as archaeological accident, not as evidence of
illiteracy.

### Argument map → primaries to VERIFY
- **The Samaria ostraca** (102 sherds, citadel, found 1910; early eighth century by stratigraphy and
  pottery): routine delivery notes for wine and oil. 16 of 27 place names match villages known in the last
  century; 7 match the Manasseh tribal lists (**Num 26:29–34; Josh 17:1–13** — Abiezer, Shemida, Heleq);
  52 personal names, 11 formed with *yw*, 6–7 with *b'l*. The point is their **ephemerality**: casual
  administrative scribbling, visible to porters and drivers — writing as ordinary, not esoteric. →
  *Primaries:* Lemaire, *Inscriptions hébraïques: Les ostraca* (Cerf, 1977), 23–81; Renz & Röllig,
  *Handbuch der althebräischen Epigraphik* (1995), 79–109; Crowfoot/Kenyon, *Samaria-Sebaste III* (PEF,
  1957) for the ~50 clay bullae with **papyrus fibre marks on the reverse** — the perished scrolls'
  fingerprints.
- **Comparative density of literacy:** at **Ur**, two houses in five in eighteenth-century BC residential
  areas held cuneiform tablets; similar in seventh-century **Assur**. → Woolley/Mallowan, *Ur Excavations
  VII* (British Museum, 1976), 12–29, 95–168; Pedersén, *Archives and Libraries in the City of Assur* II
  (1986).
- **Why no Israelite royal monuments?** Millard answers *archaeological accident*, and gives the killer
  control case: **not one monumental inscription of Herod — the greatest builder the land has seen — has
  been found within his realm**, nor any from the Hasmoneans; the **Pilate Stone** itself survived only by
  being reused as a staircase block (Frova, *Istituto Lombardo Rendiconti* 95 [1961]: 419–34). Meanwhile
  small Levantine states demonstrably *could* erect stelae: the **Moabite Stone** and the **Tel Dan
  inscription** (Biran & Naveh, *IEJ* 43 [1993]: 81–98; *IEJ* 45 [1995]: 1–18).
- **Traces of actual literature:** the **Deir 'Alla Balaam plaster text**, deliberately laid out to look
  like a scroll column (C-14 range 880–770 BC, most probably ~800); the **Ketef Hinnom silver amulets**
  bearing the Priestly Benediction of **Num 6:24–26**, rolled up and never meant to be read (Barkay, *Tel
  Aviv* 19 [1992]: 139–92); **Arad Ostracon 88** ("I became king"); the **Horvat 'Uza literary ostracon**
  (Beit-Arieh, *Tel Aviv* 20 [1993]: 55–63, with an appendix by F. M. Cross); the **Wadi Murabba'at
  papyrus** (~600 BC), reused from letter to ration list.
- **The named opponent, stated fairly:** **T. L. Thompson**, *Early History of the Israelite People*
  (Brill, 1992), 391 — "we cannot seek an origin of literature in Palestine prior to the eighth, or perhaps
  even better the seventh century." Millard's reply is that this rests on hypothetical considerations
  rather than on the written or archaeological sources.
⚠ **Scope bound:** this argues that *writing and written literature were available*, **not** that any
particular biblical book was written early, and **not** that ordinary Israelites were literate in the
modern sense (Millard's claim is "some people who could read and write within reach of most towns and
villages"). Do not upgrade "writing existed" into "the Pentateuch is Mosaic" or into a literacy-rate claim.
⚠ **Age caveat:** 1995. The literacy debate has moved a great deal since (Tel Aviv University's
handwriting-analysis work on the Arad ostraca; Rollston's more sceptical *Writing and Literacy in the
World of Ancient Israel*). Any use must check the current state of the question — cite Millard's
*primaries*, not his 1995 assessment of the field.

## Guardrail fit (both pieces)
- **Setting, not message.** Both pieces corroborate that biblical narratives fit the world they claim to
  describe. Neither shows the events happened or the message is true — the same bound
  `library/archaeology.html` already models well.
- **The Daniel-dating line is the sharpest risk here.** See the ⚠⚠ block above. Millard concedes the
  near-consensus second-century date and argues only for the accuracy of the Babylonian material. Our
  `library/daniel70.html` is about the *Seventy Weeks prophecy* and already engages the Maccabean dating —
  nothing Millard says licenses moving that.
- **Darius the Mede must stay a live proposal**, with Rowley/Whitcomb/Grabbe on the page. Overselling it
  would be a textbook manufactured-confidence failure (`CLAUDE.md` § *The mission*).
- **Denominational neutrality:** the Judith/Tobit comparison (see flag above).
- **Author tilt:** Millard was an evangelical Assyriologist at Liverpool, but a cautious, low-overstatement
  one who repeatedly labels his own arguments "possible," "speculation," or "a god-of-the-gaps approach."
  **The overstatement risk here is ours in compression, not his in writing.** He is a good tone model.
