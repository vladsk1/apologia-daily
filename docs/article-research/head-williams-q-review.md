## Thesis (our words)
A review article surveying the state of Q scholarship c. 2003 — the works arguing *against* Q, the
International Q Project's *Critical Edition of Q*, Kloppenborg Verbin's *Excavating Q*, and Maurice
Casey's *An Aramaic Approach to Q*. Its single load-bearing conclusion: **contrary to what several of
these studies imply, the actual wording of Q cannot be reconstructed with any confidence, in Greek or in
Aramaic.** Note carefully what this is and is not — it is a **methodological caution about a scholarly
reconstruction**, not a denial that Matthew and Luke shared material, and certainly not an apologetic
claim about the Gospels' reliability.

## Argument map → primaries to VERIFY

**Frame.** "Q" is used two ways: (a) simply the **double tradition** — the ~235 verses common to Matthew
and Luke but absent from Mark; (b) a hypothesised written **source** (*Quelle*) used independently by
both. The features argued to require (b) are close verbal agreement, substantial agreement in order, and
alternating primitivity. The authors note the drift by which "the boring old Sayings Source" became "the
Sayings Gospel," and that the Jesus Seminar made Q's existence one of its seven pillars (**Funk & Hoover,
eds., *The Five Gospels*** [Polebridge, 1993], 9–19). Mark Goodacre's barb is worth verifying and is
genuinely striking: Theissen & Merz's *The Historical Jesus* are "more willing to discuss doubts about
the existence of Jesus than… doubts about the existence of Q" (**Goodacre, *The Case Against Q*** [Trinity
Press International, 2002], 12).

**1 · The anti-Q works, assessed evenhandedly.** The neo-Griesbachian *Beyond the Q Impasse: Luke's Use of
Matthew* (ed. McNicol, Peabody & Dungan, 1996) and *One Gospel from Two: Mark's Use of Matthew and Luke*
(2002) are criticised for **assuming their hypothesis and mapping it out rather than testing it against
rivals**, with the linguistic evidence at pp. 21–24 judged "exceedingly weak" (the Luke 7:1 / Matt 7:28
parallel is cited in English because *the Greek shares not a single word*). **Goodacre's *Case Against Q*
gets a much warmer hearing** — a "refreshing and well-written challenge to the consensus," strong on the
minor agreements the two-source theory handles *ad hoc* — but the authors conclude he does not
*establish* Luke's use of Matthew as more plausible than a common source.
→ *Primaries to verify:* the three books above; F. Neirynck, "Q: From Source to Gospel," *ETL* 71 (1995):
421–30; Derrenbacker & Kloppenborg Verbin, *JBL* 120 (2001): 57–76.

**2 · ⭐ The core critique — the *Critical Edition of Q*.** (Robinson, Hoffmann & Kloppenborg, eds.,
*The Critical Edition of Q* [Peeters/Fortress, 2000].) The authors' objections, in our words:
- **The title and format are rhetorical.** "Critical edition" normally means editing a text from its
  **extant manuscripts**; here it is applied to a redactional-critical reconstruction of a *hypothetical*
  text. **561 pages to present ~4,500 words in ~250 verses.** They quote the project's own admission that
  it "refrained from entering into the never-ending discussion over the existence of Q," preferring to
  reconstruct the text "on the assumption that this may in the end be a more compelling… argument for its
  existence" (*CEQ*, lxvii n. 156), and Robinson's remark that a facile dismissal of Q "is harder to carry
  off with The Critical Text of Q open on the desk."
- **The sigla are unwieldy and, worse, unargued.** Their worked example is **the shortest verse in Q,
  4:13** (*kai ho diabolos aphiēsin auton*) — nine layers of brackets, braces, parentheses and raised
  numerals for six words. The decisive complaint: **the arguments are absent.** Why *kai* rather than
  *tote*? Which position for *ho diabolos*? The *CEQ* gives probability ratings (compressed from five to
  three) with no evidence, unlike the UBS *Greek New Testament* and Metzger's *Textual Commentary*, which
  the authors name as the better model.
- **No manuscript evidence for Matthew and Luke** — the very texts the reconstruction is built from — so
  it is not an adequate tool for studying the tradition as it actually appears in the manuscripts
  (Aland's *Synopsis* or NA27 remains a necessary accompaniment).
- Verdict in our words: **the wording of Q can never be reconstructed with certainty**, and the
  presentation obscures rather than exposes that.

**3 · ⭐⭐ The Casey critique — the part with a live on-site consequence.** On **Maurice Casey, *An Aramaic
Approach to Q*** (SNTSMS 122; CUP, 2002) and his earlier ***Aramaic Sources of Mark's Gospel*** (SNTSMS
102; CUP, 1998), Williams brings specialist Semitic philology and finds the **Aramaic reconstructions
unreliable**. Specific, checkable defects to verify against Casey's own pages:
- Mixed Hebrew and Aramaic within single reconstructed sentences (e.g. *yš* + *lh*); inconsistent
  spellings of the same word across pages (*śtn* / *śtn'*; *d* / *dy*); a root given as *sdk* for *sdq*;
  implausible word order.
- On **Beelzeboul**: Casey argues two transliterators could not independently reach Mark's form; Williams
  answers that the phonology makes it likely (ayin unlikely to be represented; *ou* for Hebrew *ū*; *ee*
  close to Biblical Aramaic *be'ēl*) — **the argument is overstated.**
- **The decisive case — Mark 14:72's difficult *epibalōn*.** Casey proposes a *resh*/*daleth* misreading
  (*šr'* "began" misread as *šd'* "throw"). Williams's reply: *šd'* means "throw" only in the **peal**,
  *šr'* means "begin" only in the **pael**, so the 3 m.sg. forms would differ in **two of three letters**
  and be audibly distinct (*šdā* vs. *šarrī*). He notes **four places** where Casey ignores the peal/pael
  distinction, two of them load-bearing (Mark 14:72; Luke 11:41). Cf. Mastin's separate criticism, *JTS*
  51 (2000): 655.
- **⚠ The bounded, fair verdict — reproduce it in full or not at all.** Head & Williams do **not** dismiss
  Casey. They say his contributions are **strongest in his discussion of individual texts and in locating
  sayings within particular first-century Jewish environments**, and that **"many of his positive points
  in this regard can be detached from the model of Q which he proposes and the reconstructions he makes."**
  The weakness is specifically the *continuous Aramaic reconstructions* and the far-reaching
  source-multiplicity conclusions built on them. They also record Casey's own disclaimer that he has not
  recovered the *ipsissima verba* (p. 183).

## Guardrail fit (read before pulling)
- **⚠ This is NOT an apologetic argument, and must never be dressed as one.** "Scholars can't reconstruct
  Q" does **not** mean "so the Gospels are reliable," and it does **not** mean "Q doesn't exist." The
  authors explicitly leave the two-source hypothesis standing and treat Goodacre's alternative as
  unproven too. The honest use is narrow: **a caution about how much confidence a hypothetical
  reconstruction can bear** — a point that cuts against overconfident *skeptical* reconstructions and
  against overconfident *conservative* ones alike. Any use that turns it into a scoreboard win would be
  precisely the manufactured confidence `CLAUDE.md` § *The mission* forbids.
- **⚠ Do not adjudicate the Synoptic Problem in our own voice.** Markan priority, Farrer–Goulder,
  Griesbach, and the two-source hypothesis are live intramural scholarly disputes with no doctrinal
  stake. `library/manuscript.html` and `library/coincidences.html` already handle the two-source
  hypothesis correctly — as a *concession-generating* fact ("a coincidence between Mark and Luke may be
  nothing more mysterious than Luke copying Mark"), not as a position we defend.
- **Tone model:** this is a good one — a genuinely critical review that says exactly what it thinks is
  weak, names what is strong in the same breath, and keeps its conclusion within the evidence.
- **Age caveat:** 2003. Q scholarship has moved (Goodacre's later work; the ongoing Farrer revival). Cite
  the *primaries*, and don't present a 2003 survey as the current state of the field.
- Denominational neutrality: clean. Nothing doctrinal at stake.
