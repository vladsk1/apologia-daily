## Argument map (in our own words)

**Thesis, and its bound.** No single definition of "canon" captures the phenomenon. The dominant "exclusive"
definition (canon = a fixed, final, closed list) is legitimate but cannot be the only one. Two more senses are
needed: canon as **functioning** Scripture, and canon as the books God gave (**ontological**). All three should be
used together.
- ⚠ Bound (p. 8 and the Conclusion): he is **not** trying to abolish the exclusive definition. He grants it is
  **right** that the canon's boundaries did not reach a general consensus until the fourth century. He objects only
  to treating it as the *sole* legitimate usage.

**§1 Introduction (pp. 1–3).**
- Childs (1979) said much of the canon debate is a terminology problem, and that is still true.
- A **closed-list definition has become dominant** "at least in some circles", and some of its advocates say others
  must adopt it to avoid anachronism (Ulrich 2002; Allert 2007).
- n. 3 is a methodological rule: the question is the **concept**, not the word.
  - **Hahneman** argued for a late canon partly because *kanōn* is not applied to the Christian book list until the
    fourth century (Eusebius, Athanasius). Kruger says the appearance of the term is not decisive.
  - He also faults a general "fascination with the etymology" (Metzger 289–93; Gamble 15–18; Ulrich).

**§2 The exclusive definition (pp. 3–8).**
- **Origin.** A. C. Sundberg, "Towards a Revised History of the New Testament Canon," *Studia Evangelica* 4 (1968):
  452–61, and his 1971 *Interpreter's One-Volume Commentary* article. Sundberg sharply separates **Scripture**
  (early, open) from **canon** (a closed list, fourth century or later). Showing that a book was used as Scripture is
  not enough to call it canonical.
- **Roots** (n. 6): Staerk 1929, Hölscher 1905, Semler 1771–75.
- **Supporters:**
  - Kelsey, Barr and Gamble (Gamble with later nuance);
  - **Barton** (who grants several definitions have some validity but prefers the sharp distinction);
  - **Hahneman** (no NT canon until the fourth century);
  - **McDonald** (its leading promoter, "more balanced" in how he holds it);
  - **Ulrich** (insists on it);
  - **Allert**;
  - n. 16 adds Aichele, Barrera, **Dungan (*Constantine's Bible*, 2006)** and Poirier.
  - Nienhuis (2007) is cited for the view's "widespread acceptance."
- **Merits Kruger grants:**
  - it captures the canon's fluid edges before the fourth century;
  - it keeps the church's role in recognition in view.
- **Three criticisms:**
  1. **The sharp Scripture/canon split is anachronistic.** If second-century Christians could say which books were
     Scripture, they could also say which were not. Some limitation is already implied (Provan 1997).
  2. **"Closed" is undefined.**
     - If it means uniform practice, there is no canon even today. n. 19: the Syrian Orthodox lectionary still uses
       the 22-book Peshitta New Testament, and the Ethiopian church has a broader New Testament (Metzger 218–28).
     - If it means a formal church-wide act, he says there is none before **Trent**. He cites Gamble: Laodicea (360)
       was local. He cites McDonald: the church as a whole never formally concluded it.
     - Chapman: the definition matches no historical reality, so it is itself anachronistic.
  3. **The most basic criticism: the fourth century changed nothing about the core.**
     - The four Gospels and most of Paul had been received for centuries. Barton, *The Spirit and the Letter*
       18–19, is cited for this.
     - A change of terminology implies these books had a lesser status before, which is misleading. It implies the
       canon was a "great and meritorious act of the church" (Webster). Dempster: it suggests the canon had no
       history and was created *ex nihilo* by a council.
     - Worked example: **Allert**, whose stated aim is to emphasise the church's centrality and who says the Bible
       "was not always there" in early Christianity.

**§3 The functional definition (pp. 9–14).**
- **Childs** (*The New Testament as Canon*, 1984, 25): "canon" can cover the whole process. A collection that
  functions as a religious norm is a canon, open or closed. Scripture and canon are closely related and often the
  same thing.
- **Pedigree.** Barton (*Spirit and the Letter* 4–8) shows that **Harnack** used this definition: a book is
  canonical once cited with Scripture-formulas, which dates the canon's origins to the **mid-second century**.
  **Zahn** counted widespread use, which gives an even earlier date. Kruger: both agree that function, not a closed
  list, decides.
- ⭐ **n. 35: Stuhlhofer's statistics.** Franz Stuhlhofer, *Der Gebrauch der Bibel von Jesus bis Euseb* (1988),
  found that the core canonical books were cited **far more often, in proportion to their length, than
  non-canonical books — and even than the Old Testament.** Barton is cited as giving this weight.
- **Modern supporters:**
  - Sanders ("canon as function antedates canon as shape");
  - Sheppard ("Canon 1" = rule or norm; "Canon 2" = fixed list; both legitimate);
  - Chapman, Provan, D. Meade, Dempster.
- **Merits:**
  - it captures that early Christians *had* an authoritative corpus before the fourth century;
  - it does not inflate the role of official declarations.
- **Weakness 1 (McDonald, *Forgotten Scriptures* 23–25).** Some books functioned as Scripture for some communities
  but never made the final list (the *Shepherd of Hermas*, the *Apocalypse of Peter*). Does "decanonisation" make
  sense?
  - Kruger's reply: communities had different functional canons, with core agreement and peripheral disagreement.
  - And the exclusive definition is imprecise too, because the boundaries were never absolute.
- **Weakness 2, shared with the exclusive definition: no ontology.** Both make canon depend on what the church does.
  This feeds the **"naturalisation" of canon** (Webster).
  - **Harnack** makes the canon a creative act of the church responding to **Marcion**. He is followed by
    **Campenhausen** (1968/1972). Other assessments: Hoffmann 1984, **Barton, *Spirit and the Letter* 35–62**,
    Grant 1965.
  - Others treat canon as a socio-cultural or identity marker (J. Z. Smith; Ricoeur; Kelsey's "self-description").
  - Others treat it as a **political or ideological instrument of power** (Aichele; Coote & Coote; Bowman & Woolf;
    Haines-Eitzen; and **Brakke, "Canon Formation and Social Conflict in Fourth-Century Egypt," *HTR* 87 (1994):
    395–419**, on Athanasius's 39th Festal Letter).
  - Kruger: these community-dependent views are not the historic Christian position, which holds that the books are
    God's books before anyone recognises them.

**§4 The ontological definition (pp. 14–19).**
- Canon is the authoritative books God gave his church. Books do not *become* canonical; they *are*. So there is a
  canon in the first century, recognised only in retrospect.
- **Warfield** is cited: the canon was complete when the last apostolic book was given, which Warfield puts at about
  AD 98.
- n. 53: lost apostolic letters. Following **C. Stephen Evans**, God would ensure his revelation is recognised, so
  the church's recognition is itself a strong reason to think these are the intended books.
- He defends a theological definition against a purely historical-critical approach, citing Provan, Watson, Filson
  and Vanhoozer ("history alone cannot answer what the canon finally is").
- **Integration:**
  - The three definitions map onto three stages: books written (ontological, first century); books used as
    Scripture (functional, mid-second century); consensus reached (exclusive, fourth century).
  - So the "date" of canon depends on the definition (Barton). Ask about the canon's **stage**, not its **date**,
    as with a seedling, a sapling and a grown tree.
  - The three senses imply one another.
  - They map onto speech-act theory: locution / illocution / perlocution (Austin, Searle, Wolterstorff, Vanhoozer,
    Alston, Horton).

**§5 Conclusion (pp. 19–20).**
- Each definition, used alone, distorts:
  - exclusive alone suggests little agreement before the fourth century;
  - functional alone says nothing about what the books are;
  - ontological alone suggests a canon "lowered from heaven" with no history.
- Use all three.

**Primaries the article points to (all unverified until checked).**
- *Ancient or ecclesial:* the Council of Laodicea (c. 360) as local; the **Peshitta 22-book NT** and the
  **Ethiopian broader NT** (via Metzger 218–28); Trent; Athanasius's 39th Festal Letter (via Brakke).
- *Modern:* Sundberg 1968 and 1971; Childs 1979 and 1984; Barton 1997 (*The Spirit and the Letter* = US *Holy
  Writings, Sacred Text*) chs. 1–2; McDonald 1995, 2007, 2009; Ulrich 2002; Allert 2007; Hahneman 1992 and 2002;
  Gamble 1985, 1989, 2002; Harnack 1925 and *History of Dogma* II; Campenhausen 1972; **Stuhlhofer 1988**; Sheppard
  1987; Chapman 2000 and 2003; Dempster *JETS* 52 (2009); Provan *SJT* 50 (1997); Webster 2003; Brakke 1994; Dungan
  2006; Warfield 1948; Evans 2006.
- ⚠ n. 20 cites McDonald, "The Integrity of the Biblical Canon," 131–32, with no publication details. Locate it
