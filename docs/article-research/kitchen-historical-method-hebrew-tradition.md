## Thesis (our words)
A **methodological** complaint, not primarily an archaeological one. Kitchen observes that Eissfeldt
handles the later material (Saul, David, Solomon, Canaan–Israel) with "regular historical method," using
external comparative data — but handles the **patriarchs and the Exodus** by a different standard, setting
the external evidence aside as "too ambiguous" and reconstructing from internal literary theory alone.
Kitchen's charge: that is not history but "an able summary of a maze of speculation." His two governing
principles are the portable part:
1. **Where the Old Testament is our only source, replacing it with a modern reconstruction does not
   produce better evidence — it produces none.** A hypothesis is not evidence; "no matter how brilliant…
   guesswork remains qualitatively guesswork."
2. **Negative evidence proves nothing.** Non-mention in external sources is the normal condition of almost
   everyone in antiquity, and reflects the "vast, yawning gaps" in what survives. Kitchen's own 1966
   example is devastatingly well chosen: **there was then no external mention of David or Solomon either**,
   and no one concluded from that that they were fictitious.
   ⭐ **And history vindicated the principle in a way Kitchen could not have known**: the **Tel Dan Stele**
   (1993) supplied exactly the "House of David" attestation whose absence he was arguing proved nothing.
   **That is a genuinely strong, honest, self-limiting use of this article** — and `library/archaeology.html`
   already carries the Tel Dan Stele.

## Argument map → primaries to VERIFY (all pre-1974 — re-check every one)
**I(a) · Patriarchal names.** The argument is **onomastic type**, not identification: the patriarchal
names fall consistently into the class of **human personal names** attested among West Semites in the
first half of the second millennium BC — not names of deities or tribal groups. → *Primaries:*
relation-names (Ab-ra(ha)m, Ben-jamin) in Akkadian and Ugaritic onomastica (Tallqvist, *Assyrian Personal
Names* [1918]; Gelb–Purves–MacRae, *Nuzi Personal Names* [1943]; Huffmon, *Amorite Personal Names in the
Mari Texts* [Johns Hopkins, 1965]); "Amorite imperfective" names — **Yaqub-el, Yaʿqob-har, Yisra-il**
(the last from Ugarit, 13th c.), Yasmaʿ-el; animal-names (Leah, Rachel) as ordinary human names. Kitchen
also **corrects two popular over-claims**: *Asher* has nothing to do with the goddess Ashirat, and *Gad*
as a deity is not attested in the early second millennium.
⚠ This is the strongest-wearing part of the section: it argues the names are the *kind* borne by people,
which is a bounded claim and does not by itself date anything.
**I(b) · ⚠⚠ Legal and social usages — the CONTESTED core.** Adoption of a servant as heir (**Gen
15:1–5**); a childless wife giving her handmaid (**Gen 16:1ff.; 30:1–13**); the chief wife's son
supplanting the handmaid's (**Gen 17:15ff.; 21:10–12**); sale of a birthright (**Gen 25:30–34**); Jacob
and Laban (**Gen 29–31**); the legal force of oral blessings (**Gen 27; 48–49**) — all compared to **Nuzi**
tablets and the **Lipit-Ishtar laws** §§25–28. Plus **Gen 23** (Machpelah) read against **Hittite Laws
§§46–47** (M. R. Lehmann, *BASOR* 129 [1953]: 15–18), and **Gen 37:28**'s twenty-shekel price for Joseph
against the early-second-millennium average slave price.
⚠⚠ **DO NOT USE WITHOUT THE POST-1974 LITERATURE.** This is exactly the material **Thomas L. Thompson,
*The Historicity of the Patriarchal Narratives* (de Gruyter, 1974)** and **John Van Seters, *Abraham in
History and Tradition* (Yale, 1975)** overturned, arguing the parallels are either misread or so
long-lived that they date nothing. Their critique is widely accepted, including by scholars with no
minimalist agenda. Kitchen's counter-arguments belong in *On the Reliability of the Old Testament* (2003),
which we have **not read** (see the book note). **Any on-site use must present this as a live dispute, name
Thompson and Van Seters, and not lean on it.**
**I(c) · Religion, geopolitics, travel.** The "God of the father(s)" pattern (Albright, *BASOR* 163
[1961]: 48–49; F. M. Cross, *HTR* 55 [1962]: 225–59); the prominence of **El** as patriarchal God, when El
was already an "otiose" has-been in the later Ugaritic material (Pope, *El in the Ugaritic Texts* [Brill,
1955]); the Mesopotamian royal coalitions of **Gen 14** fitting the Old Babylonian period; Palestinian
city-states-plus-hinterland-tribes matching the Egyptian **Execration Texts** and the **Story of Sinuhe**;
Semites visiting Egypt — Sinuhe B.26/R.50 and the **Beni Hasan** tomb-painting of Abishar's party of 37
Asiatics; the escort out of Egypt (**Gen 12:20**) beside Sinuhe B.245, 248; long-range travel evidenced by
the **Mari** archives and the **Cappadocian (Kültepe) tablets**.
**II · Nature vs. historicity — a distinction worth borrowing.** Kitchen separates two questions that get
conflated: *what kind of thing* the patriarchs are in the texts (individuals? personified tribes? faded
Canaanite deities?) and *whether they existed*. He argues the first is effectively closed — the evidence
indicates **individuals** — and says plainly that **"whether they were real or imaginary individuals is a
separate question."** ⭐ **That is a model of a bounded verdict and the single most transferable sentence
in the article.**
**V(A) · The Documentary Hypothesis stated as fact.** Kitchen's objection is procedural: Eissfeldt asserts
J/E/D/P (plus his own "L") apodictically, when **no manuscript or direct attestation of any of these
documents has ever been found** — and notes the irony that he says so in the same breath as denying the
patriarchs for lack of external attestation. Kitchen asks only that a personal reconstruction be labelled
as one. ⚠ **Do not turn this into an attack on source criticism as such** — the point is about *epistemic
labelling*, and pentateuchal composition is a live scholarly field, not an apologetic battleground for us.
**IV · The Merneptah ("Israel") Stele.** Kitchen rejects the Noth/Eissfeldt proposal of an *earlier,
different* "Israel" in Canaan, arguing there is no reason not to take the stele's Israel as the tribal
group of Joshua and the Judges. → *Primary:* the **Merneptah Stele** (Cairo JE 31408), c. 1208 BC.
⚠ Note the boundary: the stele's *reading* of "Israel" is essentially uncontested, but **what kind of
entity** it names (a people? how large? where?) is genuinely debated. Land the bounded claim only.
