**Source.** Michael R. Licona & Jan G. van der Watt, two companion articles in *HTS Teologiese
Studies / Theological Studies* 65, no. 1 (2009), University of Pretoria:
1. **"Historians and miracles: The principle of analogy and antecedent probability reconsidered,"**

## What the two papers argue (our words — do not reproduce their prose)

**The shared thesis.** Most biblical scholars and historians hold that investigating a *miracle*
report lies outside a historian's professional rights — a historian may grant that Jesus was crucified
and that people believed they saw him risen, but "may not go there" on whether he actually rose.
Licona & van der Watt challenge that paradigm: historians **are** within their rights to adjudicate
miracle claims, *when* the event (a) is highly unlikely given natural law/circumstances **and** (b)
occurs in a context "charged with religious significance" (one where, if a god exists, we might expect
it to act), **and** the event is the **best explanation of the relevant historical bedrock**. The
resurrection is the running worked example; the "historical bedrock" they assume is the familiar trio —
Jesus' crucifixion, the disciples' belief that the risen Jesus appeared to them, and Paul's
conversion from persecutor (the same minimal-facts set our `minimalfacts.html` already uses).

**Article #129 answers two objections:**
- **The principle of analogy** (Troeltsch; applied to the resurrection by Dunn and Craffert): the past
  does not differ in kind from the present, so if the dead do not rise now, they did not then. Replies
  gathered: (i) the principle, pressed hard, would also forbid **dinosaurs** (never experienced today —
  yet established from remains; likewise a miracle can be established from credible testimony that
  remains); (ii) it quietly **presupposes metaphysical naturalism** (that miracles don't happen now),
  which is the very point in dispute — circular (Beckwith; and Pannenberg's line that this is "not a
  historical judgment but an ideological belief"); (iii) it is limited by the individual historian's
  experience — the "net cast into a small lake" vs a worldwide cast (Habermas & Licona), plus **Lewis's
  circularity point** (we can call experience "uniform" against miracles only if we already know every
  report is false); (iv) the **fairy-tale worry** (if you drop analogy, why not accept Aesop?) is
  answered by genre and the availability of a natural hypothesis — Meier's own verdict that the one
  miracle of **Vespasian** looks like a staged "photo opportunity"; (v) fear of being thought
  superstitious is not an argument (Allison; Eddy & Boyd; Wright's "scarecrows" line); (vi) cumulative
  evidence outweighs mere analogy (Pannenberg).
- **Antecedent probability** (Ehrman; echoing Hume): a miracle hypothesis is *by definition* the least
  probable, and historians must choose the most probable, so a miracle can never be the verdict. Reply:
  a miracle is improbable *assessed by natural causes* — but the early Christians never claimed Jesus
  rose *by natural causes*; they claimed **God** raised him. Whether that is the least or the **most**
  probable explanation depends on background knowledge (does God exist? would he act here?) that
  historians are not privy to — so Ehrman would have to establish God's non-existence or non-intent to
  fix the prior near zero. A **Bayesian** calculation is inscrutable here (the inputs are unavailable;
  a free personal agent's choice can't be assigned a probability), so probability should be set by
  best-explanation criteria, not by a definition imported before the data. "Bad philosophy corrupts
  good history."

**Article #130 answers three more objections:**
- **The theological objection** (Meier's, the sharpest in the literature): even after an exhaustive
  investigation a historian may say only that *no natural cause is known*, that the context was
  religiously charged, and that *witnesses believed* a miracle occurred — but to add "**God** directly
  caused this" is to cross the line from historian to philosopher/theologian. Replies: (i) historians
  don't need **direct access** to the entities in their explanations any more than physicists do to
  quarks, gluons or black holes — all are inferences from evidence (Polkinghorne: he believes in
  quarks, the Big Bang, evolution *and* the resurrection on the same "best fit with the phenomena"
  ground); (ii) crucially, the objection disputes only the **cause**, not the **event** — historians
  routinely fix a *what* while leaving the *how/why* open (they are sure **Carloman died in AD 771**
  without settling whether Charlemagne had him killed), so a historian can in principle conclude "Jesus
  rose" (nuanced: "and appeared in what others interpreted as his transformed body") while leaving the
  theological *cause* to theology; (iii) Ehrman's version ("the whole exercise is illegitimate because
  it implies God did it") is "doing history backwards" — approach the data neither presupposing nor
  *a priori* excluding God's action, then weigh hypotheses. A second strand of the objection — *worldview
  governs the verdict, so punt to philosophers* — is answered: historians' race/nationality/values
  shape all history (Marxist vs non-Marxist accounts of the American Revolution), crossing disciplines
  is normal and necessary, and every historian already makes philosophical assumptions (an external
  world, reliable senses, the uniformity of nature, that the past is partly knowable — McCullagh), so
  philosophy is not "off-limits" to historians (the imagined Allison/Habermas dialogue).
- **Lack of consensus** (Segal; Ehrman: a historical theory should command assent "regardless of
  religious perspective"). Reply: requiring consensus is unreasonable because **historians' horizons
  prevent consensus even in non-religious history** — conceded by non-theologian philosophers of
  history (Evans, Fischhoff, McCullagh, Gilderhus, Lorenz; the 1994 *Journal of American History*
  survey where "ideological commitments" topped historians' self-reported allegiances at 41%). And
  Ehrman is **inconsistent**: Muslim historians deny Jesus' crucifixion (Q 4:157–158) and a few deny he
  existed, yet that lack of consensus doesn't stop Ehrman affirming the crucifixion — the sober-minded
  historian isn't thwarted by outliers. Consensus ≠ truth (communities have held disproven beliefs);
  horizons bias audiences too (debate "who won?" perceptions). So a historian may state a hypothesis is
  *probably* true without a field-wide stamp of approval (contra O'Collins).
- **Miracle claims in multiple religions** (Hume: rival miracles "cancel out"; Ehrman: admit Jesus'
  miracles and you must admit Muhammad's, Apollonius's, Honi's, Hanina's, Vespasian's). Reply: rival
  claims don't cancel (naturalistic vs theistic origin-of-life hypotheses can't both be true, yet
  neither is thereby struck from consideration); the evidence must be admitted but **is not equal in
  quality**. Comparative attestation (their numbers): the evangelists wrote within ~35–70 years (Mark
  ~35–45); miracles of **Muhammad** are *absent from the Qur'an* and appear only much later;
  **Apollonius** — Philostratus c. AD 225, ~125 years after, with few corroborating sources; **Honi the
  Circle-Drawer** — Josephus ~150 years after, and the Talmud three centuries later (relocating him 500
  years); **Hanina ben Dosa** — first report ~150 years after; **Vespasian** — Tacitus and Suetonius
  ~35 years after (two of three sources as close as Mark), Dio Cassius ~110 years. So a historian who
  judges Jesus' miracles historical need not grant the poorly-attested ones: **case by case**
  (Crossley), and a well-evidenced claim isn't ruled out by the existence of badly-evidenced ones.
  Ehrman's "Christians reject others' miracles out of bias" commits the **genetic fallacy** — a bad
  *reason* for a belief doesn't make the belief false, and a historian can reach "Jesus was a
  miracle-worker" by good method whatever a believer's private route.

**Conclusion of the pair:** all five objections fail; a "paradigm shift" is warranted and the
"epistemological Ice Age of anti-supernaturalism" in the historians' guild is thawing (they cite the
2006 *History and Theory* theme issue "Religion and History" — Shaw, Gregory's "secular confessional
history," Butler, Cladis — and Witherington).

## ⚠ Standing caution flags (carry into any pull)

1. **Preserve the event/cause line — it is the papers' most careful move, and it is OURS already.**
   The honest claim is that a historian may adjudicate the **event** ("the best explanation of the
   bedrock is that Jesus rose") while the theological **cause** ("God raised him") is an inference the
   reader's worldview governs. `minimalfacts.html` already draws exactly this limit ("What it does not
   do is force the conclusion on someone whose worldview assigns no room to the act of God"). Any pull
   must keep it; never let a mined line slide into "history proves God raised Jesus."
2. **Do NOT import Licona's *definition* of miracle.** His working definition — "an event for which
   natural explanations are inadequate… there could be **no** natural cause of it" — is *stronger* than
   our essay's. `miracles.html` deliberately uses **Flew's** framing (an event that would not have
   happened had nature been left to its own resources) and the **descriptive-not-prescriptive** account
   of law, precisely to avoid the "violation / god-of-the-gaps" framing. Mine the *objection-replies*,
   not the definition.
3. **Keep the possibility/actuality wall intact.** `miracles.html` is scoped to the *a priori
   philosophical bar* (Spinoza = impossible; Hume = never credible) and explicitly hands the *historical*
   question to `minimalfacts.html`/`emptytomb.html`. These papers live on a **third** question —
   *may a historian qua historian even render the verdict?* (the methodological-naturalism bar). If any
   of this is ever built, it belongs **beside** that wall, labelled as the historiographical bar, not
   blended into the Hume/Spinoza argument (which would muddy the essay's clean modesty).
4. **The multiple-religions section is Islam-tier the moment it is built.** It names Muhammad (miracles
   absent from the Qur'an) and leans on Q 4:157–158 (the crucifixion denial). Any live surface carrying
   it is **dual-consensus + EXPLICIT-VERDICT + FALSE-COMMON-GROUND** content; port with the
   land-the-verdict discipline the Islam pages hold, and cross-check `library/islam-*` first.
5. **Antecedent-probability reply is conditional — don't let it overclaim.** "If God exists and wanted
   to raise Jesus, the resurrection is the *most* probable explanation" is true but conditional on
   exactly the background knowledge historians lack; port it as the bounded "the prior can't be fixed
   near zero without first settling the God question," which is how `miracles.html` and
   `minimalfacts.html` already put it — never as a bare "the resurrection is probable."
6. **These are 2009; our Hume treatment is more current.** `miracles.html` already carries Earman,
   Fogelin and the **SEP (McGrew & Larmer, rev. 2024)** survey. On the philosophical bar our essay is
   newer and better-scoped than the papers' 2009 antecedent-probability section — do not "improve"
   `miracles.html` by importing the older framing (verdict 5 below).
7. **Josephus Testimonium / *paradoxōn*.** Article #129 leans on Josephus's "worker of amazing deeds"
   (*paradoxōn ergōn poiētēs*). Our site already handles the *Testimonium* carefully; any use must
   respect that existing treatment and not present the TF as undisputed.
