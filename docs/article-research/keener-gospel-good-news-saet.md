## Argument map (in our own words)

**Thesis.** The NT "gospel" is above all the announcement of Jesus' saving death and resurrection, "according to the
Scriptures", as the climax of Israel's restoration story. The four first-century Gospels are a subtype of ancient
biography, written within living memory, and their authors' names were known early.

**§1 Background.**
- **1.1 Greek and Roman uses.** *Euangelion* meant any glad tidings, usually plural (victories, weddings, the return of
  someone lost). Christians used the singular because they meant one supreme piece of news (Dickson, *NTS* 51, 2005).
  It was also used of imperial accessions and birthdays, even by Jewish writers (Philo *Gaius* 231; Josephus *War*
  4.618, 656). A Roman would hear "good news of God's kingdom" as a contrast with the emperor's. But this gospel's
  Messiah is crushed by Rome, not crushing it.
- **1.2 OT and Jewish context.** The Christian usage is rooted in Isaiah: 52:7 ("your God reigns"), 40:9, 61:1. These
  announce restoration as a new exodus and God's own return to Zion. Isaiah 53 follows 52:7, and Paul cites 52:7 and
  53:1 in sequence (Rom 10:15–16). All four Gospels apply Isaiah 40:3 / Malachi 3:1 (the forerunner who prepares the
  way of the LORD) to John the Baptist preparing for Jesus. John then announces Jesus as the one who baptizes with the
  Spirit, which Keener calls a role the prophets give to God (Isa 44:3; Ezek 36:25–27; Joel 2:28–29).

**§2 NT usage.**
- **2.1** The most explicit NT definition is 1 Cor 15:1–7. Its content is Jesus' death and resurrection, with an OT
  backstory, attested by named witnesses. Paul insists it is **shared with the Jerusalem apostles** (15:11: "whether
  it was I or they"). Death and resurrection recur across NT preaching (the Acts speeches).
- **2.2** The appearances list makes the gospel an event in history attested by witnesses, not a myth (Wright 2003;
  Licona 2010). "According to the Scriptures" evokes atonement, rejected deliverers, and Isaiah 53. Daniel 7 is read
  as the Son of Man embodying God's kingdom. ⚠ **Hazard:** one sentence here repeats a claim this site has retired
  (see ⑤ below).
- **2.3 "Jesus is Lord."** Romans 1:1–4 (Davidic descent; "declared Son of God with power… by resurrection"); Mark 1:1.
- **2.4–2.6** False gospels (2 Cor 11; Galatians 1), Jew–gentile unity, ethics. Pastoral and theological, not
  apologetic.

**§3 The gospel in the Gospels.**
- **3.1** Healings and exorcisms as signs that the kingdom has arrived (Matt 11:5 / Luke 7:22, echoing Isa 35:5–6 and
  61:1; Luke 11:20 / Matt 12:28, "the finger of God").
- **3.2 Imminence versus immediacy.** Jesus says the kingdom "has come near" (Mark 1:15). Keener's answer to the
  "he expected the end at once" reading: the prophets had said "the day of the LORD is near" for eight centuries (Isa
  13:6; Ezek 30:3; Joel 1:15; Zeph 1:14), so imminence never meant immediacy. Mark 9:1 ("some standing here will not
  taste death…") is followed at once by the Transfiguration (9:2), a private foretaste. The temple's fall "within a
  generation" (13:2, 14, 30) is foreshadowed by the torn veil (15:38). He reports both readings of 13:24–27 (temple's
  fall vs. Jesus' return) without choosing. The kingdom is inaugurated at the first coming and consummated at the
  second.
- **3.3** The "messianic secret": proclaiming oneself king was treason to Rome, so Jesus spoke of God's kingdom without
  naming its king until the cross; afterwards "Jesus is Lord" says the same thing more plainly.
- **3.4** Where the "gospel" begins: Mark 1:1; John the Baptist; Mark knows the appearances follow (14:28; 16:7).
- **3.5 Titles and authorship.** Individual Gospels are cited by name more often after c. 180. Titles appear in early
  papyri (he names p66 "perhaps c. 200" and "p72"). But the authors were not unknown before that: ancient works often
  did not name their author in the text, while the audience knew it orally or from the scroll's outside (Gathercole
  2018). Papias names Mark. Justin's "memoirs" of Peter (*Dial.* 106.3) "probably independently" confirm Papias.
  Matthew and Luke follow Mark so closely they likely knew whose it was (Gathercole 2018: 462, 471). Once several
  Gospels circulated, churches needed to tell them apart. Untitled works normally gather **different** titles in
  different places (Collins 2007: 2–3), so the titles' unanimity suggests early knowledge (Hengel; Gathercole; Bond).
  The KATA + name formula is unusual and would not arise in several cases by accident. *Euangelion* is used of
  written gospel material as early as the Didache (Kelhoffer 2004). ⚠ He grants "legitimate reasons" why many doubt
  the names reflect authorship rather than association.

**§4 Gospels as literary-historical works.**
- **Intro.** The four share a common story and characters (Adams 2011), unlike later "gospels" (Gathercole 2022). No
  harmony until Tatian, late in the second century. Mark is a biography by focus (a book about one person); Matthew
  and Luke move closer to the typical form with infancy narratives and genealogies.
- **4.1 Consensus.** Justin calls the Gospels "memoirs" (mid-second century). The form critics treated the Gospels as
  folk literature and a new genre; Bultmann denied they were biographies. But "folk" and "high" are not genres, and
  the biographic features form critics thought missing in Mark are equally missing from Lucian's *Demonax*, a
  biography of a sage (Burridge 2004: 93). Talbert (1977), Berger (1984) and Shuler (1982) revived the genre
  question. **Burridge's 1992 Cambridge monograph shifted the consensus.** Frickenschmidt (1997) confirmed it from a
  very large survey of ancient biographies.
- **4.2 Character of ancient biography.** Genre categories are fluid. *Bios* evolved over a thousand years: it began
  partly as funerary encomium (Isocrates, Xenophon), included works written to vilify, and by late antiquity was
  largely hagiography. But **from Nepos (late Republic) to Diogenes Laertius (early third century), full-length
  biographers tried to report the information available to them**. By the first and second centuries AD full-length
  biography was essentially a historical monograph focused on one person. Selection and interpretation, which some
  postmodern critics call "fictionalization", is distinguished even by them from **fiction**, which needs no prior
  information (De Temmerman 2016). Biographers also drew moral lessons. So the Evangelists' theological shaping is
  what the genre leads one to expect.
- **4.3 How much would be remembered?** Oral historians find the most accurate period to be **living memory**,
  "usually some sixty to eighty years". All four Gospels fall within it; most scholars put Mark within about forty
  years of the crucifixion. Jesus' disciples and his brother were leaders through that period (Gal 2:9; 1 Cor 9:5;
  15:5–7). Disciples were by definition students, normally expected to pass on a teacher's message, whatever their
  literacy (Keener 2019: 401–448). **Apart from Socrates and Jesus, few ancient sages have several surviving works
  about them from their own movement, written within living memory.** Only a minority of surviving biographies were
  written within living memory of their subjects; those that were are heavily information-based (Keener 2019:
  261–302). The best source for Alexander, Arrian, wrote about 450 years after his death.

**§5 Later usage.** Later "gospels" begin more than a century after Jesus. The church preferred first-century
accounts tied to the eyewitness circle (Luke 1:2; John 19:35; Acts 1:21–22). The apocryphal gospels are novelistic
and lack the Judean and Galilean traits of the four. Most Gnostic "gospels" are not narratives at all.

**§6 Later movements.** *Evangelisch*/evangelical; conversion as transition; a critique of "decision" preaching.
**§7 Conclusion.**
