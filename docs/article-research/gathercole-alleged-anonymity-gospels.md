## Argument map (in our own words)

**Thesis, and its bound.** The two standard reasons for calling the Gospels "anonymous" do not support the
conclusion. The most likely conclusion is that the attributions to Matthew, Mark, Luke and John **go back to the
first century and are probably original**. ⚠ Gathercole is explicit (pp. 1–2, 21): he is **not** arguing that
these four men wrote the Gospels, nor for a fourfold canon, nor for the antiquity of the full "Gospel according
to X" titles. The claim is only about **the names attached**.

**§1 — the scholarship (pp. 2–6).** The specialist studies (Aland 1961/1980; Balz 1969; **Wolter 1988**, the
first treatment of anonymity on its own; **Baum, *NovT* 50 (2008)**) and the textbooks treat anonymity as a near
consensus. Freed: "most NT scholars agree" (p. 6). The dissenters are Kirsopp & Silva Lake (1948), Dibelius and
Moles on Luke's dedication, Bauckham on John ("never anonymous") and, most extensively, **Martin Hengel** (titles
original). The majority rests on two "facts": (1) the Gospels do not name their authors in the text; (2) the
present titles were probably added in the second century. Gathercole **grants both facts** and disputes **their
significance** (p. 6). ⚠ Note that he concedes Hengel "has not really persuaded many" on (2).

**§2 — no name in the text proves nothing (pp. 6–10).** He surveys the three genre candidates.
- *Technical treatise* (Alexander's model for Luke): omitting the name is standard.
- *History*: Herodotus and Thucydides name themselves, but **Polybius, Diodorus, Josephus's *Antiquities*,
  Arrian**, and among Latin historians **Sallust, Livy, Tacitus, Florus** do not. Herkommer calls omission the
  Roman norm. Baum's claim that Greek historians named themselves is an over-generalisation.
- *Biography* (the most popular genre view): **Philo's lives, Josephus's *Life*, Plutarch's *Parallel Lives*,
  Lucian (mostly), Porphyry, Philostratus, Eunapius, Nepos and Tacitus's *Agricola*** all lack a prefatory
  self-naming. Only Lucian's *Peregrinus* (an epistolary prescript) and a spurious *Historia Augusta* life have one.
- Authors were identified by **paratext**: an initial title, an end title or colophon, a label on the roll
  (*sittubos*), the back of the roll, and later title pages, contents lists and running heads.
- Conclusion (p. 10): calling a work anonymous because the name is not in its body is **a category mistake**.

**§3.1 — practical necessity (pp. 11–12).** Hengel's settings of book trade, library and worship are weak for the
first decades. The stronger point is the "says who?" question once a Gospel left its first community. ⭐ **New in
this article:** Matthew and Luke follow Mark closely in order and content. A "professedly conscientious
investigator like Luke" would hardly follow "a naked anonymous account" without some testimony about whose it was.
Copies were also carried province to province by trusted people who would know the source. ⚠ This is
probabilistic, and he labels it "common-sense speculation."

**§3.2 — the early named testimony (pp. 12–18), working backwards.** The list runs: **P66** ("Gospel according to
John", late ii–early iii) and the **P4 flyleaf** (Matthew); **Clement of Alexandria** (c. 195–203); the Nag
Hammadi ***Acts of Peter and the Twelve*** (John as the one "beside Jesus" = the Beloved Disciple; a new
testimonium); **Polycrates of Ephesus** (190s, *EH* 3.31.3; 5.24.3); the **Muratorian Fragment**; **Irenaeus**
(*AH* 3.1.1; 3.11.8, linking each name to its Gospel's opening); **Theophilus of Antioch** (*Autolyc.* 2.22, c.
180, John 1:1 by name); **Hegesippus** via an epitome (John "apostle and evangelist," c. 175–180); **Claudius
Apollinaris** (c. 175, Matthew named on the Passover date); the ***Acts of John*** (150–200); **Heracleon**
(150–175); **Ptolemy**, the Valentinian, quoted verbatim in Irenaeus *AH* 1.8.5 ("John, the disciple of the
Lord," 150–175); ***Gospel of Thomas* 13** (Matthew as a rival authority, c. 140–180; a second new testimonium);
**Papias** (*EH* 3.39.15–16 on Mark and Matthew; probable knowledge of John); and behind Papias, **John the
Elder**.
- ⭐ **Papias's date.** Following **Yarbrough, *JETS* 26 (1983)**, he places Papias's activity **around AD 100 or
  very early in the second century**, with his inquiries perhaps a decade or two earlier (p. 18). The Mark report
  is the Elder's, which **pushes the Markan attribution "well into the first century,"** no more than about 20
  years after Mark (pp. 18, 21).
- ⭐ **"It is potentially misleading to present Irenaeus as a novum"** (p. 12). He is one of many second-century
  writers who name the evangelists.

**§3.3 — two silences (pp. 19–20).** Both are arguments from silence, and Gathercole labels them so.
1. **No second-century writer treats the Gospels as anonymous.** Talk of "the Gospel" (singular) sits alongside
   named evangelists (Justin, Irenaeus, Polycrates, Theophilus). Nobody says of the Gospels what Origen said of
   Hebrews: "who wrote it, only God knows."
2. ⭐ **No rival attributions.** Hebrews, which genuinely is anonymous, had **four** proposed authors by the early
   third century: Paul (Pantaenus, and P46's placement), Barnabas (Tertullian), and Clement of Rome or Luke (per
   Origen, *EH* 6.25.14). The Gospels show no such diversity. "The later one views the attributions to be, the
   harder these silences are to explain."

**Conclusion (p. 21).** Mark's attribution goes back to the Elder (first century). Matthew's is early and
scattered (Papias, *Thomas*). Luke is not named before Irenaeus or the Muratorian Fragment, but is the least
likely to have been anonymous, given the first-person preface and dedication (Luke 1:1–4; Acts 1:1) and the "we"
passages. John is the best attested, across the theological spectrum.

### ⑤ Hazards — ours is better; do not "improve" us backwards
- **P66's date.** Gathercole uses P66's title as a late-ii/early-iii testimonium. `library/manuscript.html` already

  the Hegesippus *epitome*; the *Acts of Peter and the Twelve* allusion. None should carry weight on our pages.
