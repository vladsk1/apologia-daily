## What the paper actually argues (our words — do not reproduce its prose)
Licona proposes a **working definition** of "historically reliable" for *ancient* texts and tests it on
two *bioi*: Suetonius's *Life of the Divine Augustus* (chosen as the finest of Suetonius's *Lives*, and
because Suetonius "wrote more closely to how modern biographers write" — Wallace-Hadrill, Mellor) and the
**Gospel of Mark** (chosen as very likely the earliest Gospel and Matthew/Luke's primary source).

- **The definition.** A text is "historically reliable" in the *broad* sense when it provides "an accurate
  gist, or an essentially faithful representation of what occurred" — not accuracy "with the precision of
  a legal transcript." He borrows Christopher Pelling's phrase "**true enough**" (*Plutarch and History*,
  2002, p. 160). The point is calibrated by **genre**: ancient *bioi* used accepted compositional liberties
  (compression, transferal, displacement, paraphrase) and modern narrative biography still does (his
  worked openers: the *Apollo 13* film's invented "Failure is not an option" tagline; Nabeel Qureshi's own
  preface to *Seeking Allah, Finding Jesus* disclosing that its quotations are "rough approximations" and
  some scenes displaced/conflated).
- **Four provisional criteria**, all of which must be met for broad reliability, applied to each text:
  1. **The author chose sources judiciously.** *Suetonius*: for the *Augustus* he named more sources than
     his peers (as many as twelve quotations from Augustus's letters, Augustus's will, the *Res Gestae*,
     senate proceedings) — but also used "questionable anecdotes," so the *Augustus* rests on "a combination
     of pristine historical sources and tabloid rumors" (Wardle); Gascou's ~900-page study concludes
     Suetonius consulted many sources but "indiscriminately." Verdict: *yes and no.* *Mark*: anonymous and
     names no sources, but (a) Gathercole 2018 shows anonymity-in-title/preface is normal for ancient
     *bioi* (Sallust, Livy, Plutarch, Caesar's *Commentaries* all omit the author's name), so it is "entirely
     irrelevant" to reliability; (b) the external tradition that Mark wrote what he remembered **Peter**
     preaching is **early, widespread and unrivalled** — Papias (via Eusebius, *Hist. eccl.* 3.39), Irenaeus,
     plus Justin, Tertullian, Clement of Alexandria, Origen, Jerome, the Muratorian Canon and the
     Anti-Marcionite Prologue; (c) 1 Peter 5:13 ("Mark, my son"); (d) Bauckham 2017. **Licona's striking
     claim: the authorship evidence for Mark is *better* than for Plutarch's *Lives*** (whose attribution
     rests on the late, misattributed Lamprias Catalogue). Dating: a majority of critical scholars date Mark
     50–70 CE (≈17–40 yrs after Jesus), vs the *Augustus* written ≈105+ years after Augustus's death.
     Verdict for Mark: "a resounding yes."
  2. **The author used his sources reliably.** *Suetonius*: paraphrases/expands the *Res Gestae* but is shown
     by Keener 2016 and Goh 2016 not to invent wholesale; occasionally quotes verbatim, even in the original
     Greek. *Mark*: can't be checked directly (no named sources), but **independent parallels** confirm the
     tradition was stable — Eve 2014 lists Mark/Paul agreements (Davidic descent, Messiah, Son of God, kingdom
     of God, divorce prohibition, love command, the Eucharist institution on the night of betrayal, death by
     crucifixion, Jewish-leadership responsibility), with the Eucharist *logia* a close Greek match
     (1 Cor 11:24–25 // Mark 14:22–25); Paul had the tradition from Peter/James/John within ~4 years of the
     crucifixion (Gal 1:18–19; 2:1–10). Matthew and Luke redact Mark **far more conservatively** than
     Plutarch reworks himself across his *Lives* (Licona 2017; Downing 1980, 2011, on the "extraordinary
     extent of verbal similarities"). Mark even retains three Aramaic sayings (Talitha koum, Mark 5:41; 7:34;
     15:34), as Suetonius retains Greek. Verdict: both used sources reliably.
  3. **We can verify numerous reported items.** *Suetonius*: "did not make things up" (Hurley). *Mark*: the
     near-universal-consensus core about Jesus (lived in 1st-c. Judea; believed God called him to usher in the
     kingdom; had brothers; baptized by John; did things he and his followers took to be miracles/exorcisms;
     challenged the leadership; arrested; crucified by Pilate in 30 or 33); Bauckham's onomastics (Gospel names
     match the frequencies of 1st-c. Palestinian Jewish names); verified places and persons (Capernaum,
     Nazareth, Jerusalem…; Augustus, Quirinius, Tiberius, the Herods, Pilate, Caiaphas, John the Baptist).
     Plus the memory argument (high-impact events recalled for decades — the 9/11 and Joe Galloway/Ia Drang
     illustrations), the eyewitnesses-still-alive point, and the anti-form-critic quip from Vincent Taylor
     (1933): "If the Form-Critics are right, the disciples must have been translated to heaven immediately
     after the Resurrection" — against Ehrman 2016.
  4. **No more than a very small percentage of reported items has a reasonable chance of being an error.**
     *Suetonius*: common errors throughout the *Lives*; Edwards cautions against relying on any of his numbers
     (dates, ages, prices). *Mark*: only a *few* candidate errors — Abiathar as high priest (Mark 2:25–26 vs
     1 Sam 21:1–6), up to three possible geographical confusions, the day of the anointing, minor Passion
     chronology — all with plausible (if uncertain) solutions, and even if every one were an error it is a
     tiny fraction of Mark.
- **The honest open challenge Licona raises himself (the abstract's "additional factor"): prejudicial
  selectivity.** Every biographer, ancient or modern, selects; Suetonius downplays Augustus's adulteries and
  softens his cruelties. Even if *every reported detail is true*, selection can leave a "sanitized" or
  "somewhat distorting" portrait (his MLK Jr. analogy). Licona flags this as a real, unresolved difficulty
  for any broad claim of "historical reliability" and calls for "more thought" — he does **not** tidy it away.
- **Conclusion.** On the four criteria, Mark comes out historically reliable in the qualified "accurate
  gist / true enough" sense — **"perhaps even more so than Suetonius's *Life of Augustus*."** Serious
  challenges (selectivity) remain.

## ⚠ Standing caution flags (carry into any pull)
1. **Scope honesty is IN the paper — keep it.** Licona himself fences the metanarrative (the deity /
   resurrection-as-divine-act claims) as beyond historical tools. A mined line may say the *bios* is
   "reliable / true enough" about the reportable events; it may **not** slide into "history proves Jesus is
   God / rose." That is the resurrection essay's own-ground argument, not this paper's.
2. **"Reliable" ≠ "inspired/inerrant" ≠ "true."** The paper is explicitly about *broad historical
   reliability* of a *bios*, and even concedes a few probable Markan errors. This is the SAME bound
   `consistency.html` already draws ("consistency removes a defeater; it does not prove the contents true").
   Don't let "even more reliable than Suetonius" be compressed into "error-free" — Licona says the opposite.
3. **Don't overstate the comparison into a slogan.** "More reliable than the finest Roman biography" is
   Licona's bounded judgement on *his four criteria*, resting largely on **dating** (17–40 yrs vs 105+ yrs)
   and **sourcing** (Peter's eyewitness testimony vs tabloid rumor). Port it WITH its grounds, not as a
   bare boast; Suetonius's selectivity challenge applies to Mark too (Licona says so).
4. **Ehrman is engaged fairly here** (the form-critical memory objection, Ehrman 2016). Any pull must keep
   the EXPLICIT-VERDICT / steelman discipline the resurrection + reliability pages already hold.
