## A · Hemer, "First Person Narrative in Acts 27–28" (1985)

### Thesis (our words)
The shipwreck voyage is the test case for the "we-passages." Hemer does two things: he **dismantles** the
then-influential claim (V. K. Robbins) that first-person-plural narration was a *literary convention* of
an ancient sea-voyage genre — and therefore no evidence of the author's presence; and he assembles
**epigraphic and documentary evidence**, most of it never used in the commentaries, showing that the
narrative's incidental details fit the real first-century world so precisely and so *unobtrusively* that
the most economical explanation is that the writer made the voyage. His own verdict is carefully bounded:
formal proof is impossible, but the burden of proof shifts to the doubter.

### Argument map → primaries to VERIFY
**1 · Refuting the "sea-voyage genre."** Robbins ("By Land and By Sea: The We-Passages and Ancient Sea
Voyages," in *Perspectives on Luke-Acts*, ed. C. H. Talbert [T&T Clark, 1978], 215–42) argued the "we" is a
convention. Hemer checks the examples one by one and finds them mishandled: **the Voyage of Hanno** (§1 is
a prefatory heading, not part of the narrative; the report proper is "we" because the explorer was there);
**a Flinders Petrie papyrus on the Third Syrian War** (Mahaffy 1893 No. 45; better text in Mitteis &
Wilcken, *Grundzüge und Chrestomathie der Papyruskunde* I.2 [1912] No. 1 — the "we/they" is
Ptolemies-vs-Seleucids, used on *land* as well as at sea); **the Antiochene Acts of Ignatius** (late and
composite — J. B. Lightfoot, *Apostolic Fathers* II.2 [1889], 383–91, 477–95). Then the counter-examples
he checked at random are all in the **third** person: **Caesar,** *BG* 4.23–24, 28; 5.8; **Lucian,**
*Navigium* 7–9. Other "parallels" collapse in context: **Achilles Tatius** 2.31.4–6, 3.1.1, 4.9.6;
**Josephus,** *Vita* 3.15; **Lucian,** *Verae Historiae* 1.5–6; **Dio Chrysostom,** *Or.* 7.2, 10;
**Petronius,** *Sat.* 114–115; **Ovid,** *Tristia* 1.2 (where the "we" is Latin verse's metrical
substitute for "I," and appears in the *non*-maritime lines too). → **Core point to keep:** voyage
narratives are often in "we" **because they recall communal experience**, not because a genre demanded
it. Hemer's own line of argument, in our words: *a natural tendency dictated by the situation, not an
artificial literary device.*
⚠ **Methodological caution he raises (worth borrowing):** he compares the "sea-voyage genre" to the
scholarly construct of the "diatribe" (Bultmann 1910; H. D. Jocelyn's demolition in *Liverpool Classical
Monthly* 4.7 [1979] 145–46 and 7.1 [1982] 3–7) — a *Gattung* assembled piecemeal from scattered stylistic
parallels. Use this cautiously and **never** to imply all genre criticism is bogus.

**2 · The Alexandrian grain fleet (the setting).** Acts 27:6 and 28:11 both specify **Alexandrian** ships,
and 27:38 says the cargo was grain. → *Primaries:* the office of *praefectus annonae* and **Gaius
Turranius** (Tacitus, *Ann.* 1.7; 11.31); Claudian famines (**Dio** 60.11; **Tacitus,** *Ann.* 12.43;
**Suetonius,** *Claud.* 18.2; cf. **Acts 11:28** and **Josephus,** *Ant.* 20.51–53; 20.101; **Pliny,**
*NH* 5.10.58); Claudius's harbour at Portus (**Suet.** *Claud.* 18.3; **Dio** 60.11.4–5; *CIL* 14.85, AD
46; Nero's *sestertii* AVGVSTI POR. OST., AD 64–66); privileges to shipbuilders and storm-loss
indemnities (**Suet.** *Claud.* 18.2, still in force in Suetonius's own day); the crowds at Puteoli
greeting the fleet, the advance ship recognised by its topsail — **Seneca,** *Ep.* 77.1 (*Gallio's own
brother*, a nice touch); the ship "Isis" (**Lucian,** *Navigium* 5–6); **Josephus,** *Vita* 3.16 (he too
disembarked at Puteoli, from a near-contemporary voyage, c. AD 61); sailing-schedule papyri (**P.Oxy.**
2191 — which uses *bradyploia*, cf. the cognate verb at **Acts 27:7**; P.Oxy. 1763; Loeb *Select Papyri*
1.113). Secondary framing throughout from **L. Casson,** *The Ancient Mariners* (1959) and *Ships and
Seamanship in the Ancient World* (Princeton UP, 1971), and **K. S. Gapp,** "The Universal Famine under
Claudius," *HTR* 28 (1935): 258–65.
→ *Payoff (our words):* the centurion's surprising authority over the professionals (**Acts 27:11, 43**)
and the crew's refusal to jettison the cargo until the last extremity are exactly what an imperial-service
grain ship late in the season would produce — and Luke never explains any of it.

**3 · Nine documentary details (the heart of the article — all checkable).**
- **Lasaea (Acts 27:8)** — long thought unattested; it appears as Λασσοία in the Delphi list of
  *theorodokoi*, arranged geographically along the Cretan coast. → *Primary:* A. Plassart, "Les
  inscriptions de Delphes: la liste des théorodoques," *BCH* 45 (1921): 1–85, col. IV.7–10 (early 2nd c. BC).
  The form in Acts is **closer to the epigraphic spelling than any literary source's**.
- **Cauda / Clauda (Acts 27:16)** — *I.Cret.* 2.7.1 ("to Caudian Zeus," c. 3rd c. BC) and a Gortyna
  treaty, *I.Cret.* 4.184 (early 2nd c. BC). **Luke places the island correctly; Pliny (*NH* 4.12.61–62)
  puts it ~90 miles too far east and Ptolemy (*Geog.* 3.17.11) displaces it north-west.**
- **"Euraquilo" (Acts 27:14)** — long treated as a *hapax* and therefore a suspect reading. A twelve-point
  Latin **wind-rose pavement at Thugga** (*CIL* 8.26652) gives *euroaquilo* in exactly the right position,
  30° N of E, between *eurus* and *aquilo*. → cf. Hemer, "Euraquilo and Melita," *JTS* n.s. 26 (1975): 100–111.
- **"Northwest / southwest" at Phoenix (Acts 27:12)** — the twelve-sided wind-name base *IGRR* 1.177 d–h;
  the *chi-* spelling of *chōros* may reflect sailors' mixed Latin patois. Topography: **R. M. Ogilvie,
  "Phoenix," *JTS* n.s. 9 (1958): 308–14**.
- **⭐ The Phoenix ship inscription** — *CIL* 3.3 = *ILS* 4395 = *I.Cret.* 2.20.7 (AD 104–114), found at
  Lutro/Phoenix: a dedication by the crew of a **wintering Alexandrian ship** whose name-device is
  *Isopharia* (Isis of the Pharos), supervised by an Alexandrian *gubernator* and involving an imperial
  freedman. **Precisely parallel to Acts 28:11's ship "with the Dioscuri as its figurehead," and from
  precisely the harbour where Paul's crew wanted to winter.** Hemer notes with surprise that the
  commentaries (Ramsay, Wikenhauser, Bruce) do not use it.
- **Malta's chief magistrate "prōtos" (Acts 28:7)** — *IGRR* 1.512 = *IG* 14.601 (AD 14–37) attests
  πρῶτος Μελιταίων as the title. ⚠ **HONESTY FLAG:** the Latin text usually cited alongside it (*CIL*
  10.7495) Hemer judges **indecisive** — its lines are mutilated at both ends and *primus* there most
  likely means "first to perform" such benefactions. Popular apologetics routinely stacks both; **use the
  Greek inscription only.**
- **Malta's "barbaroi" (Acts 28:2)** — a *linguistic* description, supported by the island's surviving
  Punic epigraphy (the Punic–Greek bilingual *CIG* 5753 = *IG* 14.600 = *CIS* 1.122; Cooke, *North-Semitic
  Inscriptions* [1903], 102–07) and the Punic legends on Malta's early coinage (B. V. Head, *Historia
  Numorum*, 2nd ed. [1911], 883).
- **"Adria" (Acts 27:27)** — in first-century usage the open sea bounded by Sicily, southern Italy, Crete
  and the Peloponnese, *not* the modern Adriatic Gulf (**Ptolemy,** *Geog.* 3 passim). This **kills the
  Mljet/Dalmatia shipwreck theories** (Acworth; Meinardus), which rest on the modern sense. Cf. the
  *navicularii maris Hadriatici* guild at Ostia (*ILS* 6146, 7277).
- **Nautical vocabulary** — *bolizō* "heave the lead" (Eustathius on *Il.* 5.396, via Wetstein's 1752
  *Novum Testamentum Graecum*, which Hemer praises); *skaphē* (Acts 27:16) used in the technical sense of
  Latin *scapha*, a towed ship's boat (Caesar, *BG* 4.26.4).

**4 · The chronological convergence (a striking, bounded lead).** "The Fast" of **Acts 27:9** = the Day of
Atonement, 10 Tishri, lunar-dated. Working the voyage backwards from a three-month Malta wintering plus the
earliest-possible spring sailing, Hemer argues the ostensible chronology only works if the Fast fell
unusually **late** that year — and independent lines converge on **AD 59**, when it fell on or about
5 October, later than in any neighbouring year. → *Primary lead:* W. P. Workman, "A New Date-Indication in
Acts," *ExpT* 11 (1899–1900): 316–18; cf. **Pliny,** *NH* 2.47.122, 126 on the sailing season.
⚠ Hemer presents this as *convergence*, not proof. Keep it there.

**5 · The comparative-method check (excellent, and quotable in our own words).** R. P. C. Hanson, "The
Journey of Paul and the Journey of Nikias: An Experiment in Comparative Historiography," *Studia
Evangelica* 4 (1968): 315–18, applies Conzelmann's method to **Thucydides 6.1–61** — a voyage narrative
every ancient historian accepts — and shows it could be dismissed as legendary on precisely the same
grounds. Hanson's point: Nikias, like Paul, is "detachable" because passengers always are.

### Hemer's own verdict (bounded)
Formal proof is impossible. But the incidental details are *too integrated* to have suffered a shift of
context, and the sceptical case rests on theoretical improbability rather than any demonstrated error —
so the onus shifts to the doubter. His term for what the passage has is **"immediacy"**: detail that is
*precise but inconsequential*, the kind even an eyewitness writing years later would have smoothed away.

## B & C · Hemer, "The Speeches of Acts" I–II (1989)

### I. The Ephesian elders at Miletus (Acts 20:17–38)
The only major speech addressed to a **Christian** audience, and the only one inside a "we-passage" — so
the best place to compare the Paul of Acts with the Paul of the letters. Against **Dibelius**'s
"biographical encomium" classification ("The Speeches in Acts and Ancient Historiography," in *Studies in
the Acts of the Apostles* [SCM, 1956], 155–57), Hemer argues the label **begs the question**: Dibelius's
only specific parallel is **Lucian,** *Peregrinus* 32 — a self-immolating exhibitionist delivering his own
funeral oration, no kind of norm. He also notes that the scholars who *do* argue from structure reach
**mutually incompatible** results (Dibelius's four paragraphs; Exum & Talbert's chiasm, *CBQ* 29 [1967]
233–36; Michel's *Abschiedsrede* [SANT 35, 1973], 27; Lambrecht) — so structure yields no agreed answer.
Simplest explanation: an emotional farewell in a real situation. Paul's self-defence fits his position
just after the Corinthian crisis (**2 Cor 10–12**; cf. **Gal 1:6–7** with **Acts 20:29**), and he was then
carrying the collection (**Acts 20:4**), which sharpens **20:33–35**. On the **agraphon of Acts 20:35**
("more blessed to give than to receive"), unparalleled in the Gospels: Haenchen treats it as a Greek
proverb Luke put in Jesus' mouth; Hemer replies the alleged parallels are not close (only **Thucydides
3.97.4** is near), and the reverent manner of its introduction tells for its authenticity.

### II. The Areopagus address (Acts 17:16–34)
Hemer locates the scene at the **Stoa Basileios in the north-west corner of the Agora** — next to the Stoa
Poikile where the Stoics got their name (**Diogenes Laertius** 7.1.5), and next to the main entrance from
the Piraeus road — giving the episode an **economical dramatic unity of place that Luke never points out**.
→ *Primaries:* Hemer, "Paul at Athens: A Topographical Note," *NTS* 20 (1974): 341–50; on the meeting-place,
Demosthenes 59.80–83; *Ath. Pol.* 57.3–4, 60.2–3; Pausanias 1.28.5; (Ps.-)Demosthenes 25.3 (explicit for
the Stoa Basileios); **Plato,** *Euthyphro* 2a (Socrates before the Basileus in the same Stoa — a parallel
Hemer expressly says is **largely fortuitous and not to be pressed**); the 1969–70 identification of the
Basileios (P. M. Fraser, *Archaeological Reports* 16 [1969–70]: 3–4); R. E. Wycherley, "St Paul at Athens,"
*JTS* n.s. 19 (1968): 619–20 on *kateidōlos* (Acts 17:16) as "luxuriant with idols" and the forest of
Herms attested by Harpocration and by the Agora excavations.
**Inside the speech:** the Stoic poet **Aratus of Soli,** *Phaenomena* 5 (cited at **Acts 17:28**) — Paul's
fellow-Cilician; cf. **Cleanthes,** *Hymn to Zeus* 4; *to theion* as philosophical-Absolute language
(**Epictetus** 2.20.22); the Epicurean god who needs nothing vs. the Stoic source of all life (**Lucretius,**
*DRN* 2.165–83; 5.146–234); *deisidaimonesteros* (17:22) as deliberately ambivalent; **Epimenides** behind
"in him we live and move and have our being," and behind the Athenian *bōmoi anōnymoi* legend (**Diogenes
Laertius** 1.110–112; **Clement,** *Strom.* 1.14; cf. **Titus 1:12**); and — the best single lead —
*anastasis* in **Aeschylus,** *Eumenides* 647–48, where Apollo declares before *the court of the Areopagus
itself* that once a man's dust drinks his blood there is **no resurrection**. → *Payoff (our words):* the
one Greek word in the speech that would land hardest on that court, in that place, is the one Greek
literature had already used to deny the thing Paul was asserting — and Luke gives no sign of noticing.
⚠ **Steelman on the record:** A. D. Nock (*Gnomon* 25 [1953]: 506) explained the Athenian colour as stock
literary "local colour" any writer could glean. Hemer concedes the point about the *obvious* motifs and
rests his case only on the **tacit** connections a redactor would have flagged if he had made them.
⚠ **Also note (a fact worth verifying and keeping):** F. F. Bruce reports that Eduard Meyer later
**converted Eduard Norden** (*Agnostos Theos*, 1913) to admitting the speech could be genuine.

## D · Seccombe, "Dating Luke-Acts: Further Arguments for an Early Date" (2020)

### Thesis (our words)
Accepting the case for the early 60s already made by others, Seccombe adds **three fresh considerations
drawn from the history of the rest of the century** — none of which is an argument from silence. He is
explicit that he has not closed the discussion.

**Argument 1 · Political inappropriateness.** From AD 66 an intense anti-Jewish climate prevailed across
the empire (massacres at Damascus, Caesarea, Scythopolis, Ascalon — **Josephus,** *BJ* 2.457, 461–65, 477–80,
487–98; Titus's victory spectacles; the punitive *fiscus Judaicus*; **Suetonius,** *Dom.* 12.2 on the
circumcision inspection; Nerva's reform coin). In that climate, **Luke 1–2** — a Jewish king who scatters
the arrogant and brings down rulers (1:51–52), rescue from those who hate Israel (1:71, 73), the
"consolation of Israel" and "redemption of Jerusalem" (2:25, 38), a child titled *Saviour* and *Lord*
(imperial titles), a disciple called a **Zealot** (6:15) — plus **Acts 1:6** and **Acts 28:20** ("bound
with this chain because of the hope of Israel") would have been dangerous to write, own, or circulate,
right through to ~AD 150. → *Primaries:* the Josephus and Suetonius refs above; Smallwood, *The Jews under
Roman Rule* (Brill, 1976), 331–88, esp. 352 on Rome's watch for messianic movements; Schwartz and
Kerkeslager in *The Cambridge History of Judaism* (CUP, 2006).
**Argument 2 · An apologia for Gentile Christianity that only fits pre-70.** Acts spends chapters 10–11
and 15 arguing that the Gentile mission was God-initiated and that circumcision is not required — an
argument whose *shape presupposes Jews in the position of strength*, being asked to accommodate Gentiles.
After 70, with Judaism subjugated and Christianity increasingly Gentile, that argument has lost its
occasion. Seccombe rebuts the counter-proposal (J. L. Martyn's *Birkat ha-Minim*; Maddox; Esler) by noting
there was no coercive Jewish authority in this period and no evidence Christians sheltered under the
synagogue (**Ignatius**, *Magn.* 8–10, *Phld.* 6, 8–9; **Pliny,** *Ep.* 10.96–97 — Christians meeting on
Sunday, already distinct).
**Argument 3 · The disproportionate voyage (a "repackaging of Rackham and Hemer").** AD 62–71 was a
cataclysmic decade — James executed under Ananus (**Josephus,** *Ant.* 20.197–203), Nero's pogrom (**Tacitus,**
*Ann.* 15–16), the war, the deaths of Peter and Paul, the Temple burned (*BJ* 6.164–284). Acts hints at
none of it; what it is *loud* on is the voyage — one of the longest and least theological sections of an
18,000-word book. Vivid, inconsequential detail like that reads as a **recent** impression, not one that
survived the following decade. (Armstrong adds that **no textual variant or expansion of Acts 28 betrays
knowledge of the 60s–70s events** either.)
**Fiction test.** Against Pervo's "novel," Seccombe's criterion is **loose ends**: a novel (e.g.
**Chariton,** *Callirhoe*) is homogeneous and every part serves the plot; Acts is full of unanswered
questions and loosely-connected events. Cf. W. F. Brosend, "The Means of Absent Ends," in *History,
Literature and Society in the Book of Acts*, ed. Witherington (CUP, 1996), 348–62; and note that even
**F. C. Baur** thought the author must have had a genuine voyage diary, and **Haenchen** treats Acts 27 as
a serious account of a real voyage.
**Currency (the most useful part for us).** The current landscape, with page-precise cites: **Craig S.
Keener,** *Acts: An Exegetical Commentary*, vol. 1 (Baker Academic, 2012), 383–401 — "my best guesses …
are in the early 70s, with dates in the 80s and 60s still plausible, and a date in the 90s not impossible"
(400), and a first-century date "should be regarded as secure" (401), with his critique of the
second-century case at 395–400. Early-date proponents: Rackham (*JTS* 1 [1899]: 76–87), Harnack (three
books, 1906–11), Reicke, **Robinson** (*Redating*, 1976), Matill (*CBQ* 40 [1978]: 335–50), **Hemer**
(*The Book of Acts in the Setting of Hellenistic History* [Mohr Siebeck/Eisenbrauns, 1989/1990], 365–410),
**Alexander Mittelstaedt,** *Lukas als Historiker: Zur Datierung des lukanischen Doppelwerkes* (Francke,
2006), and **Karl L. Armstrong, "A New Plea for an Early Date of Acts," *Journal of Greco-Roman
Christianity and Judaism* 13 (2017): 79–110** (the list of pre-70 authorities is at 98–99). Second-century
proponents to steelman: **Pervo,** *Dating Acts* (2006) and *Acts: A Commentary* (Hermeneia, 2009), 5;
**Tyson,** *Marcion and Luke-Acts* (2006); Nasrallah, *JBL* 127 (2008): 533–66.

## E · Bruce, *The Speeches in the Acts of the Apostles* (Tyndale NT Lecture, 1942)

### Thesis (our words)
A survey of the Christian speeches in Acts (evangelistic / deliberative / apologetic / hortatory), asking
whether they are, as **Dibelius** held, "without doubt … inventions of the author," or condensed reports.
Bruce's verdict, stated modestly: **not verbatim transcripts** — every speech in Acts can be read aloud in
a few minutes, while Paul preached at length (Acts 20:7–9; 28:23) — but **faithful epitomes giving the
gist**, and therefore independent sources for primitive Christian history and theology.

### The load-bearing moves → primaries to VERIFY
- **The Greek historiographical convention, honestly stated.** **Thucydides** 1.22.1 promises to make
  speakers say what the occasion demanded "adhering as closely as possible to the general sense of what
  they really said." Later writers inherited the licence without the conscience — **Dionysius of
  Halicarnassus,** *De Thucydide* 34; **Polybius** 2.56.10–12 (the historian must record what was really
  said, not what a tragedian would find probable); and **Josephus,** *Ant.* 1.13.3 and 2.6.8–9 as the
  first-century *bad* example (an elaborate invented speech for Abraham at the Aqedah).
  ⚠ **This convention is the objection, and it must be stated at full strength before any reply.**
- **The control case.** Where Luke's handling of speech material *can* be tested — his Sayings source and
  his use of Mark — he modifies style and order but not content. → *Primary:* **F. C. Burkitt,** "Luke's
  Use of Mark," in *The Beginnings of Christianity* II, 106 ff., esp. 115 on Luke 21 vs. Mark 13: the
  point is not how much Luke changed but **how little he invented**.
- **Aramaic substratum in the Petrine speeches.** Acts 2, 3, 4, 5, 10 sit in sections with strong signs of
  an Aramaic source; **Acts 10:36–38** is awkward Greek that turns word-for-word into grammatical Aramaic,
  and **Acts 3:16**'s difficulty eases on a re-pointing of a posited Aramaic substratum. → *Primaries:*
  J. de Zwaan, "The Use of the Greek Language in Acts," *Beginnings* II, 30 ff., esp. 44 ff.; C. C. Torrey,
  *The Composition and Date of Acts* (1916) — ⚠ Bruce explicitly declines Torrey's *whole-first-half*
  translation thesis; take the moderate form only.
- **Primitive, pre-Pauline kerygma.** **C. H. Dodd,** *The Apostolic Preaching and its Developments* (1936),
  esp. 56, 59–60, 62, and *History and the Gospel* (1933), 73: the Acts kerygma's substance "is as early as
  the time of Paul," with a "strong Aramaic colouring." Dodd, "The Framework of the Gospel Narrative,"
  *ExpT* 43 (1932): 396 ff., traces the same outline in Mark. ⚠ Bruce notes he dates Acts far earlier than
  Dodd does, and that Dodd's argument does not depend on it.
  ⚠⚠ **ORTHODOXY-ADJACENT FLAG (read before using).** Bruce observes that the Christology of these earliest
  speeches is "undeveloped and pre-Pauline" and that some expressions "would, if penned in the fourth
  century, have made Athanasius and his friends shake their heads." **In our voice this must never become
  "the earliest Christians had a lower Christology" or any hint of developmental Arianism.** The defensible
  point is narrow and purely historical: *the vocabulary of the earliest preaching is pre-technical, formed
  before the fourth-century controversies gave these words fixed senses* — which is itself a mark of
  antiquity, not of a different faith. Bruce's own next sentence guards it: these speeches are "none the
  less essentially theological," presenting Jesus as appointed Saviour, raised by God's power, and exalted
  as **Lord and Messiah**, and they "just as little as any other part of the NT" support a "liberal" Jesus.
  Any use of this material is **dual-consensus** territory (Christology) and must carry that guard.
- **The Pisidian Antioch sermon (Acts 13:16–41).** Its kerygma matches Peter's, confirming **1 Cor 15:11**.
  On **Acts 13:38–39** Bruce sets out the *ambiguous* grammar honestly and notes that B. W. Bacon (*The
  Story of St. Paul*, 103) reads it un-Paulinely; Bruce answers, but shows the objection first. Percy
  Gardner (*Cambridge Biblical Essays*, 397–98) runs the "too Petrine to be Pauline" argument in reverse.
  H. J. Cadbury (*Beginnings* V, 407 ff.) argues Lukan authorship from common style + common OT exegesis.
- **Testimonia.** Shared proof-texts (**Ps 110:1; Ps 118:22; Ps 16:10** at Acts 2:27 / 13:35; **Deut
  18:15** at Acts 3:22 / 7:37; **Isa 6:9–10** at Acts 28:26–27) point to an early collection of Messianic
  *testimonia* rather than to one author's hand. → *Primary:* J. Rendel Harris, *Testimonies* (CUP,
  1916–20).
- **The defences (Acts 22 and 26).** So finely adapted to their different audiences that either the
  composer was extraordinarily astute or these are substantially faithful reports; and **the differences
  between Acts 9, 22, and 26 tell for authenticity** — an inventor would not manufacture discrepancies.
  Atticising touches in Acts 26 (*isasin* for Koine *oidasin*, the true superlative *akribestatēn*) —
  → *Primary:* F. Blass, *Philology of the Gospels*, 9. The Sanhedrin scene (Acts 23) preserves an
  unflattering stratagem a hagiographer would have cut (Foakes Jackson, *The Acts of the Apostles*, 206).
- **Miletus again (Acts 20:18–38).** Rich in parallels to the epistles — but Acts shows **no sign anywhere
  of acquaintance with the Pauline letters**, which a borrower could not have resisted using. Gardner:
  this speech "has the best claim of all to be historic."

## Guardrail fit (read before pulling from ANY of the five)
- **⚠ The load-bearing bound: Acts historicity corroborates the SETTING, not the doctrine.** Every
  inscription here shows Luke knew the first-century world; **none** shows a miracle happened or a doctrine
  is true. This is the exact sibling of the Bible-reliability rule (manuscripts prove preservation, not
  truth) and of the bound `library/archaeology.html` already models. Never let "Luke gets Lasaea right"
  slide toward "so Acts is true."
- **⚠ Bruce's "undeveloped Christology" line** — see the flag above. Dual-consensus (orthodoxy +
  neutrality) required for anything that touches it.
- **⚠ Two specific over-claims to avoid, both flagged by the sources themselves.** (1) The Latin Malta
  inscription *CIL* 10.7495 is **indecisive** — cite only the Greek *prōtos* text. (2) The Erastus pavement
  and the Sergius Paulus family identification are **plausible, not secure** — `archaeology.html` already
  says so; keep it.
- **⚠ Minority-position honesty.** The early-60s dating of Acts is a **respected minority** view, not the
  consensus (Keener's ~AD 75 is nearer the centre). `library/earlydate.html` already says this exactly
  right. Seccombe's arguments strengthen the minority case; they do not convert it into the consensus, and
  Seccombe himself says he has not closed the discussion.
- **⚠ Sherwin-White caution (already live on-site).** The famous "any attempt to reject its basic
  historicity … must now appear absurd" line is about the **framework of persons, events and customs** —
  `archaeology.html` fn 3 already fences it. Keep the fence.
- **Denominational neutrality:** clean. Bruce (Brethren), Hemer, and Seccombe (Anglican) all write
  historically; nothing intra-Christian is at stake. Bruce's one exegetical aside on justification at Acts
  13:39 touches a Reformation-adjacent theme — leave it out of our neutral voice.
- **Age caveat:** Bruce 1942 and Hemer 1985/1989 are older; the epigraphy and the Greek historiographical
  primaries do not age, but the *scholarly landscape* they answer does. Pair any use with Seccombe 2020 /
  Keener 2012 for currency, and don't present 1940s-era "recent scholarship" as current.
