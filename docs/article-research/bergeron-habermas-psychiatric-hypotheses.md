## Thesis (our words)
The naturalistic explanations offered for the disciples' post-crucifixion resurrection *beliefs* —
**hallucinations, conversion disorder, and bereavement (grief) experiences** — are, at bottom, claims of
psychiatric pathology, yet they are advanced mostly by **non-medical writers** and appear in **no
peer-reviewed medical literature** (a PubMed/APA search, 1918–2012, finds none). Subjected to actual
clinical appraisal, each hypothesis fails to fit the reported data. **(Scope: this is a DEFENSE that clears
away a naturalistic theory — NOT a proof of the resurrection; the positive case leads with the 1 Cor 15
creed elsewhere. The bounded verdict is "these hypotheses don't fit the clinical profile," never
"hallucination is medically impossible.")**

## Argument map (beats in our words → the primaries/clinical sources to VERIFY)

**Frame.** Jesus' death by crucifixion is a majority-held historical fact (Licona, *The Resurrection of
Jesus*, IVP 2010, 318); the death mechanism (shock + blood loss) is medically coherent (Bergeron, "The
Crucifixion of Jesus…", *Journal of Forensic and Legal Medicine* 19 [2012]: 113–116; F. T. Zugibe, *The
Crucifixion of Jesus: A Forensic Inquiry*, 2005, 135). NT historians concur the disciples experienced
*something* (Habermas, "Experiences of the Risen Jesus," *Dialog* 45 [2006]: 288–97). A **PubMed + APA
database search (1918–2012)** finds the three psychiatric hypotheses in **no peer-reviewed medical
literature** — so a clinician's review is warranted.

**1 · Hallucination hypotheses (the proponents to steelman).**
- Celsus (2nd c.), via **Origen** (a "hysterical female… had a hallucination") — H. Chadwick, *Origen: Contra
  Celsum*, CUP 2003, 109; G. O'Collins, *Christology*, OUP 2009, 93.
- **David F. Strauss** — "subjective visions"/"hallucinations" (*A New Life of Jesus*, vol. 1, London: Williams and
  Norgate, 1879, 414–31, 440 — ⚠ the article's fn gives "Green and Sons"; the standard publisher is Williams and Norgate, verify when citing).
- **Gerd Lüdemann** — Peter's grief-hallucination spreading by "contagious religious ecstasy" into a "shared
  hallucinatory fantasy," incl. Paul's Damascus vision as self-aggrandizing ecstasy (*The Resurrection of
  Christ*, Prometheus 2004, 172, 175–76; *The Resurrection of Jesus*, Fortress 1994, 48–54, 53; the *ōphthē/
  horaō* generalization over **1 Cor 15:5–8**).
- Contra: **James D. G. Dunn** — hallucination-projection explanations carry "greater improbabilities than…
  realized" (*The Evidence for Jesus*, SCM 1985, 71–72).

**2 · Clinical appraisal of hallucination (the medical core — net-new value).**
- Hallucination = a **symptom, not a diagnosis**; three etiologies (psychophysiologic / psychobiochemical /
  psychodynamic) — B. Teeple, J. Caplan & T. Stern, "Visual Hallucinations: Differential Diagnosis and
  Treatment," *Primary Care Companion to the J. of Clinical Psychiatry* 11 (2009): 26–32.
- Prevalence 10–39% in non-clinical populations, but that "continuum" is a *psychological*, not *medical*,
  view — A. Aleman & F. Larøi, *Hallucinations: The Science of Idiosyncratic Perception*, APA 2008, 9, 61–68
  (note: **no mention of collective group hallucinations**).
- **KEY move — hallucinations are private/individual, so they cannot explain the *simultaneous group*
  appearances**; "collective hallucination" is **not in peer-reviewed medical/psych literature** and **not in
  the DSM-5** (American Psychiatric Association, *Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders*, 5th
  ed., 2013). Corroborating clinician: **Gary A. Sibcy, PhD** (surveyed 2 decades, found no documented group
  hallucination). Cf. the Licona–Aleman/Larøi email ("very little scientific documentation") in Licona,
  *Resurrection of Jesus*, fn 64, 484–85.
- The one book that entertains group apparitions (**L. Zusne & W. Jones**, *Anomalistic Psychology*, Erlbaum
  1982, 135–36) requires group "expectation" + "emotional excitement" — then waffles. **Jake O'Connell**,
  "Jesus' Resurrection and Collective Hallucinations," *Tyndale Bulletin* 60 (2009): 69–105, grants collective
  hallucinations are (rarely) real but shows they **don't fit**: they need heightened expectation, not everyone
  sees, the content differs person-to-person, the apparitions don't converse, and would appear *glorified*
  (none in the narratives). The disciples were **forlorn, not expectant** (Lk 24:10–11, 17, 21).
- The **1 Cor 15:3–7 creed** is near-unanimously an early Jerusalem formula within a few years (N. T. Wright,
  *The Resurrection of the Son of God*, Fortress 2003, 319; **Bart Ehrman**, *Did Jesus Exist?*, 2012, dates it
  to within 1–2 years — esp. 131–32, 157, 164, 170; **Bauckham**, *Jesus and the Eyewitnesses*, 2006, ch. 11).
  *ōphthē/horaō* usually = physical sight (Licona 329–33, 400–37; Wright 207–398 on *anastasis/egeirō* + the
  bodily-resurrection consensus). Even skeptics grant Paul believed the appearance was **bodily** — Crossan &
  Reed, *In Search of Paul*, 2004, 6–10, 296, 341–45; Lüdemann in Copan & Tacelli (eds.), *Jesus' Resurrection:
  Fact or Figment?*, IVP 2000, 151–52; Dale Allison, *Resurrecting Jesus*, T&T Clark 2005, 321–26. Paul sought
  no leadership ("least of the apostles," 1 Cor 15:9; a former persecutor, Phil 3:6; Gal 1:13).

**3 · Conversion-disorder hypotheses.**
- Proponents: **Jack A. Kent**, *The Psychological Origins of the Resurrection Myth*, Open Gate 1999, 21, 33,
  48, 54, 61 (Paul's Damascus = conversion disorder from Stephen's death + Gamaliel); **Michael Goulder**,
  "The Explanatory Power of Conversion-Visions," in Copan & Tacelli (eds.), *Jesus' Resurrection: Fact or Figment?*, IVP 2000, p. 94 (chapter pp. 86–103) —
  coins "conversion visions," cites **Carl Jung** (*Contributions to Analytical Psychology*, 1928, 9, 258–60)
  on "psychogenic blindness," and parallels Susan Atkins (Manson Family) + Ezekiel.
- Clinical: the term traces to Freud (Breuer & Freud, *Studies in Hysteria*, trans. Strachey, 1957, 206);
  conversion disorder = neurologic symptoms without organic cause + a stressor, ~2× in women, comorbid
  mood/personality, resolving in days–30 days, marked by *la belle indifférence*, and **NOT associated with
  hallucinations** (dual diagnosis if present) — Sadock & Sadock, *Kaplan & Sadock's Synopsis of
  Psychiatry*, Lippincott 2003, 647, 649–50; Allin, Streeruwitz & Curtis, "Progress in Understanding Conversion
  Disorder," *Neuropsychiatric Disease and Treatment* 1 (2005): 205–209; Owens & Dein, "Conversion Disorder:
  The Modern Hysteria," *Advances in Psychiatric Treatment* 12 (2006): 152–57. Paul doesn't fit (zealous,
  "blameless" Phil 3:4–6, no trauma/comorbidity); "conversion vision" is **not in the DSM-5** (p. 319).

**4 · Bereavement / grief-vision hypotheses.**
- Proponents: **Lüdemann** (Peter's "unsuccessful mourning," *Resurrection of Jesus* 100, *Resurrection of
  Christ* 165–66); **Kent** (grief hallucinations; Mary Magdalene = grief, *Psychological Origins* 21, 33, 48);
  **Dewi Rees** (bereavement can carry religious significance; *Pointers to Eternity*, Y Lolfa 2010, 176–79,
  193–95, 198, 205) — though Rees **admits bereavement can't explain the group encounters**.
- Clinical data (net-new): **W. Dewi Rees**, "The Hallucinations of Widowhood," *British Medical Journal* 4
  (1971): 37–41 (293 widowed in Wales; 46% "felt the presence," visual 14%, speaking 11.6%, tactile 2.7%);
  **Gurmeet Singh et al.**, "Acute Grief: The First Year of Bereavement," *Indian Journal of Psychiatry* 31
  (1989): 187–95 ("sense of presence" 11%→21%→4%; **bereavement *visions* not described**); **Naomi Simon et
  al.**, "Informing the Symptom Profile of Complicated Grief," *Depression and Anxiety* 28 (2011): 118–26 (782
  spouses; visual appearances 4%, 10.8% in complicated grief); Sadock, *Synopsis of Psychiatry*, 61–63
  (mourning stages).
- Why it fails: bereavement visions wouldn't strike *all* the disciples, wouldn't read as a *living* Jesus,
  tactile is rare/unpleasant, wouldn't launch public proclamation; most common in **widowhood after a long
  marriage** (not a mentor–student band); can persist *years* (unlike the brief window); and first-century Jews
  had **no near-term bodily-resurrection category** (Wright, *RSG*, 205). Plus the empty tomb; Jesus'
  predicted appearances (Licona 284–302); the unparalleled 1 Cor 15:3ff array; and bereavement *convinces the
  griever the person is dead*. Key contrast source: **Gerald O'Collins**, "The Resurrection and Bereavement
  Experiences," *Irish Theological Quarterly* 76 (2011): 224–37 (7 dissimilarities); O'Collins, *Christology*,
  99; Habermas, "Resurrection Appearances of Jesus as After-Death Communication," *Journal of Near-Death
  Studies* 30 (2012): 149–58, esp. 153.

## Guardrail fit (read before pulling)
- **Resurrection rule:** this REFUTES a naturalistic alternative; it never becomes "so the resurrection is
  proved." Keep the positive case on the early creed (1 Cor 15:3–7).
- **No overstatement (the argument gate will press this):** the honest verdict is "these psychiatric
  hypotheses do not fit the clinical profile of the reports," NOT "hallucination is medically impossible." The
  article itself is careful — mirror that bound. Note the article's own careful concession that *rare*
  collective visionary experiences exist (O'Connell) — don't flatten that into "group hallucinations are
  impossible."
- **1 Peter 3:15 / steelman:** present Lüdemann/Goulder/Kent/Rees in their strongest form (as the article does)
  before answering.
- **Popular-overstatement is LOW** (peer-reviewed ITQ), but the **specific clinical claims must be verified
  against the DSM/journal primaries themselves**, not taken on the article's word — several are single-study
  figures (Rees 1971, Singh 1989, Simon 2011) that a citations pass should confirm.
- Venue (Catholic-tradition ITQ) is doctrinally neutral for our purposes; no denominational issue.
