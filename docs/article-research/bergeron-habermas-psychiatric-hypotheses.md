## Thesis (our words)
The naturalistic explanations offered for the disciples' post-crucifixion resurrection *beliefs* —
**hallucinations, conversion disorder, and bereavement (grief) experiences** — are, at bottom, claims of
psychiatric pathology, yet they are advanced mostly by **non-medical writers** whose clinical analysis is
absent or flawed. Subjected to actual clinical appraisal, these hypotheses do not fit the data of the
early resurrection reports. **(Scope note: this is a DEFENSE that clears away a naturalistic theory — it
is not itself a proof of the resurrection; the positive case leads with the 1 Cor 15 creed elsewhere.)**

## Argument map (beats in our words → primaries/leads to VERIFY in the full text)
1. **The hypotheses on the table.** Individual and *group* hallucinations, **conversion disorder**
   (a psychiatric condition), and **bereavement/grief hallucinations** are the main naturalistic
   proposals for why the disciples believed they saw the risen Jesus. → *VERIFY the exact taxonomy and
   which authors the article attributes each to (e.g. Lüdemann, Goulder for hallucination; others).*
2. **Hallucinations are private, individual mental events — not collective or shareable.** The clinical
   literature treats a hallucination as internal to one perceiver; there is no recognized phenomenon of a
   *group* sharing one identical hallucination. That strains the reported **group** appearances. → *VERIFY
   the psychiatric-literature citations for "hallucinations are not collective" (DSM criteria / clinical
   studies the article names).* Scriptural targets: **1 Cor 15:5–8** (Cephas, the Twelve, the 500, James,
   Paul).
3. **Conversion disorder and bereavement hallucinations have specific clinical profiles that don't fit.**
   The varied, repeated, multi-person, multi-setting experiences — and the **transformed skeptics
   (Paul, James)** who were not grieving followers — fall outside those diagnostic patterns. → *VERIFY the
   specific grief-hallucination / conversion-disorder studies cited and their criteria.*
4. **Methodological point.** The hallucination theories are "primarily proposed by non-medical writers,"
   and the medical analysis in them is "generally flawed and often absent" — so a clinician's review is
   warranted (this is the article's own framing; quote the *idea* in our words, cite the primaries it uses).
