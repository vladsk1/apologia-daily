## Argument map (in our own words)

1. **The question is badly posed.** "Was Jesus God?" assumes we already know what "God" means. Wright's undergraduates
   disbelieved a "spy in the sky"; the God of Israel is a different God. Start from him, not from Deism.
2. **Three assumptions block the inquiry:** no sane person could think himself divine; no first-century Jew could; and
   high Christology is a later power play (Constantine, emperors divinising themselves). Wright names Dunn
   (*Christology in the Making*), Harvey (1980 Bamptons), Vermes and Borg as examples of the first two.
3. **First-century Jewish monotheism** was creational and covenantal, a confession of YHWH against the idols rather
   than a numerical analysis of God's being. Judaism spoke of God's action in the world through Spirit, Word, Torah,
   Presence/Glory and Wisdom.
4. **The early, Jewish, high Christology:** John 1 (Word/Wisdom/"tabernacled"), Hebrews 1 (Wisdom 7), 1 Cor 8:6 (the
   Shema rewritten, "possibly the single most revolutionary" formula), Phil 2 (Isaiah 45; and Jesus, not Caesar, as
   lord), Col 1:15–20 and 2:9 ("bodily"), Gal 4:4–6 (God sends the Son and the Spirit of the Son). "Son of God" is
   messianic in Jewish usage but in Paul says more: the Son's death reveals God's love (Rom 5:6–10) only because the
   Son is not someone else.
5. **The resurrection does not by itself prove divinity.** A raised brigand would be a marvel, not a god. Resurrection
   pointed to messiahship, messiahship to the task of the cross, and that task to the God who had promised to do it
   himself.
6. **Messiahship is not divinity.** Other claimants did not think themselves divine. The case for Jesus' own
   self-understanding runs instead through the kingdom: he believed he was embodying YHWH's long-awaited return to
   Zion, acting it out in his entry to Jerusalem and his Temple action, and explaining it in parables and God-images
   (the shepherd, the father, the son sent last). No single item is conclusive; the sayings are cryptic and come into
   focus around the public symbolic actions.
7. **"Vocation", not "knowing he was God".** Wright's category: Jesus believed he must do and be what Scripture says
   only YHWH does and is. He warns that a Jesus who simply knew his divinity is Apollinarian.
8. **The church did not read this back.** The early church used other idioms for Jesus; the overlap is partial, the
   discontinuity substantial.
9. **The identity of God, re-centred.** Wright's closing argues that the earthly Jesus should reshape what "God" means,
   with a sharp critique of a detached, "kyriarchical" Western picture of God and of the usual kenotic question.

**Is the source better than our essays anywhere? Yes, in four places.**
1. Its **central thesis** (YHWH's return to Zion, enacted in Jesus' entry and Temple action) is the engine of the
   implicit-claims argument, and our main essay has only a compressed Temple stand-in (WIG-1).
2. **Romans 5:** a Pauline argument from an undisputed letter that an agent or adopted man dying could not be "God shows
   his love" (WIG-2).
3. **The raised brigand:** the concrete form of our abstract "a confirmation needs something to confirm" (WIG-3).
4. **The discontinuity premise** against "the church read it back" (WIG-4).

**Where ours is better (⑤):** Daniel 7, 1 Cor 8:6 (we state McGrath's dissent), Rom 1:3–4, Colossians' authorship, the
Dunn characterisation, the Mark 13:32 clarifier, and everything touching Chalcedon and the doctrine of God.
