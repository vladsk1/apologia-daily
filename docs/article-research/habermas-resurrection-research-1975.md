## Thesis (our words)
A **survey of the scholarly landscape**: Habermas tracks **1,400+ academic publications** on Jesus' death,
burial, and resurrection (1975–2005, in German/French/English, 100+ subtopics) and reports where critical
scholars — conservative through skeptical — actually land. He finds an **~3:1 ratio of "moderate
conservative" to skeptical** treatments (works holding that something happened *to Jesus*, bodily or as some
"spiritual body," vs. only *to the disciples*), and argues the **single most crucial development** is the
near-unanimous concession that **the disciples had real experiences they believed were of the risen Jesus**
(NOT that the resurrection occurred — the bounded concession). **(Scope: a lit-review documenting consensus,
not a proof; and it is the survey *behind* the minimal-facts method our pages already use.)**

## Argument map (the survey's structure → the primaries/scholars to VERIFY in the full text)

**A · General tendencies.** Raymond Brown: the most popular christological view is "moderate conservatism";
the same holds for resurrection studies. Grouped by region/language (all names are *leads to confirm against
the article + then the primaries*):
- **German** — critical: Hans Conzelmann, Willi Marxsen, Gerd Lüdemann, Ingo Broer, early Rudolf Pesch;
  moderate/conservative: Wolfhart Pannenberg, Jürgen Moltmann, Martin Hengel, Jacob Kremer, Walter Künneth,
  Ulrich Wilckens. **French** — Francis Durrwell, Xavier Léon-Dufour, Jean-Marie Guillaume. **British** —
  independent: Michael Goulder, G. A. Wells, Duncan Derrett; moderate: Thomas Torrance, James D. G. Dunn,
  Richard Swinburne, Oliver O'Donovan, N. T. Wright. **North American** — skeptical: J. D. Crossan, Marcus
  Borg; moderate: Reginald Fuller, Pheme Perkins, Raymond Brown; conservative: William Lane Craig, Stephen
  Davis. Habermas reports an **~3:1 moderate-conservative : skeptical** ratio (a "barometer," proves nothing
  about whether it *happened*).

**B · Six specific research trends.**
1. A **limited resurgence of naturalistic theories** (~a dozen theses, 40+ scholars) — but the vast majority
   still reject them.
2. The **empty tomb**: Habermas compiles **23 arguments for / 14 against**; ~**75% of commenting critical
   scholars** favor historicity. The most popular pro-argument = **the women as first witnesses** (embarrassment
   / would not be invented). → *Primaries/leads:* the four Gospel tomb narratives; the women-witness argument.
3. **Paul is the most critically-respected witness** — Norman Perrin: "the one witness we have whom we can
   interrogate"; **1 Cor 15:3–8** the strongest evidence, predating any NT book; Howard Clark Kee (can be
   examined "as… in a modern court"); **Murphy-O'Connor** ("complete agreement" that Paul introduces a quotation
   at v. 3b). Paul received it from **Peter & James** in Jerusalem within a few years; the vast majority date the
   *reception* to the **mid-30s AD**; **Walter Kasper** ("perhaps in use by the end of 30 AD"); **Ulrich
   Wilckens** ("the oldest phase of all"). → *Primaries:* **1 Cor 15:3–8; Gal 1:18–19; Gal 2:1–10.**
4. **Other early creeds** beyond 1 Cor 15 — the **Acts sermon-confessions**; Gerald O'Collins ("resurrection
   formulae which stem from the thirties"); John Drane ("the earliest evidence… the early sermons in Acts"). →
   *Primaries:* the Acts kerygma passages.
5. **The "spiritual body"** — a (perhaps) majority now read Paul's *sōma pneumatikon* as a **transformed/bodily**
   resurrection: even **Lüdemann** ("I do not question the physical nature of Jesus' appearance… a transformed
   physical body," cf. 1 Cor 15:35–49); **Wright** ("Paul is a firm believer in bodily resurrection"). →
   *Primary:* **1 Cor 15:35–49**; Wright, *RSG* on *anastasis/egeirō*.
6. **Resurrection → theology**: the vast majority hold the event evidences/leads to Christian doctrine (even
   skeptics like Marxsen and Marcus Borg find "meaning," while denying/relativizing the event).

**C · A comparison of scholars — Crossan vs. Wright** (the poles): they **agree** that Paul is the best early
eyewitness, that 1 Cor 15:3–7 is an early creed Paul received decades before writing, that the *earliest
teaching was bodily* resurrection (Crossan: "already thinking along these same lines"; Crossan & Reed
"bracket" the Acts light-vision and imagine Paul seeing/hearing the risen Jesus), and that resurrection faith
has this-world ethical outworkings. They **differ** on the empty tomb (Wright: historical, a "pillar";
Crossan: Markan creation) and, decisively, on whether Jesus was **actually raised** — Crossan holds it a
*metaphor* and rejects the literal event, partly because he **disbelieves an afterlife** (no category for it).

**D · ⭐ The single most crucial development — the disciples' experiences (the payoff + the bounded-concession
guardrail).** Near-unanimous critical consensus: after Jesus' death his followers **had real experiences they
believed were appearances of the risen Jesus** — *however explained*. This is a **fact "upon which both
believer and unbeliever may agree"** — NOT a concession that the resurrection occurred. Exact concession
sources to verify:
- **Reginald Fuller** — "even the most skeptical historian has to postulate an 'x'"; the disciples' belief is
  "one of the indisputable facts of history"; the "irreducible historical minimum."
- **E. P. Sanders**, *The Historical Figure of Jesus* — the disciples "saw him" is among facts "almost beyond
  dispute"; "That Jesus' followers (and later Paul) had resurrection experiences is, in my judgment, a fact.
  What the reality was… I do not know."
- **Robert Funk** — "The conviction that Jesus was no longer dead… began as a series of visions"; takes 1 Cor
  15:3–7 seriously.
- **John P. Meier** — "the claim by some of his disciples that he had risen… and appeared to them" = an
  "empirically verifiable historical claim"; Paul an eyewitness; James in the pre-Pauline list.
- **James D. G. Dunn** — "almost impossible to dispute that at the historical roots of Christianity lie some
  visionary experiences… appearances of Jesus, raised by God"; "something had happened to *Jesus himself*."
- **N. T. Wright** — the transformation/persecution-willingness that the experience must explain.
- **Bart Ehrman** — "it is a historical fact that some of Jesus' followers came to believe that he had been
  raised from the dead soon after his execution."
- **Helmut Koester** — "that Jesus appeared to others (Peter, Mary Magdalene, James) cannot very well be
  questioned."
- The convergent paths (all "critically-ascertained texts" + criteria of eyewitness testimony / early reports
  / multiple attestation / discontinuity / embarrassment / enemy declarations / coherence): **1 Cor 9:1; 15:8**
  (Paul's own eyewitness), **1 Cor 15:3–7** (30s creed), **Gal 2:1–10** (the Jerusalem meeting), **1 Cor
  15:9–15, esp. 15:11** (Paul knew the others' teaching), the Acts confessions, James's conversion, the
  transformed lives, the Gospels, and (most scholars) the empty tomb.

## Guardrail fit (read before pulling)
- **Resurrection rule:** lead with the early creed; this survey *documents* the consensus, it does not by
  itself prove the resurrection.
- **Bounded concession (the guardrails' exact watch-point):** "even skeptics grant the disciples had
  *experiences*" — keep it to *experiences/visions*, **never** "skeptics grant the resurrection." Crossan/
  Sanders/Funk/Ehrman explicitly deny or suspend the event while granting the belief. Cite them at that bound.
- **No inflation of "consensus":** report what the article claims (e.g. "~75%" on the empty tomb; "~3:1" ratio;
  "near-unanimous" on the experiences), attributed — don't upgrade a majority to unanimity.
- **Cite the primaries, not Habermas's survey:** the survey points to creeds/texts/named scholars — we cite
  *those*, verified, never "Habermas's survey says scholars agree."
