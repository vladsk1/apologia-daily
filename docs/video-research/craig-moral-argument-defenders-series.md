## Thesis
The moral argument — *(1) if God does not exist, objective moral values and duties do not exist; (2) objective
moral values and duties do exist; (3) therefore God exists* — is logically valid, and each premise is more
plausible than its denial. Craig defends P1 by arguing naturalism has no plausible ground for either the
**value** (worth) of persons or their moral **duties**; he resolves the **Euthyphro dilemma** by grounding
value in God's *nature* and duty in God's *commands* (which flow from that nature); and he defends P2 as a
deliverance of **moral experience** that is *properly basic*, rebutting the evolutionary-debunking objection.
Throughout he insists the argument is about moral **ontology** (is there a ground?), NOT moral **epistemology**
(how we know) and NOT whether atheists can live good lives — a distinction our guardrails already require.

## Argument map (our own words → primaries to verify)

**1 · The argument + Premise 1 (video 1).**
- Two key distinctions: **values** (good/bad, worth) vs. **duties** (right/wrong, obligation); and **objective**
  (independent of opinion) vs. **subjective**. Explicitly NOT absolute-vs-relative. The Holocaust example
  illustrates *objective* (wrong even if the Nazis had won and brainwashed everyone), not proof of P2.
- P1 defense: on **naturalism**, science is morally neutral ("no moral values in a test tube"); humans are
  accidental byproducts of evolution, so there's no ground for their special moral **worth**; and with no
  moral lawgiver there's no ground for moral **duty** (a lion doesn't *murder* the zebra). Leads:
  **Richard Dawkins**, the *River Out of Eden* (1995) line "no design, no purpose, no evil and no good,
  nothing but blind, pitiless indifference" (⚠ the separate "we are machines for propagating DNA" motif is
  NOT part of that quote — it's a distinct Dawkins line; source it on its own, don't bundle the two); **Charles Darwin**, *The Descent of Man*
  (the hive-bees "sacred duty to kill" passage); the concept of *speciesism* (⚠ term coined by **Richard Ryder**, 1970; popularized by **Peter Singer**, *Animal Liberation* — not named in the lecture).
- The careful frame (matches CLAUDE.md's morality rule): the question is NOT "must you *believe in* God to be
  good" (no) or "can you *know* duties without God" (yes) or "can you build a secular ethical code" (yes) — it
  is whether objective morality has a *ground* if God does not exist. The **Paul Kurtz** debate ("Goodness
  without God is good enough," Franklin & Marshall) is the cautionary example of conflating these.

**2 · Ontology vs. epistemology + the Euthyphro dilemma (video 2).**
- The argument is about moral **ontology**; Craig is open to *any* moral **epistemology** (conscience, rational
  intuition, divine revelation, the law "written on the heart," Rom 2:14–15). Don't let the conversation slide
  into "how do we *know*."
- **Euthyphro dilemma** (from Plato's *Euthyphro*): is X good because God wills it (→ arbitrary), or does God
  will X because it's good (→ good is independent of God)? Craig's answer: a **false dilemma** — third option,
  *God wills X because He is good*. God's own nature is the standard of goodness (the "high-fidelity recording"
  / **C. S. Lewis's** "picture/idea of New York" analogy from *Mere Christianity*, Bk. II — ⚠ Lewis's word is
  "idea/picture," not "painting"); His commands express that nature,
  so duties aren't arbitrary. **Values grounded in God's nature; duties in God's will.** Defended by
  **Robert Adams**, **William Alston**, **Philip Quinn**. Voluntarism (one horn — some Reformed writers, e.g.
  *Gordon Clark*) is rejected via the **modal intuition** that some things are *necessarily* wrong (true in all
  possible worlds), so they can't rest on mere will.
- A **Trinitarian** aside: God is essentially *loving* because Father/Son/Spirit love eternally; a lone
  ("Unitarian") God couldn't exemplify love without creatures — Craig floats this as a plausibility argument
  for the Trinity. **⚠ Handle with care:** if ever used, this is *our* doctrine so not a neutrality issue per se,
  but the "a Muslim's God couldn't be loving" move must obey the Islam/charity guardrails — steelman, don't
  score cheap points; better framed as "love is grounded more deeply on a Trinitarian view" than as an attack.

**3 · Moral Platonism, Taylor, humanism (video 3).**
- **Atheistic moral Platonism** (moral values like Justice exist on their own, ungrounded). Craig's 3 replies:
  (i) unintelligible — Justice itself isn't *just*; values are properties of *persons*; (ii) gives no ground for
  moral **duty** (no lawgiver); (iii) wildly improbable that blind evolution yields creatures matching an
  independent, causally-disconnected abstract moral realm ("as if the moral realm knew we were coming"). Lead:
  **Erik Wielenberg** holds a version of this (non-natural moral realism) — *already the essay's "strongest
  objection" section*.
- **Richard Taylor** — an Aristotelian *virtue-as-skill* route (no right/wrong without a lawgiver, but virtue
  as a developed skill). The **Craig–Taylor debate**. Craig's reply: on atheism it's unclear why these count as
  *virtues* at all; a *theistic* virtue ethics fares better.
- **"Stubborn humanism"** (**Sam Harris** — *The Moral Landscape*: human flourishing as the ground). Craig:
  flourishing is an *arbitrary, premature* stopping point on naturalism (why *human* flourishing over ants'?),
  and the **supervenience** move (moral properties supervene on natural states, as wetness on H₂O) is a
  "shopping-list" assertion with no explanation. The **Walter Sinnott-Armstrong** debate ("it simply *is*
  wrong" ≠ a ground). Theism supplies a non-arbitrary stopping point: the greatest conceivable being, the Good itself.

**4 · Premise 2 + the evolutionary-debunking objection (video 4).**
- P2 is affirmed by most philosophers (Craig notes faculty are *more* objectivist than students). It is a
  deliverance of **moral experience**, held as a **properly basic belief** (Reformed epistemology, **Alvin
  Plantinga**) — like trusting our senses about the external world "unless and until" we have an overriding
  *defeater*. Fallible (bent stick, mirage) but reliable; moral progress presupposes an objective realm to
  progress toward. Illustrative cases: *sati*, foot-binding, the Crusades/Inquisition, abuse. Key lead: the
  atheist philosopher **Louise Antony** (the **Craig–Antony debate**): *any* argument for moral skepticism
  rests on premises less obvious than the reality of objective moral values — so P2 is safer than its denial.
- The **evolutionary-debunking** objection (our moral beliefs evolved for survival, not truth): as an attack on
  the *truth* of P2 it commits the **genetic fallacy** (origin ≠ truth). As an attack on *justification*, three
  replies: (i) there is in fact **no adequate evolutionary account of moral beliefs** — lead: the biologist
  **Jeffrey Schloss** (survey + the altruism-toward-non-kin gap); (ii) it **begs the question** (only *on
  atheism* are evolved beliefs truth-*independent*; if God guides the process they'd be reliable); (iii) it is
  **self-defeating** — it debunks *all* beliefs incl. naturalism (**Plantinga's Evolutionary Argument Against
  Naturalism**, EAAN).
- The subjective/objective confusion: my *experience* is subjective, but its *object* can be objective (pain,
  the external world, the round earth). ⚠ A student comment invokes **Ravi Zacharias** — **do NOT cite
  Zacharias** in any of our content (posthumous, substantiated abuse findings); noted only to flag it.

**5 · Cross-cultural relativism, the conclusion, the "weak arguments" reply (video 5).**
- Cultural diversity doesn't defeat objectivity: anthropologists report striking *commonality* of core codes;
  apparent differences are usually differences of *application* (a cannibal tribe still holds "love your
  neighbor" — they just don't count outsiders as neighbors; modesty is universal in value, variable in
  expression) or of *fallible perception* under sin. **Intentionality** ("aboutness," Aquinas/Scholastics):
  moral experience is *of* something. **Schloss** again on the unexplained evolution of altruism.
- **Conclusion:** God exists — and the moral argument uniquely tells us about the **moral nature** of the
  Creator (a personal, necessary, perfectly good being whose nature is the standard and whose commands are our
  duties). Craig calls it perhaps the **most effective** argument (though kalam is his favorite) because it
  meets people where they live. "Can we be good without God?" — not *ultimately*, though atheists can behave well.
- The **Kevin Scharp** critique (Ohio State debate, "is there evidence for God?"): the arguments are "weak" (don't
  inspire enough confidence). Craig's reply: "more plausible than not" is a *minimum floor*, not a claim his
  premises are merely 51%; and in a **deductive** argument the conclusion is *at least* as probable as the
  premises. Plus the **cumulative case** (lead: **Timothy McGrew**): many independent arguments, each raising the
  probability, sum to a high combined probability — like convergent evidence in a courtroom.

  fallible, and presents the cumulative case honestly. Low overstatement — a good template.
