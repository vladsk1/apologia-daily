## Thesis (our words)
The Bible's messy, multi-source, multi-century history should **increase**, not erode, our confidence in it:
when you actually sift the evidence for *which books the Jews and the early church received as Scripture*,
a stable core emerges. Framed against a skeptic foil — **Kristin Swenson** `[AUTO: "Kristen Swinson /
Swanson"]`, associate professor of religious studies, Virginia Commonwealth University, who argues that
because we have "copies of copies, not all of which agree," belief is unwarranted — the lecture argues the
opposite: the reception history yields, "at minimum," a shared **66-book core** that Jews and Christians
across the ages recognized, with the disputed books (the Apocrypha / deuterocanon) as a genuine but
*marginal* debate. **(Scope: this establishes *recognition/reception*, not inspiration or truth — the same
limit `canon.html` states; and it is OT-canon only.)**

## Argument map (the lecture's spine → the primaries/scholars to VERIFY)

**Definitions.** *Canon* — from Greek *kanōn*, a measuring rod / rule / list. *Apocrypha* — used two ways
that must be kept apart: (a) the **OT "Apocrypha" proper** = the deuterocanonical books (Tobit, Judith,
Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch, 1–2 Maccabees, additions to Daniel/Esther); (b) **"apocryphal" NT literature** =
the later Gnostic/spurious gospels (Thomas, Mary, Judas). Athanasius uses "apocryphal" in sense (b).

**A · The pre-Christian & first-century JEWISH witnesses to a settled OT canon** (the richest lead-harvest
here — and, unlike the deuterocanon *verdict*, this is neutral shared-tradition history):
- **Josephus, *Against Apion* 1.37–43** — "we do not have myriads of books… but only **22**"; these are the
  books "justly accredited" and "laid up in the temple"; a closed span from **Moses to Artaxerxes**, with
  nothing after Artaxerxes counted as of equal authority. (The 22 = the same books as the Protestant 39,
  counted by combining pairs — Judges+Ruth, Jeremiah+Lamentations, Ezra+Nehemiah, the 12 minor prophets as
  one, etc.) → *Primary: Josephus, Ag. Apion 1.37–43.*
- **4 Ezra 14** `[AUTO: "for Ezra"]` **(= 2 Esdras 14:44–48)** — make public the **24** books, keep back the
  **70** for the wise: a canonical 24 distinguished from 70 "esoteric" writings. → *Primary: 4 Ezra / 2 Esdras
  14:44–48.*
- **Sirach, Prologue** `[AUTO: "Sierak"]` (2nd c. BC; the translator's grandfather **Jesus ben Sira**) —
  the tripartite reference "**the Law and the Prophets and the other books of our ancestors**," an early
  witness to the Law/Prophets/Writings shape later called the **Tanakh** (Torah / Nevi'im / Ketuvim). →
  *Primary: Prologue to Sirach (Ecclesiasticus).*
- **1QS, the Community Rule** (Dead Sea Scrolls / Qumran `[AUTO: "Kuman"]`, the Essenes `[AUTO: "Essins"]`)
  — a **bipartite** authority formula: what was commanded "by the hand of **Moses** and… his servants the
  **prophets**." → *Primary: 1QS (Rule of the Community).* ⚠ The lecture's own caveat: the Dead Sea Scrolls
  are a *library*, not one group's canon — don't over-read "it's in the Scrolls." Distinct from the DSS as
  *textual* witnesses (that's the later lecture).
- **Philo of Alexandria** `[AUTO: "Pho"]` — quotes the **Torah** thousands of times and **never once quotes
  the Apocrypha/deuterocanon**. (Presented as *measured* corroboration, not proof.) → *Primary: Philo's
  corpus / citation pattern.*
- **The Qumran contents** — the DSS contain **every OT book except Esther (and possibly Nehemiah)**, plus
  non-canonical works (Jubilees, 1 Enoch, the Community Rule). Genesis, notably, is *present in many copies*
  but is *never quoted with a "as it is written / as Scripture says" formula* — yet no one disputes Genesis's
  canonicity, which is the point: **an argument from silence proves nothing.** Illustration (a framing device,
  not a source): **Marco Polo never mentions the Great Wall of China**, yet it existed — "absence of evidence
  isn't evidence of absence." Esther's absence is likely explained instead by its being *disputed in ancient
  Judaism because it never names God* (the only Hebrew Bible book without the divine name).

**B · The early-CHRISTIAN witnesses** (patristic; shared tradition):
- The **New Testament's own use of the OT** — the earliest Christians were Jews; the "13 most-quoted" OT
  books show up as Scripture. Cited cross-references worth verifying: **Hebrews 11:35** appears to allude to
  the **2 Maccabees 6–7** martyrdoms; **Jude 14–15** quotes **1 Enoch 1:9** by name; **Jeremiah 31:31** (the
  "new covenant"). ⚠ *Careful, neutral handling:* an *allusion* to 2 Macc is not a canonization of it (the
  lecture doesn't claim it is) — do not let this tip into either "the NT canonizes the Apocrypha" or "the NT
  rejects it." → *Primaries: Heb 11:35 / 2 Macc 6–7; Jude 14–15 / 1 Enoch 1:9; Jer 31:31.*
- **Melito of Sardis** `[AUTO: "Alito of Sardis / Milo"]` (2nd c.) — **coined the term "Old Testament,"** and
  gives the **earliest surviving Christian OT canon list**, which contains the Hebrew books and **omits
  Esther**. (List preserved by **Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History* 4.26**.) → *Primary: Melito's list via
  Eusebius HE 4.26.*
- **Athanasius, 39th Festal Letter (Easter, AD 367)** — the canonical books are "the **springs of
  salvation**"; other books are "**to be read**" but "not canonized"; and a *third* category (the NT-apocryphal
  gospels) is rejected outright — a **three-fold scheme: canonical / readable-but-not-doctrinal / apocryphal.**
  → *Primary: Athanasius, 39th Festal Letter* (already fn10 in `canon.html`).
- **Gregory of Nazianzus** (4th c.) — defends **22 books, matched to the 22 letters of the Hebrew alphabet**
  (the numerological reason the "22" keeps recurring). → *Primary: Gregory Naz.'s canon poem.*
- **Paul, Romans 3:1–2** — the Jews were "**entrusted with the oracles of God**" (the lecture's warrant for
  weighing the *Jewish* reception). **2 Peter 1:21** — men "carried along… by the Holy Spirit" (the
  theological side of canon = recognition of inspired authority). → *Primaries: Rom 3:1–2; 2 Pet 1:21.*

**C · The AUGUSTINE vs JEROME axis (the crux of the denominational divide — mine the history, NOT the
verdict).** Two ancient Christian instincts, *both* with real precedent:
- **Jerome** — distinguished the **Hebrew canon** from the rest; called Wisdom, Sirach, Judith, Tobit, and
  1–2 Maccabees "**apocrypha**," to be "**read for the edification of the people, not for the authoritative
  confirmation of ecclesiastical doctrine**" (preface to the books of Solomon; the *Prologus Galeatus*);
  translated the **Latin Vulgate**; reportedly consulted **rabbis in Jerusalem** on what the Jews received;
  yet he *did* render the disputed books into the Vulgate (the lecture says "at least coerced to some degree"
  by the bishop of Rome). → *Primaries: Jerome's Vulgate prefaces / Prologus Galeatus* (already fn16/fn19 in
  `canon.html`).
- **Augustine** — weighted **catholicity / wide church reception** (Greek *kata holos*, "concerning the
  whole" = universal): if more of the church than not received Maccabees as Scripture, the church recognizes
  it. This is the reception-history route to the **fuller** canon. (Historically ratified regionally at the
  **Synod of Hippo 393 / Council of Carthage 397**.) → *Primaries: Augustine, De Doctrina Christiana 2.8;
  Hippo/Carthage* (Carthage already fn11 in `canon.html`).
- **The lecture's neutral hinge (usable):** whether Augustine or Jerome is "right" **turns on your doctrine
  of the church's authority** — if the church has an infallible right to *define* the canon, Augustine's
  reception route follows; if canon is *recognition* of what was given, Jerome's antiquity route follows.
  That framing (the disagreement is *principled on both sides*) is exactly our neutral posture. ⚠ *Do not
  carry over the speaker's own tie-break.*

**D · The Reformation-era history (context; carry the same neutrality).**
- **Complutensian Polyglot** `[AUTO: "competentium polygot"]` (compiled under **Cardinal Francisco Jiménez de
  Cisneros** `[AUTO: unnamed — "the cardinal responsible for the Spanish Inquisition"]`, ~1514–1517) — its
  preface says the disputed books are "outside the canon," received "**more for the education of the people
  than for… authority.**" → *Primary: Complutensian Polyglot preface.*
- **Cardinal Cajetan** `[AUTO: "Kayatan"]` (1532) — the deuterocanon is "not canonical… for establishing
  matters of faith," though "canonical" for edification. → *Primary: Cajetan's commentary preface.*
- **Luther** — at the **Leipzig Disputation (1519)** `[AUTO: "Leipig"]` argued purgatory's only proof-text is
  **2 Maccabees**, which, "since it is not in the canon," does not bind; his **German Bible (1534)** places
  the Apocrypha in a *separate section* headed "**not equal to Holy Scripture, but… useful and good to read.**"
  The intro montage's provocative Luther line — "I want to make Moses so German that no one would suspect he
  was a Jew" — is a *translation-philosophy* remark; ⚠ verify wording + context, handle carefully (the speaker
  flags it as "controversial"). → *Primaries: Leipzig Disputation record; Luther's 1534 Bible preface.*
- **English Bibles** — the **1611 KJV**, and before it the **Geneva, Bishops', Great, and Matthew/Tyndale**
  Bibles, all printed the **Apocrypha as a section between the Testaments**; Protestants only *dropped* the
  section later (early 18th c.). (A useful neutral corrective to "Protestants deleted books.") → *Primary:
  1611 KJV front matter.*
- **Council of Trent, Session 4 (8 April 1546)** — declared the deuterocanon "**sacred and canonical**… with
  all their parts," attaching an **anathema**. The lecture's careful point: earlier councils (e.g.
  **Florence, 1442**) had *listed* 73 books **without** an anathema, and Vatican-released Trent records show
  participants `[AUTO: "Bonuccio"]` uneasy that an anathema was binding consciences on "questions long disputed
  among reputable theologians." → *Primaries: Trent Session 4 decree* (already fn17/fn20 in `canon.html`);
  *Florence 1442; the Trent acta.* ⚠ Present Trent's action *historically*, never polemically.

**E · Conclusion (the neutral, usable payoff).** After all the data is sifted, what stands is **a shared
66-book core** that Jews and Christians across the ages recognized as Scripture — "with discussions about how
we deal with the other literature." That *core* + *the fact that the remaining dispute is confined to the OT
margins* is the neutral, mission-usable conclusion (and it is the exact conclusion `canon.html` already
draws). The speaker's *personal* Protestant tie-break is **not** part of what we mine.
