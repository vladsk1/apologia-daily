The Argument from the Burial

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and stay clear and calm in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

The argument from the burial defends an unglamorous but load-bearing fact: that Jesus was buried by Joseph of Arimathea, a named member of the Sanhedrin. The large majority of historians — including many well outside Christian commitments — hold that Jesus was buried; that the tomb was known and locatable is better supported than its denials, but it is the genuinely debated point. The burial is named in the very early creed Paul cites in 1 Corinthians 15:3–7 — "he was buried" — which most scholars, including sceptics, date to within roughly two to five years of the crucifixion, far too early to be late legend. It is also reported in Mark's plain, undecorated account; every surviving line of tradition has him buried, the variation being over who did it, never whether it happened.

What makes the detail resist invention is its embarrassment. The early church blamed the Sanhedrin for Jesus' death, yet credited a member of that very council with his burial while the disciples were in hiding. A community inventing its story would not hand the act of devotion to its enemies and the shame to its heroes. The burial also fits known first-century Jewish practice and the archaeology of rock-cut tombs.

The honest scope: establishing the burial does not by itself prove the resurrection. If the tomb was known and locatable — the genuinely debated step, better supported than its denials — the empty-tomb claim becomes publicly checkable. It is the foundation other resurrection arguments build on. The resurrection is an inference drawn from the empty tomb, the appearances, and the transformation of the witnesses taken together — the inference this library defends elsewhere, that Jesus rose bodily from the dead.

The argument, formally

The unglamorous premise the whole case rests on.

P1

Jesus was buried by Joseph of Arimathea, a named member of the Sanhedrin — the early creed has he was buried; all four Gospels name Joseph, resting on Mark’s early source and on John, which shows little sign of literary dependence. Every surviving line of tradition has Jesus buried; the variations concern who did it — Acts 13:29 credits the Jerusalem authorities generically — never whether it happened.

P2

A burial by a named member of the condemning court is embarrassing and checkable — a probabilistic criterion, not a proof.

∴ C

Therefore Jesus was buried — held by the large majority of historians; that the tomb was known and locatable is better supported than its denials, but it is the genuinely debated point.

Quietly, the empty-tomb argument depends here: a known tomb makes the empty tomb checkable. Whether the tomb was known and locatable is the genuinely debated point — master this and you hold the gate.

Then why the detail resists invention

The early church blamed the Sanhedrin for the death of Jesus — then credited a Sanhedrist with his burial while the disciples hid. No community inventing freely writes its enemies the act of devotion and its heroes the shame. Fiction flatters; this does not.

Defending Premise 1

Jesus was buried by Joseph of Arimathea, a named member of the Sanhedrin — the early creed has he was buried; all four Gospels name Joseph, resting on Mark’s early source and on John, which shows little sign of literary dependence. Every surviving line of tradition has Jesus buried; the variations concern who did it — Acts 13:29 credits the Jerusalem authorities generically — never whether it happened.

Precision check: the conclusion establishes a location and a person, not a miracle. Two claims should be kept distinct. That Jesus was buried is held by most critical scholars; on that bare fact, most sceptics and believers agree. That the tomb was known and locatable — the further step the empty-tomb argument needs — is, in our judgment, better supported than its denials, but it is the genuinely debated point, and it should be defended as such rather than smuggled in under the consensus that covers the burial itself.

The creed Strong

The earliest reference to Jesus’ burial is not in any Gospel. It sits in the pre-Pauline creed that Paul quotes in 1 Corinthians 15, a formula most scholars date to within a few years of the crucifixion: “that Christ died for our sins… that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day… and that he appeared” (1 Corinthians 15:3–5). Burial is one of the four load-bearing clauses of the oldest Christian confession we possess.

Honest limit: the creed does not name Joseph or describe a tomb, but it establishes that “he was buried” was bedrock from the start — not a later embellishment but part of the original proclamation, set there to underline the reality of the death it followed.

The Gospels — Mark’s early source, and John Strong

All four Gospels record the burial by Joseph of Arimathea (Mark 15:42–47; Matthew 27:57–60; Luke 23:50–54; John 19:38–42). Mark’s account is widely regarded as resting on an early, pre-Markan passion source — a connected narrative of Jesus’ final days that circulated before Mark wove it into his Gospel. John’s burial account shows little sign of literary dependence on the Synoptics, which means the burial is not a single story copied three times but a memory surfacing in independent channels.

It is the convergence that carries the weight, not any one line of it. The creed gives antiquity; Mark gives an early connected narrative; the four-Gospel spread gives independence. Take any single strand away and the others still stand.

Honest limit: the German scholar Rudolf Pesch argued for an especially early passion tradition behind Mark; while his specific dating is debated, the broader point that Mark drew on a pre-existing passion narrative is widely held.

A burial was expected, not exceptional Strong · for Jerusalem, not the empire

Jewish law made same-day burial of the executed a religious imperative. Deuteronomy commands that the body of a hanged criminal “shall not remain all night on the tree, but you shall bury him the same day… you shall not defile your land” (Deuteronomy 21:22–23). This was not a dead letter. Josephus, describing the Jewish reverence for burial, states that the Jews are so careful about these rites that even those condemned to crucifixion are taken down and buried before sunset. Burial of the executed required the governor’s permission, and the Gospels say Joseph asked Pilate for it (Mark 15:43–45); Roman law, as the jurists Ulpian and Paul preserved it, held that the bodies of the executed should not be refused to relatives who asked for them, and could be given to whoever requested them for burial, though permission could be withheld, especially in cases of treason. And in 1968, excavators at Giv’at ha-Mivtar on the northern edge of Jerusalem found an ossuary inscribed “Yehohanan,” containing a heel bone with an iron crucifixion nail still driven through it: a man crucified under Roman rule in the very city and roughly the very period of Jesus, whose bones were collected by his family, placed in an ossuary, and laid in a rock-cut tomb in the normal Jewish way.

Honest limit: what Josephus establishes is the strength of the Jewish burial norm — the religious expectation that the executed be interred the same day — not a claim that Rome invariably complied. Philo records that on festival occasions crucified bodies could be taken down and given to relatives, a clemency he mentions precisely because a brutal prefect had violated it, which itself shows the norm could be honoured or breached. The 1968 discovery was first reconstructed by the anatomist Nicu Haas; when Joseph Zias and Eliezer Sekeles re-examined the remains in 1985 they corrected several of his anatomical details — but the correction went to the reconstruction, not to the two facts that carry the weight here: the man had been crucified, and he had afterwards been given an ordinary Jewish burial.

Objection — Crossan: the crucified were left unburied Hardest

John Dominic Crossan argues that the burial story is essentially fiction — “hope and hyperbole,” not history. On his reading, Roman crucifixion was designed precisely to deny its victims burial: the body was left on the cross as a deterrent, exposed to scavenging dogs and birds, and what little remained was tossed into a shallow common grave. If Crossan is right, there was no tomb, no Joseph, and therefore nothing for an “empty tomb” to be empty of. Roman literary sources do, in fact, describe crucified bodies left for the birds, and Crossan can cite them.

Reply

This is the serious version of the objection, and it cannot be waved away. Crucifixion was meant to humiliate, and in much of the empire the denial of burial was indeed routine. The honest reply is not to deny the general Roman practice but to ask whether first-century Judaea was an exception — and the evidence that it was is considerable. Crossan’s reconstruction describes the empire at large; it does not describe Jerusalem under the constraints of Jewish law, Roman pragmatism, and the festival calendar. The Gospels’ burial is not historically anomalous. It is what the convergence of law, politics, and archaeology would lead us to expect.

Objection — McCane: a shameful burial

The archaeologist and historian Byron McCane accepts that Jesus was buried but argues that it was a dishonourable burial — not the loving interment by a devoted follower the Gospels imply, but the hasty, shameful disposal in a criminal’s tomb that Jewish law required for the executed, without mourning rites. On this reading the Gospels have softened a disgrace into a kindness.

Reply

McCane’s challenge actually strengthens the core claim. He is disputing the manner of the burial, not its reality. A shameful burial is, if anything, a more securely historical datum, because it is even less likely to be invented: a movement proclaiming its Lord’s vindication would not gratuitously fabricate his being dumped in a criminals’ plot. The load-bearing fact survives intact: Jesus was buried. That the tomb was known and locatable — the further step the empty-tomb argument needs — is better supported than its denials, but it is the genuinely debated point.

The room: Raymond Brown, in his magisterial two-volume The Death of the Messiah, judged the burial by Joseph of Arimathea historical and defended it at length. The archaeologist Jodi Magness concludes that the Gospel accounts of Jesus’ burial are largely consistent with the archaeological and literary evidence for Jewish burial practice of the period. John A. T. Robinson, the Anglican bishop whose radical theology scandalised his own church, nonetheless treated the burial of Jesus in the tomb as among the earliest and best-attested facts we possess about him (a paraphrase, as attested in the secondary literature). Crossan and a minority deny that Jesus received any known, identifiable burial, and Bart Ehrman, on independent grounds, has likewise argued that the burial tradition is doubtful; most critical scholars have not followed. McCane and others grant the burial but contest its honour. Craig A. Evans, working against Crossan, argues that the concern to bury the executed and the pressure to avoid defiling the land make an actual burial of Jesus almost certain — but whether the tomb was also known and locatable is argued, not assumed.
Defending Premise 2

A burial by a named member of the condemning court is embarrassing and checkable — a probabilistic criterion, not a proof.

Precision check: the criterion of embarrassment is a probabilistic tool, not a proof, and it has critics. The point is not that a forger could not have invented Joseph but that he had no incentive to, and strong incentive not to.

The wrong hero Strong · probabilistic

Joseph is “a respected member of the council” (Mark 15:43) — the very body the Gospels portray as having handed Jesus over to Pilate. The early Christian movement had every motive to cast the Jewish leadership as the villains of the passion; the polemic against “the chief priests and the elders” runs through the tradition. Inventing a pious Sanhedrist who does the right thing by Jesus cuts directly against that grain. It complicates the clean story of official rejection by conceding that at least one member of the condemning council acted honourably.

Raymond Brown, whose two-volume The Death of the Messiah remains the most exhaustive study of the passion narratives and who was no apologist, put the logic plainly: a Christian fictional creation of a Jewish Sanhedrist who does what is right is, given the hostility of early Christian writing toward the Jewish authorities, almost inexplicable. Embarrassing details are remembered because they happened, not because they help.

Checkable by name Corroborating

Joseph is named and placed — “Joseph of Arimathea,” tied to a specific town. Ancient invented characters tend to be vague and generic; concrete identification is the texture of memory, and a named council member is a dangerous thing to fabricate before an audience that could, in principle, ask after him.

Naming a prominent, locatable public figure invites refutation in a way anonymous legend never does.

The women at the burial Corroborating

The Gospels make women the witnesses to where Jesus was laid (Mark 15:47). In a culture where Josephus could write that the testimony of women was not to be admitted on account of “the levity and boldness of their sex,” putting women at the tomb is the last thing a fabricator polishing his case would do. Both details point the same way.

Objection — the criterion is contested, and Luke and John soften Joseph

Some scholars note that what embarrasses one community may not have embarrassed another, and that Luke and John soften Joseph into a secret disciple, which could mark a developing legend.

Reply

That caution is accurate as far as it goes. But the trajectory runs the wrong way for the sceptic: the earliest form (Mark) gives us the most awkward version — a Sanhedrist — and the later Gospels work to relieve the embarrassment, not create it. That is the signature of a hard fact being managed, not a convenient fiction being born.

The room: Raymond Brown (no apologist) on the near-inexplicability of an invented Sanhedrist hero; the criterion of embarrassment is a probabilistic tool, not a proof.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: John Dominic Crossan’s — that crucified men were left unburied, so there was no known tomb. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"Romans left crucified men on crosses" Hard

Strongest form

Crossan: crucifixion’s horror included no burial — carrion birds and dogs. The body of Jesus was thrown in a ditch; Joseph is pious fiction.

Reply

As a general imperial statement, partly true — which is why the Judean evidence matters: Josephus says Jews buried even crucified men before sunset per Deuteronomy, which establishes the strength of the Jewish burial norm, not a claim that Rome invariably complied; Philo records that on festival occasions crucified bodies could be taken down and given to relatives — a clemency he mentions because a brutal prefect had violated it; and the crucified Yehohanan was found buried in a family ossuary. Judea at Passover is where the occupying power had reason to accommodate the practice.

"Joseph of Arimathea is a literary invention" Trickier

Strongest form

A wealthy secret sympathiser appearing from nowhere to provide a convenient tomb — he reads like a plot device, and Arimathea is unlocatable.

Reply

Plot devices serve the plot; Joseph embarrasses it — a councillor of the body that handed Jesus over honours him while the heroes hide. A named council member is a dangerous thing to fabricate before an audience that could, in principle, ask after him. Many securely historical towns resist confident location; obscurity is not fiction.

"Buried, yes — but in a criminal trench, not a tomb" Hard

Strongest form

Grant Jewish burial of the crucified: the law sent criminals to common graveyards, not family tombs. A trench grave means no known tomb — and the empty-tomb story collapses.

Reply

The criminal-plot rule was for the Jewish-executed; Jesus was condemned by Rome, and the burial was an act of private piety by a councillor with the standing to obtain the body — Mark’s rock-hewn tomb fits a rich man’s ad-hoc provision (it was nearby, John notes, for time pressure). And the women observing the location is multiply attested: a known tomb — honourable or shameful — is all the empty-tomb argument requires, and whether it was known is argued, not assumed.

"Mark wrote 40 years later — too late to trust" Common

Strongest form

Four decades of retelling separate the event from our first narrative. Details like names and tombs are exactly what storytelling supplies.

Reply

The burial does not rest on Mark’s date: he was buried is in the creed Paul received — within two to five years. Forty years is also a blink by ancient standards (our best Alexander sources run centuries late), within living memory of witnesses who could correct the record. Storytelling adds glory; it does not add embarrassing councillors.

"The creed’s burial line is theology, not history" Trickier

Strongest form

Died, buried, raised mirrors liturgy — buried may simply underline really dead, with no tomb or Joseph in view.

Reply

The creed compresses; it does not float free of events — each line summarises a known narrative, and the burial it compresses is attested in every surviving line of tradition — the variation (Acts 13:29 credits the Jerusalem authorities generically) is over who buried him, never whether he was buried. Buried also does real work in the sequence: it sets up raised as bodily vacation of a grave, which is why Paul can assume an emptied tomb without arguing it.

"Pilate would never release the body" Common

Strongest form

A governor who crucified a man as a royal pretender does not hand the corpse to sympathisers for honourable burial. Politics forbids it.

Reply

Politics commends it: Passover crowds, a city primed to riot, and a Torah command to bury before sunset — leaving a Jewish body hanging into the festival was the provocation, not the release. Josephus establishes the strength of the Jewish burial norm — not a claim that Rome invariably complied — and Philo records that on festival occasions crucified bodies could be taken down and given to relatives, a clemency he mentions because a brutal prefect had violated it; and a respected councillor was the one petitioner Pilate could grant without losing face.
