John 1:1 and the Pre-existence of Christ

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and stay clear and calm in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

John opens his Gospel with three short clauses that settle who Jesus is: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God" (John 1:1). The Word already was in the beginning (eternal, not created); was with God (a distinct person, not collapsed into the Father); and was God (fully divine in nature). Verse 14 then names him: "the Word became flesh and dwelt among us" — Jesus of Nazareth.

The decisive logic comes in verse 3: "all things were made through him, and without him was not any thing made that was made." If absolutely everything created was made through the Word, then the Word himself cannot be a created thing — a creature cannot be the agent through whom “all things were made” without being made through himself. He stands on the Creator's side of the line, not the creature's. The grammar supports this: "the Word was God" (theos en ho logos) places "God" first without the article, a construction that is usually qualitative, ascribing full divine nature while keeping the persons distinct (Philip Harner's 1973 study; this is not Colwell's rule, which concerns definiteness and is often misapplied here).

Honest scope: groups such as the Jehovah's Witnesses translate the last clause "the Word was a god." But the construction marks quality, not indefiniteness, and verse 3 rules out a created Word. This passage establishes Christ's pre-existence and full deity; it presupposes rather than proves the wider Trinitarian framework, in which the Son is distinct from the Father yet one God. And the same verse that puts the Word on the Creator’s side of the line runs on to verse 14: the Word became flesh. Fully God and fully man — John asserts both, and the incarnation is the point the prologue is travelling toward.

Before you start · key terms

Eight words this argument leans on

Learn these first — the objections below turn on them, and most weak versions of the argument come from blurring one of them.

Imperfect ēn vs. aorist egenetoThe verb of John 1:1 is the imperfect ēn (“was”), not the aorist egeneto (“came to be”) that John uses of created things in verse 3 and of John the Baptist in verse 6. The Word does not come into being at the beginning; the Word already is when the beginning happens. This is the grammar of eternal pre-existence.
Pros ton theon (“with God”)The preposition pros with the accusative suggests relationship and orientation — the Word is face-to-face with God, personally distinct from the Father. Whatever John means in the third clause, he does not mean that the Word simply is the Father. There are two who can be “with” one another.
Anarthrous predicate nounIn clause three, theos (“God”) has no article and stands before the verb: kai theos ēn ho logos. Because ho logos (“the Word”) carries the article it is the subject, and the anarthrous theos is the predicate — so the sense is “and the Word was God.”
Qualitative forceAnarthrous predicate nouns preceding the verb usually carry a qualitative force — they describe the nature of the subject. In John 1:1c theos is neither definite nor indefinite but qualitative. The grammar by itself ascribes the kind — deity as to nature; it is the monotheism of the same Gospel, which knows only one divine nature and no gradations within the Godhead, that rules out any lesser or second-rank deity. To call theos qualitative is not to say the Word is “sort of” God, or divine by degree, or a step below the Father.
Colwell’s rule — and its misuseA pattern about definiteness: definite predicate nouns that precede the verb usually lack the article. It was soon misapplied in reverse — as if an anarthrous predicate before the verb must therefore be definite, proving theos means “the God.” That overreaches; the rule does not run backward. The qualitative reading comes from later, more careful work on the construction, not from this rule.
Functional, not ontological, subordinationIn his incarnate mission the Son willingly submits to the Father’s will — an economic/functional ordering of roles. Not that the Son is lesser in being, made, or later than the Father (Arianism): he is homoousios — co-equal and co-eternal, sharing one divine nature. The Father is greater in position, within the incarnate mission, not greater in deity.
Homoousios“Of one substance” — the same divine essence — with the Father: the word the Nicene fathers used to confess the Son. The grammarian’s reading of John 1:1c and the bishops’ word for the creed name the same reality; and it is that convergence, not the missing article, that finally carries the verse.
Begotten, not madeMonogenēs (“only-begotten”) is silence rather than support for a created Son: even read as “only-begotten,” the word names no beginning — and a begetting with no beginning is not a making: begotten, not made, as Nicaea confesses. That is the distinction the Nicene fathers drew in so many words.
The argument, formally

Three clauses that settle who the Word is.

P1

John 1:1-3 states the Word already was in the beginning, was with God (distinct), and was God (fully divine) — and that all things were made through him.

P2

If absolutely all created things were made through the Word, the Word himself cannot be a created thing — a creature cannot be the agent through whom “all things were made” without being made through himself.

∴ C

Therefore the Word is eternally pre-existent, personally distinct from the Father, and fully sharing the divine nature — and verse 14 names him: the Word became flesh, Jesus of Nazareth, fully God and fully man.

The argument runs on logic any reader can check: the maker of all made things is not among the made things. Everything else is grammar in support.

Then the grammar — why 'a god' does not survive scrutiny.

In Greek, 'the Word was God' (theos en ho logos) places theos first without the article — a construction that is usually qualitative, ascribing full nature while keeping the persons distinct. John is saying the Word is everything God is, without saying the Word is the Father.

Defending Premise 1

John 1:1-3 states the Word already was in the beginning, was with God (distinct), and was God (fully divine) — and that all things were made through him.

Precision check: The Word is distinct from the Father, whom clause two calls “God,” and yet the Word is God (clause three). The orthodox reading does not collapse that tension; it confesses it. Eternity, agency in creation, and incarnation are welded to a single subject. John 1:1, carefully read, establishes that this author, writing this Gospel, confesses the Word as eternally pre-existent, personally distinct from the Father, and fully sharing the divine nature — and that he sees the man Jesus as that Word enfleshed, the one the church confesses as fully God and fully man, two natures in one person. Nor is John 1:1 the entire New Testament case for Christ’s deity — it is one strand.

Clause 1 — “In the beginning was the Word” Strong

John deliberately contrasts verbs: created things came to be (egeneto); the Word simply was (en) — continuous existence before any beginning. The verb is the imperfect ēn (“was”), not the aorist egeneto (“came to be”) that John uses of created things in verse 3 and of John the Baptist in verse 6. The Word does not come into being at the beginning; the Word already is when the beginning happens. This is the grammar of eternal pre-existence.

Clause 2 — “the Word was with God” Strong

‘With God’ makes the Word distinct from the Father. The preposition pros with the accusative suggests relationship and orientation — the Word is face-to-face with God, personally distinct from the Father. Whatever John means in the third clause, he does not mean that the Word simply is the Father. There are two who can be “with” one another.

Clause 3 — “the Word was God” Strong · broad consensus on the grammar

‘Was God’ gives him the divine nature — both clauses, side by side, on purpose. In clause three, theos (“God”) has no article and stands before the verb: kai theos ēn ho logos. Because ho logos (“the Word”) carries the article it is the subject, and the anarthrous theos is the predicate — so the sense is “and the Word was God.” Philip B. Harner (1973) studied anarthrous predicate nouns preceding the verb and argued that their primary force is usually qualitative — they describe the nature of the subject. His paraphrase of John 1:1c is precise: “the logos… had the nature of theos.” His own preferred sense is that the Word “had the same nature as God” — neither the bare “a god” (which would make the Word merely one divine being among others) nor a flat “the Word was God” read as identity with the Father, but a statement that the Word fully shares the divine nature while remaining personally distinct. Daniel B. Wallace, in his standard reference grammar, reaches the same verdict: in John 1:1c theos is neither definite nor indefinite but qualitative — the Word possesses the full nature of deity that the Father possesses, while remaining personally distinct from him.

Had John written ho theos ēn ho logos (with the article on theos), he would have said the Word is identical to the Father — the heresy later called modalism. Had he written theos ēn ho logos intending the indefinite “a god,” he would have made the Word a lesser divine being — a kind of polytheism. What John actually wrote threads the needle: the Word shares the very nature of God without being the person of the Father. To call theos qualitative is not to say the Word is “sort of” God, or divine by degree, or a step below the Father. The grammar by itself ascribes the kind — deity as to nature; it is the monotheism of the same Gospel, which knows only one divine nature and no gradations within the Godhead, that rules out any lesser or second-rank deity. Harner’s reading is a grammatical judgment about the force of the clause, but it lands on the very ground the Nicene fathers took when they confessed the Son homoousios — ‘of one substance,’ the same divine essence — with the Father. The grammarian’s reading of the clause and the bishops’ word for the creed name the same reality; and it is that convergence, not the missing article, that finally carries the verse.

Honest limit: In 1933, the grammarian Ernest Cadman Colwell observed a pattern: definite predicate nouns that precede the verb usually lack the article. “Colwell’s rule” was soon misapplied in reverse — as if an anarthrous predicate before the verb must therefore be definite, proving theos means “the God.” That overreaches; Colwell’s rule does not run backward, and many scholars who love the orthodox conclusion have leaned on it too hard. Harner pairs John 1:1 with Mark 15:39 (the centurion’s “Truly this man was the Son of God”) as the same construction; the parallel is grammatical, not theological — Mark 15:39 is a centurion’s confession using the title “Son of God,” not itself a claim to full deity.

Pre-existence elsewhere in the Gospel Confirmatory · on a common reading

The Prologue is not an isolated flourish. In 8:58, Jesus says, “Truly, truly, I say to you, before Abraham was, I am,” and his hearers pick up stones — a reaction most naturally explained by a claim that provoked a charge of blasphemy, and, on a common reading, an echo of the divine “I am.” In 17:5, Jesus prays to be glorified “with the glory that I had with you before the world existed.” The Prologue’s grammar is confirmed by Jesus’s own words in the body of the Gospel.

Jewish categories, a new claim Context

Philo could call his mediating Logos theos without the article and still deny that the title properly fit it. John sets the Word outside everything that came to be, says that this Word “became flesh” (1:14), a particular man, in history, and brings his Gospel to its climax with Thomas calling him, article and all, “my Lord and my God” (20:28). The categories were Jewish; the claim was new.

Objection — “The Word was a god”

The New World Translation renders John 1:1c, “and the Word was a god.” It rests on a real feature of the Greek, it is held by sincere people, and it needs an answer, not a dismissal. Their argument: theos here lacks the article, whereas ton theon in clause two has it; Greek can use the article to mark definiteness; therefore the anarthrous theos should be read indefinitely — “a god” — preserving the distinction between the Father (ho theos) and the Word. They can point to John 1:18’s “only-begotten” language and to a theology in which the Son is God’s first and greatest creation. A well-read Witness will put it more technically: the Watchtower’s own appendix does not rest on the claim that an article-less noun is simply indefinite, but argues from anarthrous predicate nouns that precede the verb — and cites the same Harner study.

Reply

Harner will not carry the conclusion: his own preferred sense is that the Word “had the same nature as God” — not “a god,” which would make the Word merely one divine being among others. The popular form of the argument places more weight on the grammar than the grammar can bear; the technical form takes a qualitative reading and then translates it indefinitely — a further step, and one Harner’s own paraphrase does not take. The great majority of Greek scholars reject “a god” as the best reading. The mainstream grammatical reply is that the absence of the article does not by itself make a noun indefinite, and that the construction here points to quality, not indefiniteness. Bruce Metzger judged the rendering “a frightful mistranslation.” The article-less theos does mark a distinction — and it is the historic faith’s own confession: the Word is not the Father. But the distinction John draws is between two persons sharing one divine nature, not between God and a demigod. Julius Mantey, co-author of the grammar the Watchtower had cited in its support, wrote to the Society in 1974 objecting that he had been quoted out of context, and that nothing in that grammar was ever meant to imply “a god” is a permissible rendering of John 1:1. The weight of the case is carried by Harner’s construction and by the monotheistic, creation-saturated context of John 1, which is what rules “a god” out. That reading is a mistake.

One further point, stated precisely because it is narrower than polemics often claim: within John’s own Prologue theos stands without the article again at 1:6, 1:12 and 1:13, and the NWT renders every one of them “God,” not “a god.” Those are anarthrous genitives, not preverbal predicate nominatives, so they are not the identical construction. The sharper case is 1:18: the Greek text the NWT translates prints monogenēs theos — theos again without the article, though again not in the construction of 1:1c — and the NWT renders it “the only-begotten god,” lower case, in the same verse in which it has just printed “God” of the Father, where the Greek is equally without the article. The article is constant across the two; the referent is not. Witnesses reply that context — not a mechanical rule — should govern each verse; that principle is true, and it is ours too. So this point does not by itself decide the meaning of any single verse. Its only force is the limited but real one of showing that the “a god” rendering is theology-driven rather than rule-driven.

Objection — John is late: the explicitness has no parallel in Mark

Bart Ehrman argues that belief in Jesus’s divinity evolved: the earliest Christians held an “exaltation” Christology, progressively pushed back; alongside it, on his own account already present in material older than Paul’s letters, ran an incarnation Christology in which Christ is a pre-existent divine being who took human form. John, the latest Gospel, gives that second model its most developed form. Applied to this verse: John is late, so John 1:1 reports what one community confessed near the end of the first century rather than what the first Christians believed.

Reply

The first thing to say is a concession: the explicitness of John 1:1 has no exact parallel in Mark. The second is that Ehrman reads what the Prologue asserts much as we do — a Word who was with God in the beginning, through whom the universe was made, and who became flesh. He is a witness to what John wrote, not to whether John was right. So the objection leaves the exegesis of this verse standing and moves the argument onto historical ground. On that ground it does not hold: the disagreement is not really about dates — Ehrman himself finds a pre-existent Christ in material older than Paul’s letters — but about what kind of being the earliest Christians took him to be: on his account a pre-existent divine being of a lower order, an angel or something like one. Two features of the earliest Christian material are hard to place among those intermediary figures: where Jesus is put — 1 Corinthians 8:6, on a reading that is influential, arguably dominant, and genuinely contested — and worship: the earliest Christians were praying to Jesus and singing to him within the first two decades. Development there certainly was, in vocabulary and in precision. The field is more divided on this than on the grammar: the case is set out in full, including James McGrath’s contrary reading of 1 Corinthians 8:6, in The Shema and the Trinity.

The room: On the grammar, there is a broad consensus that theos in 1:1c is qualitative, not indefinite: Harner reads it qualitatively, and Wallace reaches the same verdict. The minority who defend “a god” are concentrated outside the academic mainstream. On the history, the field is more divided: the “early high Christology” school (Hurtado, Bauckham, Hengel) is strong but not unanimous, and critics such as Ehrman — and, on the historical question only, James D. G. Dunn, a trinitarian who in Christology in the Making argued that full incarnational pre-existence is clearly present only in John — represent a real dissent. The distinction worth keeping is between a verdict and a consensus; but those disagreements are downstream of this sentence rather than about it.
Defending Premise 2

If absolutely all created things were made through the Word, the Word himself cannot be a created thing — a creature cannot be the agent through whom “all things were made” without being made through himself.

Precision check: this argument runs on logic any reader can check: the maker of all made things is not among the made things. Everything else is grammar in support. In 1:3, “all things were made through him, and without him was not any thing made that was made” — the Word is on the Creator’s side of the line between Creator and creation.

Verse 3 totality Strong

‘Without him was not any thing made that was made’ — John closes every loophole; there is no category of created things that bypasses the Word.

Athanasius’s argument Strong

Athanasius pressed this against Arius: a creature cannot be the agent through whom “all things were made” (1:3) without being made through himself. The verb ēn in 1:1 — eternal “was,” not created “came to be” — rules out a beginning to the Word’s existence, which is precisely what Arianism requires. The Nicene fathers concluded that the Son is homoousios — of the same nature as the Father — and John 1:1, read with its imperfect verb and qualitative predicate, is one of the texts that drove them there.

Begotten, not made Answers 1:18

As for “only-begotten,” it is silence rather than support: monogenēs, even on the most generous reading, is silent on whether the begetting is eternal — and a begetting with no beginning is not a making. That is the distinction the Nicene fathers drew in so many words: begotten, not made. One who shares the very nature of God is not a creature.

OT background Background

In Genesis, God creates by speaking; in the Psalms, ‘by the word of the LORD the heavens were made’ (Psalm 33:6). John identifies that creative Word as a person. John did not coin Logos in a vacuum: in the Hebrew Scriptures God’s word creates and his Wisdom is personified as present at creation (Proverbs 8:22–31).

Objection — the Arian / subordinationist reading Hardest

The fourth-century presbyter Arius held that the Son, though pre-existent and exalted above all creatures, was himself created — “there was when he was not.” Modern subordinationists echo this: John 1:1 makes the Word divine in some derived sense, not the eternal God. The strongest form of the argument leans on John 14:28 (“the Father is greater than I”) and on the genuinely subordinate language of the Son’s mission throughout John.

Reply

The reply, classically, is that the Son’s subordination in John is functional (in his role and incarnation), not ontological (in his nature): in his incarnate mission the Son willingly submits to the Father’s will — an economic/functional ordering of roles — not that the Son is lesser in being, made, or later than the Father (Arianism). He is homoousios — co-equal and co-eternal, sharing one divine nature. “The Father is greater than I” is spoken by the Son who has, in Paul’s words, taken “the form of a servant,” having been “in the form of God” (Philippians 2:6–7); the Father is greater in position, within the incarnate mission, not greater in deity. So the Arian reading does not hold.

Objection — Proverbs 8, the text Arius built on

Proverbs 8:22 has Wisdom say the LORD ‘created me’ at the beginning. If the Word is Wisdom, the Word is a creature.

Reply

Proverbs is poetry, and John does not quote it — he writes “in the beginning” to echo Genesis 1, where the only actor is God. Whatever Proverbs’ poetry means, John’s prose says the Word made everything that was made.

The room: Arius and the modern subordinationists who echo him press the created-Son reading; Athanasius answered it, and the Nicene fathers concluded that the Son is homoousios — of the same nature as the Father. The grammar of this verse is not contested in the same way as the dating and development of Christology, and neither is the shape of the claim the Prologue makes: a Word who was before creation, who was with God, and who became flesh.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: the Arian reading — that the Son, though pre-existent and exalted above all creatures, was himself created. The deep dive answers it: the verb ēn rules out a beginning to the Word’s existence, and a creature cannot be the agent through whom “all things were made” without being made through himself. Take it with you, not just the replies below.

"The Greek says a god, not God" Common

Strongest form

There is no definite article before theos — so the honest translation is 'the Word was a god,' a lesser divine being. The New World Translation gets it right.

Reply

The anarthrous predicate before the verb is how Greek ascribes nature, not how it introduces a second god — and the NWT does not apply its own principle consistently: article-less theos and theou are rendered 'God' at 1:6, 1:12 and 1:13 without hesitation, and 'a god' surfaces only at 1:1c. (Two cautions, because a well-read Witness knows both. Do not call those the identical construction — they are genitives, not preverbal predicate nominatives. And do not say 'throughout the chapter': at 1:18 the NWT prints 'the only-begotten god,' lowercase.) John is also writing 'in the beginning', echoing Genesis; a second, lesser divine being sits very awkwardly there. And verse 3 closes the case: if all created things came through the Word, the Word is not in the created category — lesser gods are.

"The Word is just a personification, like Wisdom in Proverbs" Common

Strongest form

Jewish literature personifies God's word and wisdom poetically. John is doing theology by metaphor, not describing a person.

Reply

Personifications do not become flesh, get a name, eat fish, and die under a Roman governor. John 1:14 deliberately crosses the line no Jewish personification ever crossed — and the rest of the Gospel has this Word receiving worship and accepting Thomas's 'my Lord and my God' without correction.

"John is late — high Christology evolved" Trickier

Strongest form

John was written six decades after Jesus. Even the earlier material that speaks of a pre-existent Christ has him as a subordinate figure — God's chief angel, a divinity of a lesser order. The co-equal Son is the end point of a long development.

Reply

It does not hold — but two honest concessions first: John’s explicitness has no exact parallel in Mark, and how fast the whole church arrived at this confession is genuinely contested. The dispute is not really about dates — Ehrman himself finds a pre-existent Christ in material older than Paul. (Not every critic does: Dunn argues full incarnational pre-existence is clearly present only in John.) It is about what kind of being he was taken to be: on Ehrman’s account a pre-existent divine being of a lower order, an angel or something like one. The agency literature he appeals to is real. Two things in the earliest Christian material are hard to place among it. First, where Jesus is put: in 1 Cor 8:6 Paul divides the two divine titles of Israel’s confession that the LORD is one, so the ‘one God’ is the Father and the ‘one Lord’ is Jesus Christ, ‘through whom are all things’ — in the middle of an argument against idolatry. That reading is arguably dominant but genuinely contested (James McGrath: ‘one Lord’ is set alongside the creed, not inside it). On the majority reading, Wisdom is praised and Enoch is exalted, but no Jewish writer we know of wrote a second figure into the creed of divine uniqueness itself (Philo’s “second god,” the Logos, in Questions on Genesis 2.62, is the nearest case, and it is never placed inside that confession). Paul did. That is a difference in kind and not in degree — a creed is either rewritten or it is not — and say the limit out loud, because it cuts both ways: a plank of this shape cannot be discounted a little, so on McGrath’s reading it yields nothing at all. Do not run the ‘then Paul confessed a second divine power’ dilemma on Ehrman: he accepts that ancient Judaism had room for one. Second, worship — and the point needs care, because a gesture of homage was not itself the line: Israelites could bow before a king as well as before the LORD (1 Chronicles 29:20 uses one verb of prostration for both). What Jewish monotheism guarded for God was not the posture of homage but the substance of worship — sacrifice, invocation, prayer. Hurtado’s narrower point — he grants the chief-agent category — is that the angelic intermediaries of Second Temple texts characteristically refuse worship and redirect it to God (a pattern Bauckham first identified and Hurtado builds on), while the earliest Christians were praying to Jesus within the first two decades. Marana tha (1 Cor 16:22) is not the homage due a vice-regent but an invocation — prayer, offered by the first Aramaic-speaking church. Name the disagreement exactly rather than claiming the point: both sides agree the worship is early; they disagree over how to interpret it and how far it reaches toward later dogma. The weight falls on the placement and the worship. Development there certainly was, in vocabulary and in precision — but on the earliest Christian evidence we have, the figure was already where a developmental account of this kind needs him not to be.

"Wisdom in Proverbs 8 was created — same category" Trickier

Strongest form

Proverbs 8:22 has Wisdom say the LORD 'created me' at the beginning. If the Word is Wisdom, the Word is a creature.

Reply

Proverbs is poetry, and John does not quote it — he writes 'in the beginning' to echo Genesis 1, where the only actor is God. Whatever Proverbs' poetry means, John's prose says the Word made everything that was made.

"Was God only means represented God" Hard

Strongest form

Agents in Judaism could carry God's name and authority — the angel of the LORD speaks as God. The Word was God functionally, not ontologically.

Reply

Agency explains speaking for God; it does not explain creating all things, possessing glory with the Father 'before the world existed' (John 17:5), or being the proper object of worship — things Judaism reserved for God alone. John's contrast of en and egeneto is ontology in miniature: agents come to be; the Word was.

"Colwell’s rule is overclaimed by apologists" Hard

Strongest form

The grammatical rule cited for 'was God' does not prove definiteness; scholars admit a qualitative sense. So the verse is ambiguous at best.

Reply

Correct — and the qualitative sense is all the argument needs: the Word has the full nature of God. 'A god' is the one reading the grammar disfavors and the context forbids. The argument never rested on Colwell; it rests on verse 3 — the creator of all is not a creature — which no grammatical ambiguity touches.

The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

On the grammar, there is a broad consensus that theos in 1:1c is qualitative, not indefinite; read with the monotheism of the same Gospel, that is the full deity of the Word. The minority who defend “a god” are concentrated outside the academic mainstream. On the history, the field is more divided: the dating and development of Christology is genuinely contested terrain. But those disagreements are downstream of this sentence rather than about it.

A fairness most defenders skip: Some on our own side have claimed too much. Colwell’s rule was soon misapplied in reverse — as if an anarthrous predicate before the verb must therefore be definite — and many scholars who love the orthodox conclusion have leaned on it too hard. And the argument from the NWT’s rendering of the article-less theos at 1:6, 1:12 and 1:13 as “God” is narrower than polemics often claim: those are anarthrous genitives, not preverbal predicate nominatives, so they are not the identical construction.

The bounded verdict: John 1:1, carefully read, establishes that this author, writing this Gospel, confesses the Word as eternally pre-existent, personally distinct from the Father, and fully sharing the divine nature — and that he sees the man Jesus as that Word enfleshed, the one the church confesses as fully God and fully man, two natures in one person. That is enough to anchor the Nicene confession in the text and to answer both the “a god” and the Arian readings. It does not, by itself, settle how quickly the whole church arrived at this confession, and it is one strand of the New Testament case for Christ’s deity, not the whole of it.

    GS
    Greek student
    "The study you lean on pairs John 1:1 with Mark 15:39, where the centurion says Jesus was “Son of God” in the very same construction — and a Roman soldier was not confessing full deity. So the construction can’t give you deity in John 1:1 either."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    Grant the first half: the parallel is grammatical, not theological — Mark 15:39 is a centurion’s confession using the title “Son of God,” not itself a claim to full deity. But that is exactly what the pairing shows: grammatically the two are the same construction, and Harner reads both qualitatively — the stress falls on the nature the predicate ascribes, not on whether it should be counted “a” son or “the” son. The construction ordinarily says what the subject is; it does not set the content of the predicate. In John 1:1c the predicate is theos. The grammar by itself ascribes the kind — deity as to nature; it is the monotheism of the same Gospel, which knows only one divine nature and no gradations within the Godhead, that rules out any lesser or second-rank deity. Put the two together and what is left is the full divine nature, without remainder, with the predicate withholding only the personal identity of the Father. So the reading that makes the Word fully God as to nature is not an expedient invented to rescue a single verse; it is how the Greek construction regularly works — and it is that convergence, not the missing article, that finally carries the verse.
