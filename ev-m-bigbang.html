The Argument from the Big Bang

The deep dive tells you what the argument is. This track makes you able to use it — reconstruct it from memory, answer its hardest objections, and hold the line in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

The eternal universe was the default assumption of Aristotle and, again, of most modern science up to the early twentieth century. Twentieth-century cosmology overturned that picture. The expansion of space discovered by Edwin Hubble, the cosmic microwave background radiation, and the abundance of light elements all point back to an extremely hot, dense early state, commonly called the Big Bang; classical physics points to a beginning, while what a future theory of quantum gravity will say is not yet settled. The principle in play is modest and intuitive: whatever begins to exist has a cause. The claim is not that "everything has a cause," but that things which come into being do not pop into existence from nothing on their own.

If the universe — all matter, energy, space, and time — began to exist, then it too plausibly has a cause. And that cause cannot itself be part of the physical universe; it would have to transcend space and time, be immensely powerful, and exist independently of the cosmos. The convergence with what theists have long meant by God is real.

Sceptics ask whether some cosmological model avoids a beginning. It is worth being careful here: cosmologist Alexander Vilenkin, co-author of a key theorem on this question, has said “there are no models at this time that provide a satisfactory model for a universe without a beginning” — a statement about the current state of the science, not a final proof; his co-author Alan Guth and Sean Carroll both lean against a beginning while granting that nobody knows. The honest conclusion is an inference to the best explanation, offered for examination rather than as a knock-down demonstration. Note the scope: this argument reaches a cause of space, time and matter — not yet the Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Identifying that being with the God revealed in Christ is the work of the other arguments and of revelation — above all, the historical evidence for the resurrection. So our answer is a bounded yes: the Big Bang does not prove God, but a universe that began is better explained by a transcendent cause beyond it than by no cause at all.

Understand it

Not more prose — the load-bearing structure. See the skeleton, then learn to defend each bone.

The argument, formally

Three lines. The whole weight rests on the two premises.

P1

Whatever begins to exist has a cause.

P2

The universe began to exist — the best current cosmology is naturally suggestive of a finite past: evidence, not proof.

∴ C

Therefore, the universe has a cause of its existence.

The form is valid — deny the conclusion and you must reject P1 or P2; nearly every objection targets P2, disputing whether the Big Bang really marks an absolute beginning rather than a transition from some prior state.

Then the conceptual step — what the evidence converges on

Multiple independent lines of evidence — none of which were designed to test theism — all point in the same direction: back to an extremely hot, dense early state. Classical physics points to a beginning; what a future theory of quantum gravity will say is not yet settled.

Defending Premise 1

Whatever begins to exist has a cause

Precision check: Premise 1 says “whatever begins to exist,” not “everything has a cause.” The theist does not claim God needs a cause; the claim is about things that come into being, and a being that never began to exist is simply not in the argument’s scope. The conclusion as stated is modest: it yields a cause, not yet a God.

The metaphysical intuition The core warrant

Out of nothing, nothing comes. Things don’t pop into being uncaused from non-being — if they could, it’s inexplicable why we don’t see anything and everything doing so constantly.

The track record Confirmatory · limited

The causal principle is confirmed throughout ordinary experience; it is among the most robustly confirmed principles we have. A principle with that standing is rational to accept even before we reach the universe.

Honest limit: that track record is entirely of causes rearranging pre-existing material within the universe and within time, so extending the principle to the absolute beginning of the universe itself — where there is no prior material and no prior moment — is a step a thoughtful critic can resist. At that point it is the metaphysical intuition, not the inductive record, that is meant to carry the weight.

Objection — “A universe from nothing” (Krauss)

In A Universe from Nothing (2012), Lawrence Krauss argued that quantum field theory lets a universe arise spontaneously from the vacuum, so that no creator is required; at its strongest, he argues that quantum gravity may allow even space and time themselves to arise, with no pre-existing vacuum at all.

Reply

The trouble is the word “nothing,” and the sharpest critic here is not a theologian but the philosopher of physics David Albert, who reviewed the book for The New York Times. Albert observed that relativistic quantum fields are emphatically something: “if what we formerly took for nothing turns out, on closer examination, to have the makings of protons and neutrons and tables and chairs and planets and solar systems and galaxies and universes in it, then it wasn’t nothing.” A quantum vacuum governed by laws is a structured physical reality, not non-being; and the stronger version, with no vacuum at all, still presupposes quantum laws, a wave function, the mathematical structure that does the “creating.” The question merely retreats one step: where did the quantum substrate and the laws governing it come from? That is not an objection to physics; it is a reminder not to let a shift in the meaning of ‘nothing’ stand in for an answer.

The room: the argument is revived in our day above all by the philosopher William Lane Craig, from the medieval Islamic kalām tradition. Krauss presses the vacuum objection; David Albert, a philosopher of physics, is its sharpest critic.
Defending Premise 2

The universe began to exist — the best current cosmology is naturally suggestive of a finite past: evidence, not proof

Precision check: the claim is deliberately modest. The best current science is consistent with — indeed naturally suggestive of — a universe with a finite past. It needs only that, weighing the convergence of evidence against the alternatives, a real beginning is more reasonable than its denial. That is an inference to the best explanation, offered for examination, not a demonstration that forecloses inquiry. The Big Bang, properly handled, is evidence for Premise 2. It is not, on its own, a proof of theism.

Expansion, the relic glow and the light elements Established · not itself a beginning

Hubble’s 1929 paper established that galaxies recede from us at speeds proportional to their distance — the farther away, the faster. Space is stretching, and if it is stretching now it was denser in the past. The cosmic microwave background (CMB), the faint, uniform glow of microwave radiation discovered by accident by Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson in 1965, is exactly what one would expect as the cooled-down relic of an early hot, dense state — a prediction of the hot Big Bang model made years before its detection. The model also correctly predicts the cosmic abundances of the lightest elements, the roughly three-to-one ratio of hydrogen to helium by mass forged in the first few minutes of cosmic history. No single observation carries the weight; the case is a convergence of independent lines that were never designed to test anyone’s theology.

Honest limit: the standard Big Bang model describes the expansion and cooling of the universe from an extremely hot, dense early state. It does not, by itself, prove that this was the absolute first moment of all reality.

A past boundary: the singularity theorems and Borde–Guth–Vilenkin Strong corroboration · not proof

The BGV theorem (2003), using only the assumption that a universe has, on average, been expanding throughout its history, shows that the spacetime cannot be extended infinitely into the past along the worldlines of observers — it must have a past boundary. Crucially, the theorem requires no assumption about the matter content or the energy conditions; it is a kinematic result about expansion as such. That makes it apply even to inflationary and many multiverse scenarios, which was the authors’ original target. Vilenkin himself has written: “With the proof now in place, cosmologists can no longer hide behind the possibility of a past-eternal universe. There is no escape: they have to face the problem of a cosmic beginning.”

Honest limit: The Hawking–Penrose singularity theorems of 1965–70 strictly establish a boundary to the classical spacetime — that it cannot be extended indefinitely into the past — rather than a literal point of zero size. The BGV theorem is powerful corroboration that the kinds of cosmologies we can actually write down run into a past boundary — but it is a classical result, its most famous co-author, Alan Guth, suspects the universe did not begin, and it is therefore evidence and not proof.

No satisfactory past-eternal model, at this time Suggestive

Vilenkin, for his part, reviewed the leading models with Audrey Mithani in 2012 and found that none could be genuinely past-eternal; summarizing his own view, he has put it bluntly: “there are no models at this time that provide a satisfactory model for a universe without a beginning.” In a separate 2013 email to Lawrence Krauss, Vilenkin discussed the most-cited would-be escape — a contracting phase before the expansion, as in the Aguirre–Gratton and Carroll–Chen models — but judged that such a scenario “is essentially equivalent to a beginning.”

Honest limit: note the careful phrase “at this time.” That is the voice of science: a report on the present state of the models, not a closed book.

The theory that could have died Historical

A model invented to smuggle in a Creator would not have spent its first decades failing its own tests in public — yet that is very nearly what the Big Bang did. Three problems in particular could have killed it. Early measurements of the expansion rate implied a universe younger than the Earth: on the distance scale of the 1930s, an age of only about two billion years, set against rocks already known by radiometric dating to be older. The model had no account of where the heavier elements came from. And the faint radiation its hot early state should have left behind could not be found. Any one of these, left standing, would have sunk it. Each was resolved on evidence — and, tellingly, part of the resolving was done by the theory’s most prominent opponent. Walter Baade announced in 1952 that the yardstick used for cosmic distances had been miscalibrated; correcting it began to push the universe’s age upward, and Allan Sandage’s later revisions carried that age well past the age of the Earth and dissolved the paradox. The heavy-element problem was cracked in large part by Fred Hoyle — a lifelong champion of the rival steady-state model — who worked out how stars forge carbon and the elements beyond it. And the missing radiation was not, in the end, missing: Ralph Alpher and Robert Herman had predicted it in 1948, estimating a leftover glow of roughly five degrees above absolute zero, some seventeen years before Penzias and Wilson chanced upon the real thing — a genuine, risky prediction that the steady-state picture gave no reason to expect. What makes the history worth telling is that the beginning was, for many of the physicists who found it, unwelcome — and they reported it anyway. Arthur Eddington wrote in 1931 that “philosophically, the notion of a beginning of the present order of Nature is repugnant to me… I should like to find a genuine loophole.” Robert Dicke and his colleagues, defending an oscillating universe in the very 1965 paper that helped interpret the newfound background radiation, observed that an infinitely old cosmos would relieve us “of the necessity of understanding the origin of matter.” These were not men reading Genesis into their equations; several would have preferred no beginning, and the field came to accept one because the data insisted.

Objection — Carroll: a classical past boundary is not a beginning of being Hardest

The physicist Sean Carroll, debating Craig in 2014, pressed the central point: BGV is a theorem about classical spacetime descriptions, and the regime it reaches back into is precisely where classical descriptions fail and quantum gravity takes over. A “past boundary” in the classical model need not be a metaphysical beginning of being; it may simply mark where our current equations stop applying. Carroll argued further that “beginning” and even “cause” are not fundamental features of physics at all but emergent, macroscopic notions ill-suited to the boundary of spacetime.

Reply

What is disputed is the metaphysical interpretation of the past boundary: whether it is a true beginning of being, or the place where our physics runs out and a deeper theory might continue the story. Vilenkin reads the evidence as favoring a beginning; Carroll and Guth both lean against a beginning while granting that nobody knows; many cosmologists regard it as simply unsettled pending a theory of quantum gravity we do not yet have. The argument does not need cosmology to be finished. It needs only that, weighing the convergence of evidence against the alternatives, a real beginning is more reasonable than its denial.

Objection — Cyclic and bouncing universes

A different strategy keeps the universe past-eternal by having it bounce — contracting, rebounding, and expanding again without end. Ekpyrotic models (Steinhardt and Turok) and loop quantum cosmology replace the singularity with such a bounce. As far back as 1934 Richard Tolman noted that entropy accumulates across cycles, so each cycle would be larger and longer than the last; traced backward, the cycles shrink toward a beginning rather than receding forever. That was the classic objection to the older oscillating models, and modern cyclic cosmology was built to answer it, so its strongest form deserves a hearing. In Steinhardt and Turok’s cyclic model, each cycle includes a long phase of accelerated expansion which, in their words, “dilutes by an exponential factor the entropy and the density of black holes and any other debris produced since the preceding big bang.” They grant Tolman his arithmetic—“Globally, the total entropy in the Universe grows from cycle to cycle, as Tolman suggested”—but answer that the entropy density, “which is all any real observer would actually see, has perfect cyclic behavior,” being created at each bounce and diluted before the next. In 2019 Anna Ijjas and Steinhardt went further, in a paper titled “A New Kind of Cyclic Universe”: intervals of ultra-slow contraction end in a non-singular classical bounce, and “the Hubble parameter, energy density and temperature oscillate periodically, but the scale factor grows by an exponential factor from one cycle to the next.” In a follow-up they argued that such a universe “can undergo an unbounded number of cycles in the past and/or the future.” This is serious physics from serious physicists, and Tolman’s objection, in its 1934 form, does not settle the matter against it.

Reply

The entropy is diluted because the universe grows from cycle to cycle, and a universe that grows on balance is expanding on average — the one assumption the BGV theorem needs. On the analysis of William Kinney and Nina Stein, answering Tolman this way walks into BGV, and that worry is not merely an apologist’s gloss. Asking of their own earlier model, “Does this mean that the cycling has no beginning?”, Steinhardt and Turok answered, “This issue is not settled at present,” and judged that “the most likely story is that cycling was preceded by some singular beginning.” The question is not closed: Petar Pavlović and Marko Sossich claim a class of cyclic models that “can in principle solve the problem of the entropy growth, and are at the same time geodesically complete.” So our verdict is bounded. The cyclic escape has not been refuted; what can be said is that its best-known answer to the entropy problem trades that difficulty for another, and that its own architects judged a beginning the most likely story for their earlier model. It is a live hypothesis, not a demonstration that the past is eternal.

The room: the hot Big Bang model — expansion, the CMB, light-element abundances — is not in serious dispute. Vilenkin reads the evidence as favoring a beginning; Carroll and Guth both lean against a beginning while granting that nobody knows; many cosmologists regard it as simply unsettled pending a theory of quantum gravity we do not yet have. None of these is a fringe position. Scope: the argument does not reach the God of the Nicene Creed by physics alone — it points, at most, to a transcendent, immaterial, uncaused, immensely powerful cause of space and time, though not, by itself, to the Triune God.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

This is what separates someone who knows the Big Bang argument from someone who can defend it. Each gets its best form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged just below.

The one that matters most: Sean Carroll’s — a classical past boundary marks where our equations stop, not where being began. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"The Big Bang is just where our physics breaks down, not a true beginning" Trickier

Strongest form

The initial singularity is where classical general relativity stops working — infinite density is a sign the theory has broken, not a real first moment. A full theory of quantum gravity could smooth it into a transition from some earlier state, so there's no genuine beginning.

Reply

The Borde–Guth–Vilenkin theorem is independent of the singularity — it assumes nothing about the matter content or the energy conditions. But be honest about its reach: it is a result about classical spacetime, and the boundary it finds is exactly where a quantum-gravity description would take over. Vilenkin reads the evidence as favoring a beginning; his co-author Alan Guth suspects the universe did not have a beginning — very likely eternal, though nobody knows. Strong corroboration, not proof.

"Quantum cosmology lets the universe arise from nothing" Hard

Strongest form

Models like Hartle–Hawking's no-boundary proposal, or Krauss's account of the universe coming from the quantum vacuum, show the cosmos can emerge spontaneously "from nothing" — at its strongest, Krauss argues that quantum gravity may allow even space and time themselves to arise, with no pre-existing vacuum at all — so no creator is required.

Reply

Those "nothings" are not non-being. A quantum state, a vacuum seething with energy, a set of physical laws — these are richly structured somethings. The question simply moves back a step: where did the quantum substrate and the laws governing it come from? Calling a law-governed state "nothing" is an equivocation, not an explanation.

"The multiverse / eternal inflation removes the beginning" Hard

Strongest form

In eternal inflation, our universe is one bubble nucleating out of an eternally inflating background. The background has always been inflating, so there's no overall beginning to explain.

Reply

The Borde–Guth–Vilenkin theorem applies directly to inflating multiverses: because the inflating background expands on average, the theorem implies it too must have a past boundary — in its classical description. Vilenkin has said “there are no models at this time that provide a satisfactory model for a universe without a beginning,” though he also flagged a multiverse scenario, explored with Jaume Garriga, where the theorem “may not apply.” The multiverse pushes the beginning up a level; it doesn't erase it.

"A cyclic or bouncing universe is eternal" Trickier

Strongest form

Ekpyrotic and loop-quantum-cosmology models replace the singularity with a bounce — the universe contracts, rebounds, and expands again, cycling forever with no first moment.

Reply

Entropy accumulates across cycles (Tolman, 1934): each cycle would be larger and longer than the last, so traced backward the cycles shrink toward a beginning — and a universe that expands on net still inherits a BGV past boundary. There are genuine loopholes, which Vilenkin judged “essentially equivalent to a beginning”; this is contested territory, not a closed case.

"Even if it began, why a personal cause rather than impersonal?" Hard

Strongest form

Grant a cause of the Big Bang. Why think it's a mind that chooses, rather than some impersonal mechanism or law? You've reached at most a first cause, not a personal God.

Reply

A cause of all space and time is itself spaceless and timeless. But a timeless mechanical cause whose sufficient conditions are eternally present would produce an eternal effect — the effect would be as old as the cause. Yet on the evidence the universe is not eternal; it began. The one way a changeless, timeless cause can give rise to a temporal effect with a beginning, Craig argues, is through the free choice of an agent — agent causation. That points to a personal cause, not a mechanism; it is a philosophical inference, disputed, not a scientific result — and it reaches a personal Creator, not yet the Triune God, whose identity rests on revelation and, above all, on the historical evidence for the resurrection.

Where this comes from — primary sources, not our summary

Georges Lemaître1927/1931 · "primeval atom"
Belgian priest-physicist who in 1927 derived an expanding universe from Einstein’s equations of general relativity, two years before Hubble’s observations, and four years later proposed that it began from a “primeval atom.”
Edwin Hubble1929 · redshift-distance relation
Provided the observational evidence that the universe is expanding — galaxies recede faster the farther away they are, the data that grounds running cosmic history back to an origin.
Arno Penzias & Robert Wilson1965 · cosmic microwave background
Detected the relic radiation predicted by Big Bang theory — the leftover glow of the early hot universe. Awarded the Nobel Prize in 1978.
Borde, Guth & Vilenkin2003 · cosmology paper
Proved that a universe expanding on average has a past boundary in its classical description — the modern scientific spine of P2. Worth reading their own framing of how general the result is.
