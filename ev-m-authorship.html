Who Wrote the Gospels?

The deep dive tells you what the argument is. This track makes you able to use it — reconstruct it from memory, answer its hardest objections, and hold the line in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

No Evangelist names himself within the body of his text, and the familiar titles—“The Gospel according to Matthew,” and so on—may well, in their present form, be later than the books themselves. But most ancient biographies and many ancient histories did not name their authors in the text either, because an author’s name normally travelled with the title on the copy rather than inside the prose. An unsigned ancient book was not, for that reason, an authorless one.

The names attached to the four Gospels are attested comparatively early (Mark’s and Matthew’s by Papias, John’s before or alongside Irenaeus, Luke’s by the end of the second century), consistently, and with no rival tradition of authorship (an argument from silence). Papias wrote in the first half of the second century (most current proposals run from about AD 95 to about 140), and on Papias’s own report Mark’s link with Peter reaches back to the Elder before him. Luke is not named before about AD 180, but his is the Gospel least likely ever to have been authorless to its first reader. An early name “John” does not settle which John, but the witnesses do tie the Fourth Gospel to a disciple named John before the end of the second century. The nearest thing to an exception to the ‘no rival’ point, a disputed report that some opponents of John’s Gospel ascribed it to the heretic Cerinthus, describes a polemical rejection of that Gospel, not a rival tradition.

The honest scope: the ‘no rival’ premise is an argument from silence; most scholars still regard the Gospels as originally anonymous, so the question remains open; but the evidence does not fit the picture of the names as late, scattered guesses. On that evidence, the case that the names were attached early, and probably from the start, is strong and live, though not settled. Who wrote a book is one question; whether its reports are accurate is a second; whether its central claims are true is a third. And while an early attribution is not proof of authorship, and authorship is not truth, the four names deserve to be weighed as serious historical testimony, not waved away as late inventions.

The argument, formally

Three lines. The whole weight rests on the two premises.

P1

When an ancient work does not name its author in its text, its author was normally identified by the label it carried and by external testimony; and when the testimony to a name is comparatively early, consistent, and unrivalled, the name probably travelled with the work from the start, though other explanations remain possible.

P2

The names of the four Evangelists are attested comparatively early (Mark’s and Matthew’s by Papias, John’s before or alongside Irenaeus, Luke’s by the end of the second century), consistently, and with no rival; the ‘no rival’ premise is an argument from silence.

∴ C

Therefore the traditional attributions are probably original. Most scholars still regard the Gospels as originally anonymous, so the question remains open; and while an early attribution is not proof of authorship, and authorship is not truth, the four names deserve to be weighed as serious historical testimony, not waved away as late inventions.

The logic is valid; the contest is over the premises: whether the names really are early and unrivalled, and whether that pattern is better explained by names that were there from the start than by names fixed later.

Then the conceptual step — why is an unsigned book not an authorless one?

Most ancient biographies and many ancient histories did not name their authors in the text either; Plutarch’s Lives and Philostratus’s Life of Apollonius are examples, as are Livy’s and Sallust’s histories, because an author’s name normally travelled with the title on the copy rather than inside the prose. Plutarch’s authorship is also secured in other ways—by his many other writings, and by personal asides in the Lives about his own family and town—so for the Gospels the external testimony carries more of the weight. But the comparison shows that an unsigned ancient book was not, for that reason, an authorless one.

Defending Premise 1

When an ancient work does not name its author in its text, its author was normally identified by the label it carried and by external testimony

Precision check: two facts usually stand behind the word “anonymous,” and both should be granted. First, no Evangelist names himself in his text. Second, the familiar titles (“The Gospel according to Matthew,” and so on) may well, in their present form, be later than the books themselves. Neither observation is the issue. The question is what follows from them. And the premise is hedged in its own words: the name probably travelled with the work from the start, though other explanations remain possible.

Ancient authors were named on the copy, not in the prose The core warrant

Most ancient biographies and many ancient histories did not name their authors in the text either; Plutarch’s Lives and Philostratus’s Life of Apollonius are examples, as are Livy’s and Sallust’s histories, because an author’s name normally travelled with the title on the copy rather than inside the prose. Plutarch does not name himself in the Lives, any more than the Evangelists name themselves in the Gospels. An unsigned ancient book was not, for that reason, an authorless one. Its author was identified by the material around the text, such as a title at the head or the end of the roll or a label attached to it, rather than by a signature inside it.

Honest limit: Plutarch’s authorship is also secured in other ways—by his many other writings, and by personal asides in the Lives about his own family and town—so for the Gospels the external testimony carries more of the weight.

The titles are later in their present form — the names need not be Granted, then narrowed

The full titles in the form we know—“The Gospel according to…”—probably took that shape in the second century. The German scholar Martin Hengel argued that titles were attached early, when the books first had to be identified, but his case for the originality of the titles has won few followers, as Gathercole himself notes. The question that matters, though, is not when the formula took its present shape. It is whether the name Mark was attached to that book from early on.

Honest limit: our manuscripts begin after a gap of one to several generations from the autographs, and so the case leans instead on the writers who name the Evangelists.

Objection — “Gospels did circulate without names”

In the middle of the second century Marcion, who severed Christianity from Judaism entirely, used a single Gospel, and Tertullian reports: “Marcion, on the other hand, you must know, ascribes no author to his Gospel.” Justin Martyr, writing at about the same time, describes the Gospels collectively as “the memoirs of the apostles” and, in the passages where he describes them, does not name the individual Evangelists. If the names were fixed from the start, why do two of the best-known Christian figures of the mid-second century leave them out?

Reply

The Marcion objection rests on a real datum, and it cuts both ways. It shows that a Gospel could circulate without a name in the middle of the second century. But Tertullian, writing some sixty years later, treats the omission as a mark against Marcion, not as normal practice, and Irenaeus, writing around 180, reports that Marcion “mutilates the Gospel which is according to Luke.” Some scholars contest that account; Joseph Tyson’s reconstruction rests on a contested premise—that Marcion used an earlier form of Luke which our Gospel revised. On the ancient reports, however, the case is one of a single radical editor who, as Tertullian tells it, would not put a name to a Gospel the church called Luke’s; it is not evidence of a church that never had one, though reports written a generation or more later cannot by themselves show that the name was already attached in Marcion’s day. As for Justin, his description of the memoirs as “drawn up by His apostles and those who followed them” does not name the Evangelists, but it is consistent with the later attributions, which credit two Gospels to apostles and two to their followers.

The room: Most New Testament scholars hold that the Gospels were published anonymously; Gathercole himself documents that majority, and it is represented in the specialist literature by Wolter, and, as a deliberate stylistic choice modelled on Old Testament historiography, by Baum. The live issue is not whether a missing name in the text proves anonymity, since the ancient comparisons tell against that, but whether the uniform early names are best explained as original, or as fixed later, whether by a single decision when the four were first gathered or by later guesses that converged.
Defending Premise 2

The names of the four Evangelists are attested comparatively early, consistently, and with no rival — and the ‘no rival’ half is an argument from silence

Precision check: one caution governs all of it. The testimony is early, named, and widespread—from Asia Minor, Antioch, and Gaul, and from a Gnostic teacher the church opposed as well as from its own bishops—though not all of it is demonstrably independent of Papias, and later writers may repeat earlier ones.

Mark — Papias and the Elder The earliest link

The earliest testimony comes from Papias of Hierapolis, preserved by the church historian Eusebius; Papias wrote in the first half of the second century (most current proposals run from about AD 95 to about 140). He preserved a tradition he traced to “the Elder”: that “Mark, having become the interpreter of Peter, wrote down accurately, though not in order, whatsoever he remembered of the things said or done by Christ.” If Papias is right that he is passing on what the Elder used to say, the link between Mark and Peter is older than his own book.

Honest limit: the tradition presents Mark not as an eyewitness but as the recorder of one. Irenaeus may well depend on Papias here, so the weight rests where it began: on Papias’s own report, Mark’s name reaches back behind Papias’s book to the Elder.

Matthew — an early name, a real puzzle Less secure

Papias also reports that “Matthew wrote the oracles in the Hebrew language, and every one interpreted them as he was able.” What Papias meant by Matthew’s “oracles” in “the Hebrew language” is disputed—our Matthew is in Greek—so his report about Matthew is less secure than his report about Mark, and the Mark–Peter link does not depend on it.

Honest limit: the honest summary for Matthew is an early name, recorded by the same writer who gives us Mark, with a real puzzle about exactly what that writer was describing.

Luke — named late, but not faceless The thinnest evidence

No surviving writer names Luke as the author of the third Gospel before Irenaeus and the Muratorian Fragment, late in the second century. Yet Luke is, on Gathercole’s argument, the Gospel least likely to have been anonymous at its origin, because of how it begins. Luke opens with a formal preface in the first person, and its second volume opens the same way (Acts 1:1). The preface shows that the work was not composed as a faceless community product: its author speaks in his own voice, to a named reader, about his own method.

Honest limit: whether the “we” passages mean that the author was himself present is debated, and none of this shows that the author’s name was Luke; the name still rests on the later testimony. Luke is named late, but his is the Gospel least likely ever to have been authorless to its first reader.

John — named from opposite sides of the church The most widely named

John is the most widely named of the four, and the witnesses come from opposite sides of the second-century church. The Valentinian Gnostic Ptolemy (or a teacher of his school), whom Irenaeus quotes in order to refute and who wrote before him, attributed the Fourth Gospel’s opening words to “John, the disciple of the Lord.” Theophilus of Antioch, writing at about the same time as Irenaeus, introduces the Gospel’s first line the same way. And in the 190s Polycrates, bishop of Ephesus, names among the church’s great dead in Asia “John, who was both a witness and a teacher, who reclined upon the bosom of the Lord.”

Honest limit: an early name “John” does not settle which John, but the witnesses do tie the Fourth Gospel to a disciple named John before the end of the second century, by writers on both sides of the church’s quarrel with the Gnostics.

Irenaeus was not the first Corroboration

Irenaeus, around AD 180, names all four Evangelists and ties each name to the opening of its Gospel, but he was not the first after Papias to name one. He insists “it is not possible that the Gospels can be either more or fewer in number than they are,” comparing them to the four zones of the world and the four winds—a strange-sounding argument to modern ears, and one aimed at groups he names who used one Gospel or more than four; but it takes the four books and their names as already in the churches’ hands, not as something he is introducing. Irenaeus is a witness to what the churches already held, not the origin of the names.

No rival names Argument from silence

Wherever the surviving sources attribute the Gospels, they attribute them to Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John, with no rival tradition of authorship (the nearest thing to an exception to the ‘no rival’ point, a disputed report that some opponents of John’s Gospel ascribed it to the heretic Cerinthus, is taken up below). And no surviving second-century writer treats the four Gospels as books whose authors were unknown.

Honest limit: the silence cuts both ways: early writings such as Ignatius’s letters, Polycarp’s letter and the Didache cite Gospel material as what ‘the Lord’ said, or as ‘the Gospel’, without naming any Evangelist; on its own that is compatible with either view. No rival tradition of authorship appears in the surviving sources, though uniform names can still be mistaken. That is an argument from silence, and most scholars still regard the Gospels as originally anonymous, so the question remains open; but the evidence does not fit the picture of the names as late, scattered guesses.

Objection — “Papias is a fragile foundation” Hard

To build on Papias is to lean on a single second-century figure whom Eusebius himself, centuries on, judged to have been “of very limited understanding.” If Papias is unreliable, the oldest link in the Mark chain is weak, and much of the later testimony may simply repeat him. A skeptic can press further: what Papias describes need not be our Gospels. His Matthew is a Hebrew collection of “oracles,” which our Greek Matthew is not, and his Mark is a record of Peter’s recollections written “not in order.”

Reply

Eusebius’s disdain was aimed chiefly at his millenarian eschatology rather than at his reliability about Mark, and what Papias passes on is not his own speculation but what he had from “the Elder”—so, on Papias’s own report, the link between Mark and Peter is older than his writing. The further objection, that Papias need not be describing our Gospels, has already been granted for Matthew. For Mark it is weaker: “not in order” is often read as a defence of a book whose arrangement had been criticised, which fits a Gospel rather than counting against one, and many scholars take Papias to be describing our Mark or something very close to it. Nor is Papias the only early witness to the Gospels’ names, though he is the earliest for Mark.

Objection — “There was a rival after all”

A disputed report found chiefly in the fourth-century Epiphanius says that some opponents of John’s Gospel ascribed it to the heretic Cerinthus. If even one early group gave John’s Gospel a different author, the claim that there was no rival tradition is weaker than it sounds.

Reply

The Cerinthus report, the nearest thing to an exception to the ‘no rival’ point, describes a polemical rejection of that Gospel, not a rival tradition. Its aim was to discredit the book, not to report who had written it; and even that ascription, to a man Irenaeus makes a contemporary of John, placed the Gospel around the end of the first century. It is also uncertain in itself: T. Scott Manor, in a specialist study of these opponents, questions both the second-century dating of that opposition and the authenticity of the Cerinthus attribution.

The room: Against the majority stands a minority whose case has grown more detailed: Hengel’s argument that the titles were attached early; Richard Bauckham’s historical argument that Mark stands very close to Peter and that John’s Gospel was written by a disciple of Jesus; and Gathercole’s 2018 study, which argues that the names are probably original while stressing that this does not by itself show who wrote the books. The dissenters have not won the field, and a defender should not pretend otherwise. In our judgment, what they have done is sharpen the question, and the evidence does not fit the picture of the names as late, scattered guesses.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

This is what separates someone who knows the argument from someone who can defend it. Each gets its best form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: the collection hypothesis — the names may have been fixed once, when the four were first gathered together, which would explain the uniformity without making them original. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"The names were attached when the four were collected" Hardest

Strongest form

Most New Testament scholars hold that the Gospels were first issued without names, a view represented by Michael Wolter’s study of the anonymous writings of the New Testament; Armin Baum has argued that this silence is a deliberate stylistic device, modelled on the anonymous historical books of the Old Testament and the ancient Near East rather than on Greco-Roman historiography. On the common reconstruction, the titles that now name the Gospels were added later, once the four circulated together and had to be told apart. Nor need uniform names be original: later guesses can converge when they follow the same clues in the texts (the tax collector called Matthew in Matthew 9:9, Paul’s companion Luke [Colossians 4:14] behind the “we” passages, the disciple whom Jesus loved) or when later writers follow one early authority. And the skeptic has a sharp reply to the ‘no rival’ point: the names may have been fixed once, when the four were first gathered together, which would explain the uniformity without making them original.

Reply

The evidence cannot rule it out, but it has costs. On Papias’s own report, at least one of the names—Mark’s—was already being passed on by the Elder before Papias wrote. If so, whoever first gathered the four did not invent Mark’s name but inherited it, unless the gathering came earlier still, which would itself place that name very early. Matthew’s case is less clear, since it is disputed whether Papias’s Matthew is our Greek Gospel; and for Luke and John, whose earliest named witnesses fall in the second half of the second century, Papias does not touch the hypothesis; there its main cost is that a decision made at one moment left no record of anyone saying that the authors were unknown, though that too is an argument from silence. None of this refutes the hypothesis; it makes it more expensive than it first appears. That the later guesses converged by following shared clues cannot be excluded, but such clues explain some of the agreement more easily than all of it.

"Gospels did circulate without names" Trickier

Strongest form

In the middle of the second century Marcion used a single Gospel, and Tertullian reports: “Marcion, on the other hand, you must know, ascribes no author to his Gospel.” Justin Martyr, writing at about the same time, describes the Gospels collectively as “the memoirs of the apostles” and, in the passages where he describes them, does not name the individual Evangelists. If the names were fixed from the start, why do two of the best-known Christian figures of the mid-second century leave them out?

Reply

The Marcion objection rests on a real datum, and it cuts both ways. It shows that a Gospel could circulate without a name in the middle of the second century. But Tertullian, writing some sixty years later, treats the omission as a mark against Marcion, not as normal practice, and Irenaeus, writing around 180, reports that Marcion “mutilates the Gospel which is according to Luke.” Some scholars contest that account; Joseph Tyson’s reconstruction rests on a contested premise—that Marcion used an earlier form of Luke which our Gospel revised. On the ancient reports, however, the case is one of a single radical editor who, as Tertullian tells it, would not put a name to a Gospel the church called Luke’s; it is not evidence of a church that never had one, though reports written a generation or more later cannot by themselves show that the name was already attached in Marcion’s day. As for Justin, his description of the memoirs as “drawn up by His apostles and those who followed them” does not name the Evangelists, but it is consistent with the later attributions, which credit two Gospels to apostles and two to their followers. Justin’s silence is weak evidence in itself: in his surviving works he never names Paul either.

"Apostolic names were cheap" Common

Strongest form

Early Christians did attach the names of Jesus’ followers to books those followers did not write: the Gospel of Peter and the Gospel of Thomas borrow such names, and the Gospel of Judas trades on another. And Ehrman presses a further point: several canonical books are pseudonymous by the judgment of most critical scholars—the Pastoral Epistles, 2 Peter, perhaps Ephesians—so the church enforced a criterion it could not actually verify and sometimes got wrong. Why trust its attributions of the Gospels any more?

Reply

The pseudonymity objection is answered in part by chronology. The Gospel of Thomas as we have it, the Gospel of Peter, the Gospel of Judas, and the rest are second-century or later compositions, written generations after the apostles whose names they borrow. And the church tested such books rather than simply accepting the names they bore. Around AD 200 Serapion, bishop of Antioch, who had at first let the Gospel of Peter be read at Rhossus without examining it, later obtained and read it and stated the principle he was applying: “we receive both Peter and the other apostles as Christ; but we reject intelligently the writings falsely ascribed to them, knowing that such were not handed down to us.” The existence of forgeries shows that a name could be borrowed; it does not show that every name was. On the disputed canonical letters, it must be acknowledged that the church’s judgments about authorship were made with the limited tools of antiquity and are regarded as insecure by many modern critics—though the critics’ verdicts are themselves contested by other scholars, many of them evangelical and some Catholic. The case for the Gospel names does not rest on the church’s having judged every attribution rightly. It rests on how early, how consistent, and how unrivalled these particular attributions are, and that is evidence any historian can weigh.

Touch the originals

Where this comes from — primary sources, not our summary

The earliest testimony to Mark’s and Matthew’s names, preserved by the church historian Eusebius; Papias’s own work is lost. On his date, most current proposals run from about AD 95 to about 140.

Gathercole grants that the Gospels do not name their authors in the text and that the present titles are probably later in their present form, and disputes what follows from those facts; he argues that the names are probably original while stressing that this does not by itself show who wrote the books.
Michael Wolter1988 · “Die anonymen Schriften des Neuen Testaments”
The majority view: most New Testament scholars hold that the Gospels were first issued without names, a view represented by Michael Wolter’s study of the anonymous writings of the New Testament.
The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

The debate is live, and it should be described accurately. Most New Testament scholars hold that the Gospels were published anonymously; Gathercole himself documents that majority, and it is represented in the specialist literature by Wolter, and, as a deliberate stylistic choice modelled on Old Testament historiography, by Baum. Against it stands a minority whose case has grown more detailed: Hengel’s argument that the titles were attached early; Richard Bauckham’s historical argument that Mark stands very close to Peter and that John’s Gospel was written by a disciple of Jesus; and Gathercole’s 2018 study, which argues that the names are probably original while stressing that this does not by itself show who wrote the books. The dissenters have not won the field, and a defender should not pretend otherwise.

A fairness most defenders skip: The testimony is early, named, and widespread, though not all of it is demonstrably independent of Papias, and later writers may repeat earlier ones. The two silences—that no surviving second-century writer treats the Gospels’ authors as unknown, and that no rival tradition of authorship appears—are arguments from silence, and they should be weighed as such. And the case does not settle which John stands behind the Fourth Gospel, or whether Papias’s Matthew is our Greek Matthew.

The bounded verdict: The evidence does not fit the picture of the names as late, scattered guesses. On that evidence, the case that the names were attached early, and probably from the start, is strong and live, though not settled. In the popular sense of ‘anonymous’—that nobody in the early church knew or agreed who wrote them—the description does not fit the evidence. In the scholarly sense—that the books were issued without names which were attached later—the question remains open. This evidence does not prove that the four men named wrote the books that bear their names. And while an early attribution is not proof of authorship, and authorship is not truth, the four names deserve to be weighed as serious historical testimony, not waved away as late inventions.

    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    Part of that is simply true, and it cannot be waved away: an early attribution is not proof of authorship. That is fair, and nothing above claims more. What it does not show is that early, uniform testimony counts for little: by the ordinary standards for assigning ancient works to their authors this is comparatively early and named testimony; for many classical works the formal evidence of authorship is later and thinner. And authorship is not truth: who wrote a book is one question; whether its reports are accurate is a second; whether its central claims are true is a third. What this evidence earns is narrower and still real: the four names deserve to be weighed as serious historical testimony, not waved away as late inventions.
