Did Jesus Claim to Be the Messiah?

The deep dive explains the case. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild from memory how Jesus presented himself as Israel's Messiah, more in deed than in word, and what the case does and does not establish — then hold it together in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

Above the head of a dying man outside Jerusalem, the charge against him was posted: "THE KING OF THE JEWS" (Mark 15:26). All four Gospels report it. It is a strange charge to hang over a preacher from Galilee who raised no army and was arrested at night with no force behind him. Did Jesus of Nazareth present himself as Israel's Messiah, the anointed king the prophets had promised? Or did his followers give him the title after his death and write it back into his life?

The answer is yes, with a qualification that matters. Jesus seldom used the title of himself, and he discouraged others from spreading it. He made the claim mainly by what he did: he answered John's question with the anointed one's deeds (Matthew 11:3–6; Luke 7:20–23), appointed twelve to judge Israel's tribes (Luke 22:29–30; compare Matthew 19:28), rode into Jerusalem on Zechariah's pattern (Zechariah 9:9), acted with authority over the temple, did not deny the title at his trial, and died under a royal charge. No one of these strands is decisive by itself, and each has been disputed. The case rests on how they converge.

Honest scope: it shows that Jesus made a claim; it does not show that the claim was true — that turns on what happened after the cross. All six strands reach us through the Gospels, so the case is a historian's judgment of probability, not a demonstration, and the field is divided. And "Messiah" did not, by itself, mean "divine": it meant God's anointed king, the heir of David who would restore Israel — though Jewish hope in Jesus' day was not one thing. We hold that the claim was true: that God raised the crucified Jesus and so vindicated the one Rome had executed as a pretender, and that the Messiah of Israel is the eternal Son, fully God and fully man, the Lord of all.

The argument, formally

Jesus presented himself as Israel's Messiah, more in deed than in word, and he died for it.

P1

In his ministry Jesus made the claim mainly by what he did: he answered John's question with the anointed one's deeds (Matthew 11:3–6; Luke 7:20–23), appointed twelve to judge Israel's tribes (Luke 22:29–30; compare Matthew 19:28), rode into Jerusalem on Zechariah's pattern (Zechariah 9:9), and acted with authority over the temple (Mark 11:15–17).

P2

On Mark's account, asked whether he was the Christ, Jesus said, "I am" (Mark 14:61–62); and Rome executed him under a royal charge, "The King of the Jews" (Mark 15:26). The charge records what Rome concluded, not by itself what Jesus said; what it does make secure is that Jesus died as a man Rome treated as a royal pretender.

∴ C

Therefore Jesus presented himself as Israel's Messiah, more in deed than in word, and he died for it. No one of these strands is decisive by itself; the case rests on how they converge. (It shows that Jesus made a claim; it does not show that the claim was true. And "Messiah" did not, by itself, mean "divine.") We hold that the claim was true: that God raised the crucified Jesus and so vindicated the one Rome had executed as a pretender, and that the Messiah of Israel is the eternal Son, fully God and fully man, the Lord of all.

All six strands reach us through the Gospels, so the case is a historian's judgment of probability, not a demonstration. They are not all independent: the entry, the temple action, the trial and the charge belong to one sequence, and a single alternative can account for much of it. But a Jesus who never thought of himself in messianic terms must still explain the reply to John and the appointing of the Twelve, which come from his Galilean ministry, apart from that week. And "Messiah," "Son of David," even "Son of God" in the royal sense could be used of a man by a first-century Jew holding the strictest monotheism: these titles are royal and human before they are anything more — not adoptionism, not that Jesus was a mere man later promoted to divine sonship; his unique, eternal Sonship is affirmed, and the point is only the titles' native range.

The objection to know first: "the church made him Messiah after Easter."

Perhaps Jesus was a prophet of the kingdom and nothing more. His disciples, convinced after his death that God had raised him, read Psalm 110 ("Sit at My right hand") and Psalm 2 as describing his enthronement, concluded that he must be the Messiah, and read the title back into the story of his life. Some of the earliest Christian formulas can sound like this: God "made" him Lord and Christ (Acts 2:36), and he "was declared with power to be the Son of God by His resurrection from the dead" (Romans 1:4). The reply: in the categories of the time a resurrection would mark him out as a vindicated martyr; it would not, by itself, show him to be the Messiah. Where Jews hoped for resurrection, they looked for the dead to be raised together at the end of the age (Daniel 12:2), but nothing in our sources treats the Messiah’s own rising from the dead as his sign. So the title the first Christians gave the crucified Jesus is very hard to derive from Easter alone. The apostles did read the resurrection as God's confirmation of it (Acts 2:36) — but a confirmation needs something to confirm. Paul's formula has the same shape: the one "declared with power to be the Son of God by His resurrection" is already "His Son, who was a descendant of David according to the flesh" (Romans 1:3–4). The enthronement psalms could explain how the first Christians spoke of a messiahship they already believed in. They do not explain why they chose those psalms for a crucified man, unless the royal question had been raised before the cross.

Defending Premise 1

In his ministry Jesus made the claim mainly by what he did

Precision check: Jesus seldom used the title of himself, and he discouraged others from spreading it. The premise is not that he campaigned for the title; it is that he made the claim mainly by what he did. Each strand has critics, and each, taken alone, allows a lower reading. What is harder to explain away is the pattern.

"He has anointed Me" — and the reply to John One strand of the cumulative case

Luke opens Jesus' public ministry in the synagogue at Nazareth, reading Isaiah 61: "The Spirit of the Lord is on Me, because He has anointed Me to preach good news to the poor." Then he says, "Today this Scripture is fulfilled in your hearing" (Luke 4:18–21). What keeps the theme from being only Luke's is a second scene, which Matthew and Luke share. John the Baptist, in prison, sends to ask: "Are You the One who was to come, or should we look for someone else?" Jesus does not answer yes or no. He answers with a list: "The blind receive sight, the lame walk, the lepers are cleansed, the deaf hear, the dead are raised, and the good news is preached to the poor. Blessed is the one who does not fall away on account of Me" (Matthew 11:3–6; Luke 7:20–23). The list is largely drawn from Isaiah (35:5–6; 61:1) (the raising of the dead is not in Isaiah 35 or 61). Jesus invokes it as his credential. The tradition also has rough edges that a writer polishing a hero would be unlikely to add: the Baptist, whom the Gospels honour, is shown in doubt.

At least one Jewish text written before Jesus already made a similar connection. The fragmentary "Messianic Apocalypse" from Qumran (4Q521) opens with heaven and earth listening to "his anointed" and goes on to the deeds of the coming age. Who performs those deeds is debated. On the majority reading, God himself "makes the dead live," and specialists disagree over whether he acts through the anointed figure. Either way, the pairing is there before Jesus. In that setting Jesus' reply to John is not an evasion. It points to the signs of the anointed one's time and leaves John to draw the conclusion.

Honest limit: A concession is owed here. The Nazareth scene in this form is Luke's alone. Mark and Matthew tell of a rejection at Nazareth without the reading, and Luke has placed the episode at the head of the ministry as a kind of programme. Taken by itself, it could be Luke's theology rather than Jesus' own words. 4Q521 is cited as Jewish background to how Jesus' reply would have been heard, not as a source the Gospels used.

The kingdom and the Twelve One strand of the cumulative case

Announcing God's reign is not, by itself, claiming to be its king; prophets announced it too. What gives the message a royal shape is the movement Jesus built around it. He chose twelve men from among his followers (Mark 3:13–19), and the number was not accidental: Israel had twelve tribes. E. P. Sanders, no friend of easy harmonization, put the call of the Twelve on his short list of the most secure facts about Jesus. "I bestow on you a kingdom, just as My Father has bestowed one on Me, so that you may eat and drink at My table in My kingdom and sit on thrones, judging the twelve tribes of Israel" (Luke 22:29–30; compare Matthew 19:28). The Twelve are to judge Israel's tribes. The one who appoints them stands above them. Matthew's form promises "twelve thrones" to the disciples while Judas is still among them, which is not the kind of promise the church would have been likely to invent after the betrayal.

Honest limit: The common core in both forms is the promise of thrones and of judging the twelve tribes; the language of "My kingdom" and "My table" is Luke's. The saying does not use the title. It does describe a king's court.

A donkey: acting it out Debated

Jesus entered Jerusalem on a young donkey he had sent his disciples to fetch, and the crowd shouted, "Blessed is the coming kingdom of our father David!" (Mark 11:1–10). Zechariah had pictured Jerusalem's king arriving "humble and riding on a donkey" (Zechariah 9:9). Our essay on messianic prophecy calls this the clearest example of a stageable prophecy, one the Gospels themselves present Jesus as deliberately arranging. That is exactly why it is weak evidence of fulfilled prediction and strong evidence of a claim. A man who arranges to ride into the city of David on a donkey, days before Passover, in front of pilgrims who knew Zechariah, is not keeping his self-understanding to himself. He is acting it out.

Honest limit: How large and how public the scene was is debated — had it been a mass royal procession, one would expect Rome to have acted at once — and the argument needs only that Jesus chose the gesture.

A temple: the royal reading Inference

Shortly afterwards he drove the traders out of the temple courts, quoting Isaiah: "My house will be called a house of prayer for all the nations" (Mark 11:15–17; Isaiah 56:7). Around that act clusters a saying about destroying the temple and raising another, which surfaces as an accusation used against Jesus at his trial (Mark 14:58) and as a taunt at his execution (15:29). E. P. Sanders treated Jesus' conflict over the temple as among the most secure things we can know about him. Read against Israel's Scriptures, the act can carry a royal meaning. In Nathan's oracle it is David's heir who builds God's house: "He will build a house for My Name, and I will establish the throne of his kingdom forever" (2 Samuel 7:13); at Qumran, the Florilegium (4Q174) already read Nathan's promise as a promise about a coming figure.

Honest limit: Two limits apply. The exact form of the destroy-and-rebuild saying is debated, and Mark puts it on the lips of false witnesses; and Jesus nowhere explains his action by citing 2 Samuel 7. The royal reading is an inference, made by N. T. Wright among others, not a statement of the text. But it is the inference the setting invites, and in Mark it is straight after the temple testimony that the high priest asks whether Jesus is the Christ (14:57–61).

Objection — “the messianic secret” (Wrede) Most influential

Wrede noticed how often the Jesus of Mark commands silence: to demons who name him (Mark 3:11–12), to people he has healed (1:43–44; 5:43; 7:36), to the disciples after Peter's confession (8:29–30), and after the transfiguration, "until the Son of Man had risen from the dead" (9:9). He argued that these commands are not memories but a theological device: the church believed after Easter that Jesus was the Messiah, but it had inherited traditions of a life that had not been messianic; the secrecy motif reconciled the two.

Reply

The observation is sound; the theory built on it, we argue, does not hold. The commands are not one thing: many concern healings, not messiahship, and the healing commands do not work as concealment ("the more He ordered them, the more widely they proclaimed it," Mark 7:36). Much of Mark's messianic material is not secret at all: Bartimaeus shouts "Son of David" (Mark 10:46–52); the entry into Jerusalem and the temple action are public acts, and the answer at the trial is not hushed. And the reticence that is real has a better explanation: "Messiah" was a dangerous word (John 6:15). Mark has certainly shaped the secrecy motif, and nothing in this case rests on any one command being historical. But Wrede's theory explains the secrecy only by leaving the cross unexplained. A life with nothing messianic about it, neither claim nor acclaim, does not end under the charge "King of the Jews."

The room: William Wrede (The Messianic Secret, 1901) made the most influential case against the claim, and versions of his thesis are still held; Ulrich Luz distinguished a "miracle secret" from a "messianic secret," and J. D. G. Dunn argued that the secrecy in Mark has a historical rather than a purely theological origin. E. P. Sanders put the call of the Twelve and the temple controversy among the most secure facts; N. T. Wright reads the temple action as a messianic act.
Defending Premise 2

On Mark's account Jesus said "I am" — and Rome executed him under a royal charge

Precision check: the trial scene must be held with an honest caveat — the historicity of Mark's night trial before the Sanhedrin is itself disputed — and the argument does not stand or fall with it. The charge over the cross records what Rome concluded, not by itself what Jesus said. What it does make secure is the end of the story: Jesus died as a man Rome treated as a royal pretender.

"Are You the Christ?" Bounded

According to Mark — the earliest Gospel — the high priest asks directly, "Are You the Christ, the Son of the Blessed One?" Jesus answers, "I am, and you will see the Son of Man sitting at the right hand of Power and coming with the clouds of heaven" (Mark 14:61–62). (Matthew's parallel gives the more oblique "You have said it yourself"; some judge that indirect form more original.) Either way the question is put in messianic terms, and Jesus does not deny it. Before Pilate the same question returns in the form a Roman governor would use: "Are You the King of the Jews?" "You have said so" (Mark 15:2). Luke has the accusers spell out what the title meant to Rome: they charge him with "proclaiming Himself to be Christ, a King" (Luke 23:2).

A messianic claim does not appear, on its own, to have counted as blasphemy: about a century later, by rabbinic tradition, Akiba hailed the rebel Simon bar Kosiba as "King Messiah" and drew a colleague's ridicule, not a charge of blasphemy. So the blasphemy verdict in Mark turns on the second half of Jesus' answer, Daniel's clouds and Psalm 110's seat — which belongs to the question of deity, argued in Did Jesus Claim to Be God? For this question the first half is enough: on Mark's account, asked whether he was the Christ, Jesus said, "I am."

Honest limit: The historicity of Mark's night trial is disputed — Ehrman, among others, regards it as likely a later construction rather than a reported transcript. If the trial scene is set aside, the weight shifts back onto the acts already surveyed, and onto the charge Rome wrote over the cross.

"The King of the Jews" Bounded

The charge posted over Jesus named his offence, and it was a royal one. All four Gospels report it (Mark 15:26; Matthew 27:37; Luke 23:38; John 19:19), and its wording is telling. "King of the Jews" is not how Christians spoke of Jesus; they called him Christ and Lord. In the Gospels the phrase is on the lips of outsiders — the Magi, Pilate, the soldiers, and the titulus itself; the only Jewish leaders who use it are the chief priests of John 19:21, and they quote the inscription in order to object to it. Where Jews speak for themselves they say "King of Israel" (Mark 15:32; John 1:49; 12:13). It reads like a Roman formula, not a church confession. And Rome had reason to treat such a claim as sedition: after Herod the Great died in 4 BC, Josephus reports a run of men who reached for royal power in Judea and Galilee, and remarks that whenever a band of rebels found a leader, "he was created a king immediately." Pilate's charge places Jesus, in Rome's eyes, in that category, though he had raised no army.

Honest limit: The charge records what Rome concluded, not by itself what Jesus said, and that limit should be kept. What it does make secure is the end of the story: Jesus died as a man Rome treated as a royal pretender. The question is how the title came to be attached to him.

Objection — no one friendly to Jesus could have known what passed at the trial

The historicity of Mark's night trial before the Sanhedrin is itself disputed; on this view it is a later construction rather than a reported transcript.

Reply

The sharpest form of that doubt — that no one friendly to Jesus could have known what passed in the chamber — is itself overstated, for a transmission route need not be sympathetic: a follower was in the high priest's courtyard (Mark 14:54, 66–72); the tradition names a member of the council who, on Luke's account, had not consented to its verdict (Mark 15:43; Luke 23:50–51); and the movement soon drew in people with access to those circles, the trained Pharisee Paul among them (Acts 22:3; Galatians 1:13–14), and, Acts reports, priests and Pharisees (Acts 6:7; 15:5). None of that makes the scene a verbatim transcript, but it blunts the claim that its words could not possibly have been reported. The argument does not stand or fall with it.

The room: Martin Hengel (the standard study of crucifixion) and Josephus on the royal pretenders after Herod; Bart Ehrman doubts the night hearing, yet writes of "my claim that Jesus himself told the disciples that he thought he was the messiah" — on his reconstruction, a king in the kingdom God was about to bring, not a crucified one. Paula Fredriksen doubts that Jesus made the claim while agreeing that he died under it.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — the most influential is flagged below; the strongest form of the objection is Fredriksen's.

The most influential objection: William Wrede's "messianic secret" — that Jesus' silence commands in Mark are a theological device, and that belief in his messiahship began at the resurrection. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the reply below.

"The messianic secret shows Jesus' life was not messianic at all" Hard

Strongest form

The most influential case against all this was made in 1901 by the German scholar William Wrede. Wrede noticed how often the Jesus of Mark commands silence, and argued that these commands are not memories but a theological device. The church believed after Easter that Jesus was the Messiah, but it had inherited traditions of a life that had not been messianic; the secrecy motif reconciled the two by explaining why no one had recognised him at the time. Mark 9:9 is the key on this view: the secret lasts until the resurrection, which, Wrede held, is when belief in Jesus' messiahship began. Wrede changed the study of the Gospels, and versions of his thesis are still held.

Reply

The observation is sound; the theory built on it, we argue, does not hold. First, the commands are not one thing: many concern healings, not messiahship, and Ulrich Luz distinguished a "miracle secret" from a "messianic secret" in Mark. Second, much of Mark's messianic material is not secret at all — Bartimaeus, the entry, the temple action, the answer at the trial. A device meant to hide an unmessianic life has left the most messianic scenes in plain view. Third, the reticence that is real has a better explanation. "Messiah" was a dangerous word: after the feeding of the crowd Jesus withdrew, "realizing that they were about to come and make Him king by force" (John 6:15). A man who meant something other than a war on Rome had every reason to avoid the title in public and to redefine it in private, and that is what Mark shows: Peter confesses him as the Christ, and Jesus at once "began to teach them that the Son of Man must suffer many things" (8:29–31). Wrede's theory explains the secrecy only by leaving the cross unexplained.

"The church made him Messiah after Easter" Hard

Strongest form

Perhaps Jesus was a prophet of the kingdom and nothing more. His disciples, convinced after his death that God had raised him, read Psalm 110 ("Sit at My right hand") and Psalm 2 as describing his enthronement, concluded that he must be the Messiah, and read the title back into the story of his life. Some of the earliest Christian formulas can sound like this: God "made" him Lord and Christ (Acts 2:36), and he "was declared with power to be the Son of God by His resurrection from the dead" (Romans 1:4).

Reply

Suppose the first followers did become convinced that God had raised Jesus. In the categories of the time, that would mark him out as a vindicated martyr; it would not, by itself, show him to be the Messiah. Where Jews hoped for resurrection, they looked for the dead to be raised together at the end of the age (Daniel 12:2), but nothing in our sources treats the Messiah’s own rising from the dead as his sign. When popular talk held that Jesus was John the Baptist risen from the dead (Mark 6:14–16), no one concluded that the risen John was the Christ. So the title is very hard to derive from Easter alone. The apostles did read the resurrection as God's confirmation of it — God has "made this Jesus, whom you crucified, both Lord and Christ" (Acts 2:36) — but a confirmation needs something to confirm. Paul's formula has the same shape: the one "declared with power to be the Son of God by His resurrection" is already "His Son, who was a descendant of David according to the flesh" (Romans 1:3–4). The enthronement psalms could explain how the first Christians spoke of a messiahship they already believed in. They do not explain why they chose those psalms for a crucified man, unless the royal question had been raised before the cross. In Mark it is Jesus himself who twice reaches for Psalm 110 (12:35–37; 14:62).

"He never plainly said it — the crowds hailed him, he did not claim it" Strongest form

Strongest form

Jesus rarely calls himself Messiah in the Gospels. Paula Fredriksen, in Jesus of Nazareth, King of the Jews, argues that Jesus proclaimed the coming kingdom of God but did not publicly claim the title. On her reconstruction, on his last Passover visit his followers and the Passover crowds hailed him as Messiah, and Pilate executed him to stop the excitement from spreading; the excitement grew from his preaching that the kingdom was near. The account sets out to explain two things: the royal charge without a royal claim, and a puzzle any account must face, why Rome killed Jesus but did not round up his followers.

Reply

This objection is right about how often Jesus used the title, and that much should be conceded. The reply is that the acclamation has to come from somewhere, and in the Gospels it comes from something Jesus did. The crowd's shout about "the coming kingdom of our father David" answers an entry that, on Mark's account, Jesus himself arranged on Zechariah's pattern; the details of that arrangement may owe something to the evangelist's telling, but the reply to John and the appointing of the Twelve cannot be explained by a crowd in Jerusalem. An account in which others called him Messiah while he did nothing to invite it has to read each of them — the reply to John, the appointing of the Twelve, the entry, the temple action — as a prophet's sign rather than a king's, and set aside the answer at the trial. Part of Fredriksen's case should be granted: Jesus did not campaign for the title, and the crowds' enthusiasm may well have weighed with Pilate. Her puzzle about the disciples has an answer on our account too: Jesus led no armed rising, so Pilate could kill the claimant and let his followers go. The disagreement is over whether Jesus invited the hope placed on him, and the strands above say he did. That is a judgment of the best explanation, not a proof.

"The trial scene was invented — no one could know what was said" Trickier

Strongest form

The historicity of Mark's night trial before the Sanhedrin is itself disputed — Ehrman, among others, regards it as likely a later construction rather than a reported transcript. No one friendly to Jesus could have known what passed in the chamber.

Reply

Hold the scene with that honest caveat — and notice that the sharpest form of the doubt is itself overstated, for a transmission route need not be sympathetic: a follower was in the high priest's courtyard (Mark 14:54, 66–72); the tradition names a member of the council who, on Luke's account, had not consented to its verdict (Mark 15:43; Luke 23:50–51); and the movement soon drew in the trained Pharisee Paul (Acts 22:3; Galatians 1:13–14) and, Acts reports, priests and Pharisees (Acts 6:7; 15:5). None of that makes the scene a verbatim transcript. And the argument does not stand or fall with it. If the trial scene is set aside, the weight shifts back onto the acts already surveyed, and onto the charge Rome wrote over the cross.

"So this proves Jesus is God?" Common

Strongest form

Christians sometimes speak as if "Messiah" were itself a claim to deity: if Jesus claimed to be the Messiah, he claimed to be God.

Reply

No — this is not an argument that Jesus is God, and it should not be used as one; Christians sometimes speak as if 'Messiah' were itself a claim to deity, and that runs two questions together. "Son of David" carries the same freight as "Messiah": it names Jesus as the legitimate royal heir, the king of the line of David. None of these titles, taken in isolation, settles the question of divinity. A thoughtful first-century Jew could call a man "Messiah," "Son of David," even "Son of God" in the royal sense, while affirming the strictest monotheism. These titles are royal and human before they are anything more — which is not adoptionism, not that Jesus was a mere man later promoted to divine sonship: his unique, eternal Sonship is affirmed, and the point is only the titles' native range. That he also claimed more than royalty — to forgive sins as only God forgives, and to sit at God's right hand — is a separate argument, made in Did Jesus Claim to Be God? and The Titles of Jesus. The church confesses both: the Messiah of Israel is the eternal Son, fully God and fully man, two natures in one person.

"Even if he claimed it — many were hailed as kings, and the Messiah's expected work was not done" Hard

Strongest form

Many men in that century were hailed as kings, and their causes died with them. Jewish tradition has also weighed the claim by whether the work long expected of the Messiah — the ingathering of the exiles, a rebuilt temple, peace among the nations — was accomplished.

Reply

What this case does not establish is just as important. It shows that Jesus made a claim; it does not show that the claim was true. Whether Jesus' claim was true turns on what happened after the cross, and that case is made on its own evidence in our essays on the early creed and the minimal facts. Christians answer that the Messiah's work is begun and not yet finished, a question taken up in our essay on messianic prophecy. And the case must never be turned into a weapon against Judaism; it should be made in gratitude, not in triumph. We hold that the claim was true: that God raised the crucified Jesus and so vindicated the one Rome had executed as a pretender, and that the Messiah of Israel is the eternal Son, fully God and fully man, the Lord of all.

The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

This is a live question, and the field is divided. Wrede’s view, that the messianic claim belongs to the church rather than to Jesus, still has heirs, and Fredriksen doubts that Jesus made the claim while agreeing that he died under it. But the claim is not defended only by believers: Bart Ehrman, the best known of skeptical New Testament scholars, writes of “my claim that Jesus himself told the disciples that he thought he was the messiah” — on his reconstruction, a king in the kingdom God was about to bring, not a crucified one.

A fairness most defenders skip: Two limits apply to every side. All six strands reach us through the Gospels, so the case is a historian’s judgment of probability, not a demonstration. And those who agree that Jesus claimed the title still disagree about what he meant by it. Nor may the case ever be turned into a weapon against Judaism — as if his rejection by some authorities counted against his own people; division over a prophet is among the most Jewish features of the whole account, and the argument should be made in gratitude, not in triumph.

The bounded verdict: The distinction worth keeping is between a verdict and a consensus. The answer to the question is yes: Jesus presented himself as Israel’s Messiah, more in deed than in word, and he died for it. It shows that Jesus made a claim; it does not show that the claim was true. We hold that the claim was true: that God raised the crucified Jesus and so vindicated the one Rome had executed as a pretender, and that the Messiah of Israel is the eternal Son, fully God and fully man, the Lord of all. This essay has given its reasons for its verdict, and it does not pretend the field has settled the question.

    One strong way to answer — in a 1 Peter 3:15 tone

    "I'd grant you part of this: Jesus rarely used the title. And Mark has certainly shaped the secrecy motif. But suppose the first followers did become convinced that God had raised Jesus. In the categories of the time, that would mark him out as a vindicated martyr; it would not, by itself, show him to be the Messiah. Where Jews hoped for resurrection, they looked for the dead to be raised together at the end of the age (Daniel 12:2), but nothing in our sources treats the Messiah’s own rising from the dead as his sign. When popular talk held that Jesus was John the Baptist risen from the dead, no one concluded that the risen John was the Christ. So the title is very hard to derive from Easter alone. The apostles did read the resurrection as God's confirmation of it — Acts 2:36 — but a confirmation needs something to confirm. Paul's formula has the same shape: the one 'declared with power to be the Son of God by His resurrection' is already 'His Son, who was a descendant of David according to the flesh' (Romans 1:3–4). The enthronement psalms could explain how the first Christians spoke of a messiahship they already believed in. They do not explain why they chose those psalms for a crucified man, unless the royal question had been raised before the cross. Something like the claim was already attached to him before he died, at least in the charge Rome wrote over his cross, its way of naming a royal pretender: 'The King of the Jews.' And the reply to John and the appointing of the Twelve come from his Galilean ministry. Now — does that prove the claim was true? No. It shows that Jesus made a claim; it does not show that the claim was true. That turns on what happened after the cross. We hold that the claim was true: that God raised the crucified Jesus and so vindicated the one Rome had executed as a pretender, and that the Messiah of Israel is the eternal Son, fully God and fully man, the Lord of all."

    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    Neither saying distances him from the royal question. The riddle — “How can the scribes say that the Christ is the Son of David?” when “David himself calls Him ‘Lord’” (Mark 12:35–37, citing Psalm 110:1) — is a question that does not deny he is the son of David but presses the hearers to see more than they have seen. Jesus does not name himself in it, but the riddle has a point only if the question of the Messiah is live in that place and that week; and in the same Gospel, when blind Bartimaeus shouts “Son of David,” the crowd tries to silence him and Jesus calls him over and heals him (Mark 10:46–52). As for Solomon — David’s son, the king who built the temple — the Greek is indeed neuter, and some read it of the in-breaking kingdom rather than of Jesus himself; but in context the surpassing reality is embodied in the one who brings it, and Jesus lets the comparison rest on himself (Matthew 12:42; Luke 11:31). Where the riddle leads beyond royal sonship is a separate question, taken up in The Titles of Jesus.
