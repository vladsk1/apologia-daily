Daniel's 70 Weeks

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and stay clear and calm in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

Daniel 9:24-27 lays out a prophetic timeline. Starting from a decree to restore and rebuild Jerusalem, it counts off a span of "weeks" (units of seven) until the coming of "an Anointed One" (Messiah), who is then "cut off." After that, the text says, the city and the sanctuary are destroyed. The book of Daniel was written and circulating well before Jesus — copies survive among the Dead Sea Scrolls at Qumran.

The careful way to argue this is from the sequence, not from a precise day-count. Every mainstream reading shares the same order of events: decree, then the Anointed One arrives, then he is cut off, then Jerusalem and the Temple are destroyed. The destruction of the Temple is a fixed, securely known date: 70 AD. So on Daniel's own ordering, the Messiah had to have come and been "cut off" before 70 AD — a window in which Jesus of Nazareth is a striking fit. Whether you read it through Onias III or through Jesus, the shape is the same: an anointed death, then ruin. But only one candidate fits the whole of it.

Honest scope: this argument deliberately does not rest on the famous to-the-day calculation (such as Sir Robert Anderson's 173,880-day count), which is one influential interpretation, not a scholarly consensus, and depends on contested choices about the starting decree and the length of a year. Critical scholars also commonly date Daniel to the 2nd century BC. The modest, defensible claim is the sequence and its pre-70-AD horizon — not exact arithmetic.

The argument, formally

A timeline that lands the Messiah before 70 AD — on any mainstream reading.

P1

Daniel 9:24-27 sets a prophetic timeline running from a decree to restore Jerusalem to the coming of 'an Anointed One' (Messiah) — who is then 'cut off', after which the city and sanctuary are destroyed.

P2

The text was written and circulating well before Jesus — Daniel is preserved at Qumran — and its terminal event is the destruction of the city and the sanctuary (Dan 9:26). After Onias III's death Antiochus profaned the sanctuary but did not destroy it (1 Maccabees 4:52–54); the city and temple fell in the Roman destruction of 70 AD — so on Daniel's own order the Messiah arrives and is 'cut off' before it.

∴ C

Therefore the Messiah, on Daniel's own sequence, had to come and be cut off before 70 AD — and Jesus is the anointed one the prophecy foresaw (arriving in the Second Temple era, anointed, and cut off, before the sanctuary's end): no other candidate answers its whole shape as he does.

Be candid about what is and is not disputed: the famous 173,880-day calculation (Anderson) is one influential interpretation, not a consensus — and the argument here deliberately does not depend on it. The before-70-AD sequence does not need the arithmetic.

The key move — argue the sequence, not the day-count.

Conservative interpreters disagree over start-decrees and year-lengths — so build on the order the text itself lays out: decree, then an Anointed One, then he is cut off, then the city and sanctuary are destroyed. That terminal event — a destruction of Jerusalem and the Temple — fits one date in the era: 70 AD. Whoever the Messiah is, Daniel's own sequence requires him to have come and died before it.

Defending Premise 1

Daniel 9:24-27 sets a prophetic timeline running from a decree to restore Jerusalem to the coming of ‘an Anointed One’ (Messiah) — who is then ‘cut off’, after which the city and sanctuary are destroyed.

Precision check: “An anointed one” — mashiach, the word that becomes “Messiah.” Hebrew has no capital M; the term simply means “anointed,” and in the Old Testament it can name a king, a priest, or even the pagan Cyrus (Isaiah 45:1). So Daniel 9 does not say “the Messiah” in the later technical sense. It says an anointed one comes, and then an anointed one is “cut off.” The famous 173,880-day calculation (Anderson) is one influential interpretation, not a consensus — and the argument here deliberately does not depend on it. The before-70-AD sequence does not need the arithmetic.

The order is in the text The core warrant

“And after the sixty-two weeks, an anointed one shall be cut off and shall have nothing. And the people of the prince who is to come shall destroy the city and the sanctuary…” (Daniel 9:26, ESV). Messiah’s death precedes the destruction in the text’s own order.

The subject: an anointed one who is “cut off” Identity contested

Mashiach — anointed one — with karat, ‘cut off’: a dying Anointed One, in the Hebrew Bible, centuries before a crucified Messiah was preached. The verb (karat) is used elsewhere for violent death and judicial execution.

Honest limit: The whole interpretive war is about who these figures are. Daniel 9 genuinely seems to expect an Anointed One within the Second Temple era, and to expect him to be killed — that is the claim, no more.

The shape of the whole Suggestive

The qualitative force is real. Daniel 9 expects an anointed figure, ties his coming to a Persian rebuilding decree, places his violent death before the destruction of the city and temple, and dates the whole to within the Second Temple period. Jerusalem and its temple were destroyed, in AD 70, by “the people of the prince” — the Romans. For a text composed no later than the second century BC, that is a striking convergence even before anyone counts a single day.

The structural reading underneath the math is shared far beyond dispensationalism: the venerable view, held by Calvin, E. W. Hengstenberg, and Edward J. Young alike, is simply that the 69 weeks run from a Persian decree to the public ministry and death of Christ in the first century AD — no 360-day gymnastics required, the rough order of magnitude being the point.

Which decree starts the clock? Not cleanly settled

“From the going out of the word to restore and build Jerusalem.” Which word? Scripture records more than one Persian decree, and the candidates land decades apart: Cyrus’s decree, c. 538 BC (Ezra 1) — but it authorizes the temple, not the city; Artaxerxes I’s decree to Ezra, his seventh year, 458/457 BC (Ezra 7); Artaxerxes I’s authorization to Nehemiah, his twentieth year, 445/444 BC (Nehemiah 2) — the one that explicitly rebuilds the walls and gates.

Honest limit: The “decree” question is not cleanly settled. The text’s “word to restore and build Jerusalem” most naturally evokes the rebuilding of the city, which favors Nehemiah’s 444 commission — but Daniel 9:25 may instead refer to the divine word through Jeremiah, not any Persian edict at all, which is the critical default.

Objection — the Masoretic accent splits the weeks

The Masoretes placed a major dividing accent (the atnach) after “seven weeks” in verse 25, which reads as: “from the word… to an anointed one, a prince, seven weeks; and for sixty-two weeks it shall be built.” On that reading the anointed one arrives after only 49 years — the era of the return, not the time of Jesus — and the 7 and 62 are two separate periods. Modern critical translations such as the NRSV follow the Masoretic accent; the King James and NIV traditions join the 7 and 62 into 69 to reach the first century, following the earlier Greek versions (Septuagint, Theodotion) and the Vulgate, which did not separate them.

Reply

This is a genuinely contested philological point, but it matters less than it first appears. Even on the Masoretic division, verse 26 still places the anointed one’s cutting-off “after the sixty-two weeks,” which follow the first seven. What the accent changes is the identity of the figure in verse 25, not the timing of the death in verse 26.

Objection — “Anderson’s 173,880 days is numerology”

The to-the-day calculation needs a 360-day ‘prophetic year,’ a contested decree, and a chosen crucifixion date — assumptions stacked to hit a target. The precision is manufactured. There is no statement in Daniel that its years are 360 days; the inference is imported from Revelation, and the suspicion is hard to shake that the 360-day figure was selected precisely because 365-day years do not produce a clean landing on Jesus.

Reply

Largely agreed — which is why this argument refuses to stand on it. Drop every disputed assumption and argue only the sequence all readings share: Anointed One comes and is cut off before the destruction of city and sanctuary. The destruction happened in 70 AD. The honest apologist retires the stopwatch.

The room: The venerable first-century-AD reading is held by Calvin, E. W. Hengstenberg, and Edward J. Young; Anderson and Hoehner built the day-precise calculations on it, which the argument here does not use. J. Paul Tanner defends the joined reading of the weeks and treats both the 457 and 444 BC reckonings as viable. On the critical side, John J. Collins (Daniel, Hermeneia) is the standard scholarly commentary, and the majority of academic Old Testament scholars do not read Daniel 9 as pointing to Jesus at all.
Defending Premise 2

The text was written and circulating well before Jesus — Daniel is preserved at Qumran — and its terminal event is the destruction of the city and the sanctuary (Dan 9:26). After Onias III’s death Antiochus profaned the sanctuary but did not destroy it (1 Maccabees 4:52–54); the city and temple fell in the Roman destruction of 70 AD — so on Daniel’s own order the Messiah arrives and is ‘cut off’ before it.

Precision check: Our claim is not that Daniel 9 calculates Jesus to the day. It is that Jesus is the anointed one the prophecy foresaw (arriving in the Second Temple era, anointed, and cut off, before the sanctuary’s end) and that no other candidate answers its whole shape as he does. The premise needs a text that even its critics date to no later than the second century BC — it does not need the critical date to be wrong.

Qumran: the prophecy verifiably predates Jesus Strong

Fragments of Daniel were found at Qumran, and the book was treated as authoritative scripture there soon after its supposed 165 BC composition.

Honest limit: Conservatives argue this presses an uncomfortably short window for a freshly written work to gain such status, though critics judge the gap sufficient (the earliest fragments are dated paleographically to around 125 BC). The Maccabean thesis is the majority view but not a proof.

The terminal event: city and sanctuary destroyed Suggestive

Whether you read it through Onias III or through Jesus, the shape is the same: an anointed death, then ruin. But only one candidate fits the whole of it. After Onias III’s death, Antiochus’s forces sacked and burned parts of the city and profaned the sanctuary (1 Maccabees 1:31, 39), but the sanctuary itself was not destroyed and was rededicated in 164 BC (1 Maccabees 4:52–54); after Jesus’s, the city and its temple were destroyed, by “the people of the prince” (verse 26), in AD 70.

Honest limit: That is a suggestive strand, not a proof. It is the kind of evidence that rewards the person already weighing the whole cloth of the case for Christ, and frustrates the person demanding a single decisive thread.

The closing window Suggestive

Whether one starts at 457 or 444 BC, the weeks run out in the first century — and 70 AD closes the window.

Ancient Jewish interpretation: the horizon is the Second Temple’s end Narrow point only

The rabbinic chronicle Seder Olam Rabbah (compiled c. AD 160 and edited later) measured 490 years from the first temple’s fall to the second’s, mapping Daniel’s seventy weeks onto the lifespan of the Second Temple. Josephus, an eyewitness to AD 70, likewise treats Daniel as a prophet whose visions were most naturally read as pointing to the Roman desolation of the sanctuary.

Honest limit: The rabbis read those weeks non-messianically, and on a famously compressed Persian chronology; this is no Jewish endorsement of the Christian reading. The narrow point it does establish is only this: Jewish tradition itself tied Daniel 9’s clock to the end of the Second Temple era. Neither source reads Daniel 9 as Christians do — but both locate its horizon at the end of the Second Temple era, which is precisely the era of Jesus.

Objection — “Daniel was written in 165 BC — prophecy after the fact”

Critical scholarship dates Daniel to the Maccabean crisis; its ‘predictions’ describe Antiochus retrospectively. The critical consensus — represented by John J. Collins’s Daniel in the Hermeneia series, the standard scholarly commentary — holds that Daniel reached its final form around 165 BC, during the persecution of the Jews by the Seleucid king Antiochus IV Epiphanes. Daniel 11 narrates the wars of the Hellenistic kingdoms with such accuracy that critics read it as history written after the fact (vaticinium ex eventu).

Reply

The Maccabean reader ties verse 26 to Antiochus — so meet them on the text. Daniel’s terminal event is that “the people of the prince who is to come shall destroy the city and the sanctuary” — a destruction of both, which fits 70 AD and strains the Maccabean frame. And even granting the late date, 165 BC is still two centuries before that destruction: the relevant prediction remains a prediction. Conservative scholars press the dating on other grounds—the short Qumran-reception window and disputed linguistic arguments—while granting that the apparent break in Daniel 11’s accuracy at Antiochus IV’s death is the critic’s strongest datum.

Objection — the Maccabean reading: Onias III and the numbers Hardest

On this reading the seventy weeks are not a stopwatch but a theological structure: ten jubilees of years (490 = 70 × 7, and 49 × 10), a symbolic span answering Jeremiah’s seventy years. The “anointed one cut off” of verse 26 is Onias III, the legitimate Zadokite high priest deposed and then murdered around 171 BC; the desolator of verse 27 is Antiochus IV, who halted the temple sacrifices and erected the “abomination of desolation” from 167 to 164 BC. And the chronology: the first seven weeks fit the critic’s reading well (587 to 538 BC), but the 434 years of the sixty-two weeks overshoot the time to Onias III’s murder by some sixty-five to seventy years — the author’s numbers, Collins argues, are simply inexact, schematic figures, as we should expect from apocalyptic literature trading in symbolic completeness rather than chronometry.

Reply

The messianic reading and the Maccabean reading are not symmetrical guesses: the Maccabean reading fits the book’s own preoccupations and requires no special arithmetic, while the messianic reading requires a particular decree, a particular punctuation, and (for the day-precise version) a particular year-length. Those are real interpretive choices. But the Maccabean reading is not dial-free either: it must identify the “anointed one” who is “cut off” with Onias III, rely on its own inexact numbers (which overshoot the era they aim at by some sixty-five to seventy years), and take the same disputed punctuation the other way. That the numbers are “inexact” cuts both ways: if the author of a Maccabean Daniel was writing about events in his own lifetime, his inability to make 490 years reach cleanly from any starting point to 164 BC is itself a little awkward — he, of all people, knew when Antiochus desecrated the temple. Neither reading simply falls out of the text; the claim to resist is that the non-messianic reading is the effortless default and the messianic one a contrivance.

The room: John J. Collins’s Hermeneia commentary is the standard scholarly statement of the Maccabean setting (c. 165 BC) and of the Onias III identification, which is standard in critical scholarship (see also John E. Goldingay, Daniel). Conservative scholars press the dating on the short Qumran-reception window and disputed linguistic arguments, while granting the break in Daniel 11 is the critic’s strongest datum. The rabbinic Seder Olam Rabbah and Josephus are cited only for the narrow point about the Second Temple horizon.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: the critical reading — Collins's Maccabean date, Onias III as the anointed one cut off, and the Masoretic accent. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"Anderson’s 173,880 days is numerology" Common

Strongest form

The to-the-day calculation needs a 360-day 'prophetic year,' a contested decree, and a chosen crucifixion date — assumptions stacked to hit a target. The precision is manufactured.

Reply

Largely agreed — which is why this argument refuses to stand on it. Drop every disputed assumption and argue only the sequence all readings share: Anointed One comes and is cut off before the destruction of city and sanctuary. The destruction happened in 70 AD. The window closed nineteen centuries ago; the question 'who, then, was he?' remains — without a single day counted.

"Daniel was written in 165 BC — prophecy after the fact" Common

Strongest form

Critical scholarship dates Daniel to the Maccabean crisis; its 'predictions' describe Antiochus retrospectively. A late book is no oracle.

Reply

The Maccabean reader ties verse 26 to Antiochus — so meet them on the text. After Onias III's death, Antiochus's forces sacked and burned parts of the city and profaned the sanctuary (1 Maccabees 1:31, 39), but the sanctuary itself was not destroyed and was rededicated in 164 BC (1 Maccabees 4:52–54). But Daniel's terminal event is that "the people of the prince who is to come shall destroy the city and the sanctuary" — a destruction of both, which fits 70 AD and strains the Maccabean frame. And even granting the late date, 165 BC is still two centuries before that destruction: the relevant prediction remains a prediction. Conservative scholars press the dating on other grounds—the short Qumran-reception window and disputed linguistic arguments—while granting that the apparent break in Daniel 11's accuracy at Antiochus IV's death is the critic's strongest datum.

"The anointed one is Onias III, the murdered high priest" Trickier

Strongest form

Verse 26's cut-off anointed one fits Onias III, assassinated in 171 BC — a known event in the Maccabean frame. No Jesus required.

Reply

Onias fits a clause, not the architecture: his death restored nothing in verse 24's list — finishing transgression, atoning for iniquity, bringing everlasting righteousness — and after his death the sanctuary itself was not destroyed and was rededicated in 164 BC (1 Maccabees 4:52–54). The sequence's terminal event is the sanctuary's destruction — which belongs to 70 AD, not 171 BC. The Maccabean reading is not dial-free either: it must identify the "anointed one" who is "cut off" with Onias III, rely on its own inexact numbers (which overshoot the era they aim at by some sixty-five to seventy years), and take the same disputed punctuation the other way.

"Weeks of years is itself an interpretive leap" Trickier

Strongest form

Shavuim just means sevens. Years, days, jubilee cycles — the unit is supplied by the interpreter, and your whole timeline hangs on the choice.

Reply

The unit is supplied by the context: Daniel has just been praying about Jeremiah's seventy YEARS (9:2), and the angel answers with seventy sevens — a deliberate escalation of the same unit; literal weeks would end the prophecy in 16 months, absurd against its content. Years is not the apologist's choice; it is the reading of virtually all interpreters, Jewish and Christian, ancient and modern.

"Translations differ — some have two anointed ones and no Messiah" Hard

Strongest form

The RSV reads 'an anointed one, a prince' in v25 and another in v26, with punctuation splitting the weeks — priestly figures, lowercase, centuries apart. Your messianic reading rides on Christian punctuation.

Reply

The punctuation dispute (Masoretic accents vs older versions) is real — and the argument survives both readings: on either, v26 still has an anointed one cut off, followed by the destruction of city and sanctuary. The sequence and its 70 AD terminus are punctuation-proof. The rabbinic chronicle Seder Olam Rabbah (compiled c. AD 160 and edited later) measured 490 years from the first temple's fall to the second's, mapping Daniel's seventy weeks onto the lifespan of the Second Temple. The rabbis read those weeks non-messianically; this is no Jewish endorsement of the Christian reading. Neither Seder Olam nor Josephus reads Daniel 9 as Christians do — but both locate its horizon at the end of the Second Temple era, which is precisely the era of Jesus.

"Even granting it all — it shows a window, not Jesus" Hard

Strongest form

Suppose Messiah had to come and die before 70 AD. Thousands died then. The prophecy buys you a deadline, not an identification.

Reply

Correct — and that is exactly the argument's honest scope: Daniel supplies the deadline; the identification comes from the convergence. Within that window, no other candidate answers its whole shape as he does: Jesus came, was 'cut off' by execution, and was followed within a generation by the destruction Daniel orders next — which he himself predicted. A deadline plus a fit that satisfies the full description plus the sequel on schedule is how identifications work.
