The Trinity in the New Testament

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and stay clear and calm in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

The doctrine of the Trinity holds that there is one God who exists eternally in three persons — the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit. This is not three gods (tritheism), nor one person wearing three masks (modalism), but one divine being in three distinct, co-equal, co-eternal persons. The word "Trinity" is not in the Bible, but the question this page addresses is whether the teaching itself is rooted in the New Testament.

The case is cumulative. The New Testament affirms that there is only one God, then repeatedly speaks of the Father as God, identifies Jesus as God (for example John 1:1, John 20:28, Titus 2:13), and treats the Holy Spirit as a divine person (Acts 5:3-4). It also places the three together as equals — most famously in the baptismal formula of Matthew 28:19, "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit," and in Paul's benediction in 2 Corinthians 13:14. Hold those threads together and the Trinity is the doctrine that makes sense of all of them at once.

Sceptics fairly note that no single verse states the full doctrine, and that it was clarified over later centuries. That is true. The Christian reply is that the doctrine is the most faithful synthesis of what the New Testament already teaches — the Church later named and defended what the apostolic writings had affirmed all along. So the claim that the New Testament is itself unitarian does not hold. And this is what we hold to be true: the one God exists eternally as Father, Son, and Holy Spirit.

Before you start · key terms

Eight words this argument leans on

Learn these first — the objections below turn on them, and most weak versions of the argument come from blurring one of them.

The three givensThe argument stated almost as a syllogism. First given: there is exactly one God. Second given: the Father is God, the Son is God, and the Holy Spirit is God. Third given: the Father, Son, and Spirit are really distinct — they address one another, send one another, relate to one another. The Trinity is simply the doctrine that holds all three givens together without dropping any.
One being, three personsThe classical, creedal form: one God who exists eternally as three persons — Father, Son, and Holy Spirit — each fully and equally God, yet genuinely distinct. One being; three persons. It is not tritheism (three gods), not modalism (one person wearing three masks), not Arianism (a supreme God plus a created, lesser Son), and not partialism (three thirds). What God is is one; who God is is three.
Binitarian devotionLarry Hurtado's term for the pattern Christians showed from astonishingly early — within the first two decades after the crucifixion — directing to Jesus the cultic devotion Jews reserved for God alone. Hurtado calls it an “unprecedented” innovation within Jewish monotheism rather than a drift away from it: the worship arose inside the one-God confession, not outside it.
Divine identityRichard Bauckham's proposal that Second Temple Jews defined their one God by what only he does — he alone created all things, he alone rules all things — and that the New Testament writers deliberately place Jesus on the divine side of that line. The proposal is enormously influential, though the divine-identity category is itself genuinely contested: James D. G. Dunn and James F. McGrath judge that it reads later precision back into more developmental first-century language, and Hurtado preferred to frame the same data as devotional practice.
The reworked Shema (1 Corinthians 8:6)Paul takes the central Jewish confession of the one Lord God and distributes it between “one God, the Father” and “one Lord, Jesus Christ” — not adding a second god but, on N. T. Wright's reading, expanding the Shema from within to include the Son. That reading is influential and arguably dominant, yet genuinely contested: James F. McGrath argues “one Lord” is set alongside the creed rather than folded into it.
The triadic textsFormulas naming the three together, which the church did not have to invent because it inherited them from worship: baptism “in the name” — singular — “of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit” (Matthew 28:19); the grace of 2 Corinthians 13:14; “one Spirit… one Lord… one God” (Ephesians 4:4–6). Not developed dogma, but the raw triadic grammar the doctrine of the Trinity was later coined to explain.
The economy (subordination in role, not in being)The distinction classical orthodoxy draws between the eternal nature the persons share and the order in which they act in salvation — what theologians call the economy. In the mission the Son is genuinely sent, obeys, and prays: a subordination of order and role. It is not a subordination in deity, nature, or eternity (that is Arianism); the persons are co-equal and co-eternal in being, distinguished by relations of origin that are real and eternal, not roles the one God adopts.
Biblical unitarianismThe strongest contemporary non-Trinitarian reading, represented most prominently by the analytic philosopher Dale Tuggy: not modalism, not Arianism, and not a denial of Scripture's authority, but the claim that the New Testament is itself unitarian — that the one God simply is the Father and no one else is, that Jesus is his uniquely exalted human Messiah, and that the Spirit is God's active presence.
The argument, formally

Three lines of data the doctrine was minted to hold together.

P1

The New Testament teaches uncompromised monotheism ('there is no God but one') while presenting three who are each fully God: the Father; the Son (John 1:1, 20:28; Titus 2:13; Hebrews 1); the Spirit (lying to the Spirit is lying to God, Acts 5:3-4).

P2

The three are genuinely distinct persons — the Son prays TO the Father, the Father SENDS the Son, the Spirit is ANOTHER Advocate — yet stand in one divine Name, singular, at baptism (Matt 28:19).

∴ C

Therefore the Trinity is not imposed on the New Testament — it is the reading that keeps all the data at once — one God, three persons — where every rival buys its simplicity by dropping a line of it.

Frame it as a data problem: unitarianism deletes the deity texts, tritheism deletes the one-God texts, modalism deletes the distinction texts. The doctrine is what remains when nothing is deleted.

The key concept — doctrine as best fit to stubborn data.

The church did not start with philosophy and bend texts to it; it started with worship-shaped facts — praying to Jesus, baptising in a threefold Name, calling the Spirit God — and spent centuries finding the formulation that loses none of them. One essence, three persons is the line of best fit.

What follows — and what doesn’t

Don’t read the conclusion — reason to it

Working only from what the argument has established: what does each piece of evidence rule out, and how far does the inference reach? Reason each line out, then reveal.

The New Testament does not soften Jewish monotheism to fit Jesus in — “there is no God but one,” and Jesus himself recites the Shema. If anything it sharpens it. So when these same uncompromising monotheists worship Jesus, that worship is…?
startling — and it needs explainingIt is precisely because the monotheism is uncompromising that the worship of Jesus becomes so startling, and the doctrine of the Trinity so necessary. Hurtado documents it from within the first two decades after the crucifixion: the cultic devotion Jews reserved for God alone, directed to Jesus — an unprecedented innovation within Jewish monotheism, not a drift away from it.
One God; Father, Son and Spirit each treated as God; the three genuinely distinct and relating. Each of the three givens is actually taught. So the Trinity is…?
the reading that keeps all three at onceThe Trinity is simply the doctrine that holds all three givens together without dropping any. Every rival buys its simplicity by dropping a line: unitarianism deletes the deity texts, tritheism deletes the one-God texts, modalism deletes the texts of distinction.
The Son genuinely prays to the Father, is genuinely sent, genuinely obeys. Does that make him lesser in deity?
no — lesser in role within the mission, not in beingClassical orthodoxy not only allows the subordination but insists on it — of order and role within the mission of salvation, the economy. It is not a subordination in being: the persons are co-equal and co-eternal, distinguished by relations of origin, not by rank. “The Father is greater than I” is spoken by the one who, having been “in the form of God,” took “the form of a servant” (Philippians 2:6–7) — greater in position within the incarnate mission, not greater in deity. To read role as nature is the Arianism the church rejected.
“Baptize in the name — singular — of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit.” The church did not have to compose that formula; it inherited it. Where did it come from, and what does that show?
from worship — the grammar of worship is three-in-one before philosophy isOne name; three who share it. The triadic texts — Matthew 28:19, the grace of 2 Corinthians 13:14, “one Spirit… one Lord… one God” — are the raw triadic grammar the church inherited from worship, not a developed dogma. They are what the doctrine of the Trinity was later coined to explain.
“Trinity,” “person,” “essence” are all post-biblical. So is the doctrine just a later invention?
no — absence of a label is not absence of the thing“Monotheism,” “omnipotence,” “incarnation,” even “Bible” are not in Scripture either; summary terms name what a body of texts teaches. The data the word gathers is already present — one God; Father, Son and Spirit each God; the three distinct — demanding exactly the synthesis the word names. The worship came first; the word came later.
Grant every given. Does the New Testament evidence, by itself, prove the Nicene formula the way a theorem is proved?
no — it shows the doctrine is the most faithful reading, not the only one a thoughtful person could reachThe New Testament does not hand us the doctrine in finished technical form; it hands us the materials — the three givens — and the church spent three centuries drawing out the formulation that holds them. The argument establishes that the Trinity is the most faithful reading of the data, not that no thoughtful person could read it otherwise. The positive case rests on revelation and the resurrection.
Defending Premise 1

The New Testament teaches uncompromised monotheism (‘there is no God but one’) while presenting three who are each fully God: the Father; the Son (John 1:1, 20:28; Titus 2:13; Hebrews 1); the Spirit (lying to the Spirit is lying to God, Acts 5:3-4).

Precision check: the doctrine is not tritheism — three gods; not modalism — one person wearing three masks; not Arianism — a supreme God plus a created, lesser Son; and not partialism — three thirds that add up to one God. The three are not parts of God, nor roles God plays, nor a ranking of greater and lesser divinities. What God is is one; who God is is three. The argument runs on three givens: there is exactly one God; the Father is God, the Son is God, and the Holy Spirit is God; and the three are really distinct.

First given — the New Testament keeps strict monotheism Strong

It would be a serious mistake to think the New Testament softens Jewish monotheism to make room for Jesus. If anything, it sharpens it. “There is no God but one,” Paul writes (1 Corinthians 8:4). The demons, James notes acidly, believe that God is one — and shudder (James 2:19). Jesus himself, asked for the greatest commandment, recites the Shema (Mark 12:29). Whatever the early Christians were claiming about Jesus, they were not abandoning the conviction that the God of Israel is the sole creator and the only proper object of worship.

It is precisely because the monotheism is uncompromising that the worship of Jesus becomes so startling. Hurtado documents this tension: from astonishingly early — within the first two decades after the crucifixion — Christians directed to Jesus the cultic devotion that Jews reserved for God alone, producing what he called a “binitarian” pattern of worship.

Second given — the Son Strong · a convergence, not one proof text

Thomas’s ‘my Lord and my God’ accepted and blessed (Jesus’ next words pronounce a blessing, not a correction); ‘our great God and Saviour Jesus Christ’ (Titus 2:13); Hebrews 1 — the Son addressed as God and as the unchanging LORD who founded the earth (Psalm 102 applied). The deity of the Son does not rest on a single proof text but on a convergence of many.

The most penetrating recent work locates Jesus not merely among lofty titles but within the identity of the one God. Richard Bauckham argues that Second Temple Jews defined their one God by what only he does — he alone created all things, he alone rules all things — and that the New Testament writers deliberately place Jesus on the divine side of that line. The clearest case is Paul’s reworking of the Shema itself in 1 Corinthians 8:6: “for us there is one God, the Father… and one Lord, Jesus Christ.” As N. T. Wright has argued, Paul has taken the central Jewish confession of the one Lord God and, astonishingly, distributed it between the Father and Jesus—not adding a second god, but expanding the Shema from within to include the Son.

Honest limit: Bauckham’s proposal that the New Testament writers deliberately place Jesus within the divine identity has been enormously influential but remains genuinely contested: James D. G. Dunn and James F. McGrath, among others, judge that “divine identity” reads later precision back into more developmental first-century language. The precise relation of 1 Corinthians 8:6 to the Shema — splitting, supplementing, or “brokering” — remains debated.

Second given — the Spirit Strong

The Spirit speaks YHWH’s first-person words (Acts 28:25 / Isaiah 6); blasphemy against him is named the one sin not forgiven (Matt 12:31-32) — strange gravity for a force. Peter tells Ananias, “You have lied to the Holy Spirit” — and in the next breath, “You have not lied to men but to God” (Acts 5:3–4). To lie to the Spirit simply is to lie to God. Gordon Fee’s exhaustive study of the Spirit in Paul concludes that for Paul the Spirit is no impersonal force but a fully personal divine presence — “God’s empowering presence.”

Honest limit: the deity of the Spirit is established more by what the Spirit is and does than by the word “God” applied as a label.

Objection — the biblical-unitarian case: the one God is the Father alone Hardest

Contemporary biblical unitarianism — represented most prominently by the analytic philosopher Dale Tuggy — is not modalism, not Arianism, and not a denial that the New Testament is authoritative. It is the claim that the New Testament is itself unitarian: that the one God simply is the Father, and no one else is. The texts that most clearly say “the only true God” identify that God as the Father: Jesus prays to the Father as “the only true God” and distinguishes himself from him (John 17:3); Paul writes that “there is one God, the Father… and one Lord, Jesus Christ” — one God, says Tuggy, is reserved for the Father, while Jesus gets the lesser title Lord (1 Corinthians 8:6). The clean, simple reading, on the unitarian’s telling, is that the Father alone is God, Jesus is his uniquely exalted human Messiah, and the Spirit is God’s active presence. The Trinity, Tuggy argues, is not what the apostles taught but a fourth-century construction.

Reply

The unitarian reading purchases its simplicity by deleting data. Take John 17:3, the unitarian’s strongest verse. Two verses later, the same Jesus asks the Father to glorify him with the glory he had “before the world existed” (John 17:5) — glory that Isaiah says YHWH shares with no other (Isaiah 42:8), in the same Gospel that ends with Thomas saying “my Lord and my God.” John 17:3 excludes the idols, not the Son; it is monotheism against paganism, not the Father against Jesus. The same move answers 1 Corinthians 8:6: as Wright and Bauckham observe, Paul is not demoting Jesus by calling him “Lord” but including him in the Shema, for “Lord” (kyrios) is the Greek translation’s standard rendering of the divine name YHWH. That reading of the verse is influential, arguably dominant, and genuinely contested: James F. McGrath argues that “one Lord” is set alongside the creed rather than folded into it. Unitarianism must explain away the deity texts and the worship of Jesus that Hurtado documents from the earliest period. So the claim that the New Testament is itself unitarian does not hold: it keeps the first given only by setting aside the second.

Objection — “The Spirit is only God’s power”

The Spirit, the unitarian adds, is regularly spoken of in impersonal terms — poured out, given by measure, the very “power” of God (Luke 1:35; 24:49).

Reply

The Spirit in the New Testament speaks (Acts 13:2), teaches (John 14:26), grieves (Ephesians 4:30), intercedes (Romans 8:26), and can be lied to (Acts 5:3–4). Forces don’t grieve, teach, or get lied to.

The room: Hurtado (devotion from the earliest layer), Bauckham (Jesus within the identity of the one God), Wright (1 Corinthians 8:6 and the Shema), Fee (the personal Spirit); Dunn and McGrath question the “divine identity” framing; Dale Tuggy presses the unitarian reading.
Defending Premise 2

The three are genuinely distinct persons — the Son prays TO the Father, the Father SENDS the Son, the Spirit is ANOTHER Advocate — yet stand in one divine Name, singular, at baptism (Matt 28:19).

Precision check: the third given forbids collapsing the three into one another — but it does not rank them. The three are not parts of God, nor roles God plays, nor a ranking of greater and lesser divinities. The distinction the doctrine draws is between the eternal nature the persons share and the order in which they act in salvation — what theologians call the economy; the persons are co-equal and co-eternal in being; they are distinguished by relations of origin that are real and eternal, not roles the one God adopts.

Distinction — the persons relate Strong

At the baptism all three act at once — Son in the water, Father’s voice, Spirit descending (Mark 1:9–11); in Gethsemane the Son prays TO the Father — real address to one who is really other, the incarnate Son’s human will yielded to the Father’s (Luke 22:42); the Father sends the Son into the world (John 3:17), and the Son promises to send ‘another Advocate’ — the Spirit — a phrase that distinguishes the Spirit from himself even as it puts the Spirit in his own class (John 14:16). One who prays to another, is sent by another, and sends another is not a mask the others wear. Persons relate; modes cannot.

Unity — the triadic texts Raw grammar, not a developed dogma

One Name at Matt 28:19 — to baptize “in the name” — singular — “of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit.” One name; three who share it. The triadic grace of 2 Cor 13:14; one throne in Revelation shared by God and the Lamb. The grammar of worship is three-in-one before the grammar of philosophy is. Paul also lays out the church’s gifts and unity in a threefold pattern: “the same Spirit… the same Lord… the same God” (1 Corinthians 12:4–6), and “one Spirit… one Lord… one God and Father of all” (Ephesians 4:4–6).

Honest limit: none of these is a developed dogma. But they are the raw triadic grammar — Father, Son, Spirit, named as equals in the same breath — that the doctrine of the Trinity was later coined to explain.

Objection — why not one person who appears as Father, Son, and Spirit?

Why can’t Christians just say God is one person who appears as Father, Son, and Spirit?

Reply

That view, called modalism, cannot keep the New Testament’s texts of distinction. The Son prays to the Father in Gethsemane, is sent by the Father, and promises to send another Advocate, the Spirit. One who prays to another, is sent by another, and sends another is not a single person wearing three masks. The persons genuinely relate to one another, so the doctrine holds one being in three distinct persons, not one person in three modes.

Objection — the New Testament subordinates the Son

The New Testament constantly subordinates the Son: he is sent, he obeys, he does not know the day or hour (Mark 13:32), the Father is “greater than I” (John 14:28), and in the end the Son himself will be subjected to the Father (1 Corinthians 15:28).

Reply

Several of these facts must be granted outright: the New Testament does subordinate the Son in role — he is genuinely sent, genuinely obeys, genuinely prays. This is a subordination of order and role in the mission of salvation, not a claim that the Son is lesser in deity, nature, or eternity (that is Arianism); the persons are co-equal and co-eternal in being, and only in the mission is there an order of sending and obeying. “The Father is greater than I” is spoken by the Son who has, in Paul’s words, taken “the form of a servant,” having been “in the form of God” (Philippians 2:6–7); greater in position, within the incarnate mission, not greater in deity. When Jesus says that “of that day or that hour no one knows… nor the Son, but only the Father” (Mark 13:32), he speaks as the incarnate Son who has genuinely assumed a human nature — and with it a human mind that grows in wisdom and can be ignorant (Luke 2:52); the ignorance belongs to his human mind, not to his divine nature. As for 1 Corinthians 15:28, it is the incarnate Son, his mediatorial work complete, who hands the kingdom back so that “God may be all in all”: an eschatological ordering of the mission, not a ranking of the eternal Son beneath the Father in nature.

The room: Trinitarian philosophers (from Brian Leftow’s “Latin” models to the “social” models of figures like Cornelius Plantinga Jr. and William Lane Craig) continue to debate the best account among themselves.
Defending the conclusion

The Trinity is not imposed on the New Testament — it is the reading that keeps all the data at once — one God, three persons — where every rival buys its simplicity by dropping a line of it.

Precision check: the New Testament evidence does not prove the Nicene formula the way a theorem is proved. The argument establishes that the doctrine is the most faithful reading of the data — not that no thoughtful person could read it otherwise.

Each rival drops a line of evidence Strong · not a knockdown proof

Unitarianism must explain away the deity texts and the worship of Jesus. Modalism must explain away the texts of distinction — the Son who prays to the Father is not praying to himself. Tritheism must explain away the relentless monotheism. The Trinity is the reading that keeps every given at once, where every rival buys its simplicity by dropping a line of it. It is the best explanation of the whole body of data rather than the tidiest explanation of a selected part.

Honest limit: the New Testament does not hand us the doctrine in finished, technical form. It hands us the materials — the three givens — and the church spent three centuries working out the formulation that holds them. It was a faithful drawing-out of what was already there, not the invention of something new.

Objection — “The word Trinity is not in the Bible”

The word “Trinity” appears nowhere in Scripture; neither do “person,” “essence,” or “consubstantial.” If the teaching were so central, surely the apostles would have stated it plainly.

Reply

Absence of a label is not absence of a thing. The word “monotheism” is not in the Bible either; nor is “omnipotence,” nor “incarnation,” nor even the word “Bible.” Summary terms exist to name what a body of texts teaches, and the only question worth asking is whether the data the term summarizes is present: one God; Father, Son, and Spirit each treated as God; the three genuinely distinct. Deny the word and every one of those givens remains — demanding precisely the synthesis the word names.

Objection — the charge of incoherence

The Trinity, Tuggy argues, is not what the apostles taught but a fourth-century construction—and one whose coherence (one being, three persons who are each the one God) remains, in his judgment, philosophically unproven; critics such as Tuggy press it as a charge of incoherence, chiefly through the transitivity of identity.

Reply

How one being can be three persons is a live philosophical question, and it must be conceded. Yet the charge presses chiefly through the transitivity of identity — an argument that depends on reading “is God” as absolute identity, a reading the doctrine never required and which several models explicitly reject; no one has succeeded in exhibiting a contradiction in the doctrine carefully stated, and there exist coherent (if costly and contested) ways of articulating it. A contradiction requires the same thing affirmed and denied in the same respect, and “one in essence, three in person” carefully avoids that — what God is is one, who God is is three. What the centuries of attack have produced is not a demonstrated contradiction but a recognized mystery.

The room: near-consensus that a remarkably high view of Jesus, and devotion offered to him as to God, appears at the very earliest layer — documented especially by Hurtado and reflected in the work of Wright and Fee. The divine-identity framing is a powerful but disputed interpretation of that datum; the philosophical articulation remains genuinely contested between Trinitarians and critics like Tuggy.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each objection is stated as you will actually meet it, then answered — with the hardest one named above and answered at length in the deep dive.

The one that matters most: the biblical-unitarian cumulative case (Dale Tuggy). The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"The word Trinity is not in the Bible" Common

Strongest form

Search the text: Trinity, three persons, one essence — absent. Post-biblical vocabulary for a post-biblical idea.

Reply

'Monotheism' is not in the Bible either; nor 'omnipotence,' nor 'Bible.' Summary terms label what texts teach — the question is whether the DATA is there: one God; Father, Son, Spirit each God; the three distinct. Deny the label and the data remains, demanding exactly what the label summarises.

"John 17:3 — the Father is the ONLY true God" Common

Strongest form

Jesus himself prays to the Father as the only true God, distinguishing himself from him. The texts that say "only true God" name the Father, not a triune being.

Reply

Keep reading John: two verses later the Son claims the glory he had WITH the Father before the world existed — glory Isaiah says YHWH shares with no other — and the Gospel ends at 'my Lord and my God.' John 17:3 excludes the idols, not the Son — just as Jude calling Jesus 'our only Master and Lord' does not un-Lord the Father.

"The Son is subordinate — the Father is greater than I" Trickier

Strongest form

Jesus obeys, is sent, does not know the hour, hands the kingdom back (1 Cor 15:28). That is not co-equality.

Reply

Distinguish what the texts distinguish: the Son INCARNATE — 'made for a little while lower than the angels' — speaks from the role he assumed; 'greater' is position, not nature ('I and the Father are one' stands in the same Gospel). The order of sending, obeying and handing over belongs to the incarnate mission — the economy of salvation — not to any inequality of essence. Mark 13:32 speaks from his human mind, which grows in wisdom (Luke 2:52); in 1 Cor 15:28 the incarnate Son, his mediatorial work complete, hands back the kingdom (Psalm 8, 110): an ordering of the mission, not a ranking in nature. Philippians 2 gives the arc: in the FORM OF GOD, he took the form of a servant.

"The Spirit is God’s power, not a person — it is poured and fills" Trickier

Strongest form

Poured out, filled with, came upon — impersonal verbs everywhere. Persons are not poured.

Reply

And this 'power' speaks ('the Spirit said to Philip'), decides ('it seemed good to the Spirit and to us'), distributes gifts 'as HE wills,' intercedes, is grieved and lied to — and in Acts 16:7 forbids Paul's travel plans. Pouring and filling describe how a personal God gives himself generously; they cannot cancel the first-person verbs. Electricity does not say no.

"Mark’s Jesus denies deity — why call me good?" Hard

Strongest form

In the earliest Gospel Jesus deflects the very honour you build on. Pre-inflation, he points away from himself.

Reply

Jesus does not say 'I am not good' — he asks the man to weigh his own words: do you know what calling ME good commits you to? Socratic, like 'whose son is the Messiah?' And 'low early Mark' is overstated: Mark opens with prepare-the-way-of-YHWH applied to Jesus, has him forgive sins to blasphemy cries by chapter 2, walk the sea saying ego eimi by chapter 6, and claim Daniel 7 at trial. The earliest Gospel is this objection's problem, not its proof.

"One being, three persons is a contradiction — reject some data" Hard

Strongest form

The texts pull three ways; the rational response to contradictory data is to drop some, not canonise mystery.

Reply

Contradiction has a form: same respect, same sense. One God and three GODS would qualify; one ESSENCE and three PERSONS does not — what-God-is is one, who-God-is is three. Centuries of attack have produced discomfort, not a demonstrated contradiction but a recognised mystery (the philosophical track ahead takes this head-on). The sharpest charge (Tuggy) runs through the transitivity of identity, and it depends on reading "is God" as absolute identity, which the doctrine never required and several models reject. The church kept the data and enlarged the theory.

"One God is reserved for the Father; Jesus gets the lesser title Lord" Hard

Strongest form

In 1 Cor 8:6 Paul keeps ‘one God’ for the Father alone and gives Jesus only the lesser title ‘Lord’ — the biblical-unitarian reading (Dale Tuggy).

Reply

Lord (kyrios) is the Greek translation’s standard rendering of the divine name; as Wright and Bauckham read it, Paul is including Jesus in the Shema, not demoting him. That reading is influential, arguably dominant, and genuinely contested: James F. McGrath argues ‘one Lord’ is set alongside the creed rather than folded into it. Wisdom is praised; Enoch is exalted; but no Jewish writer we know of wrote a second figure into the creed (Philo’s “second god,” the Logos, is the nearest case, and even it is never placed inside the confession). The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

The high-devotion datum is firm. There is broad, hard-won near-consensus — documented especially by Hurtado and reflected in the work of Wright and Fee — that a remarkably high view of Jesus, and a pattern of devotion offered to him as to God, appears at the very earliest layer of the movement, not as a late Hellenistic accretion; this is what makes the older “Constantine invented it” story untenable. What the New Testament does not do is hand us the doctrine in finished, technical form: the decisive terms (one ousia, three hypostaseis) are the product of three centuries of labor, and no single verse states the doctrine whole.

A fairness most defenders skip: Bauckham's sharper proposal — that the writers deliberately place Jesus within the divine identity — has been enormously influential but remains genuinely contested: James D. G. Dunn and James F. McGrath judge that “divine identity” reads later precision back into more developmental first-century language, and Hurtado read the same data as a pattern of devotion rather than in Bauckham's identity categories. The precise relation of 1 Corinthians 8:6 to the Shema — splitting, supplementing, or “brokering” — remains debated. The most serious contemporary non-Trinitarian reading is the biblical unitarianism of the analytic philosopher Dale Tuggy, who presses the coherence of “one being, three persons” as philosophically unproven; the conversation between Trinitarians and critics like Tuggy continues.

The bounded verdict: So the claim that the New Testament is itself unitarian does not hold: it keeps the first given only by setting aside the second. The three givens are each genuinely and pervasively taught, they sit in real tension, and the Trinity is the synthesis that loses none of them while every rival loses one — the best explanation of the whole body of data, not the tidiest explanation of a selected part. The New Testament evidence does not prove the Nicene formula the way a theorem is proved; what it shows, far stronger than the caricature allows, is that the historic doctrine is the most faithful reading of the data, not that no thoughtful person could read it otherwise. The word came later. The worship came first — and the worship is where the doctrine was born.

    SK
    Skeptic
    "The whole doctrine is just Greek philosophy smuggled into a Hebrew faith — abstract metaphysics about “essence” and “persons” that a Galilean fisherman would never have recognised, dressed up as Scripture centuries later."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    Concede the true part first: the technical vocabulary is genuinely later and genuinely philosophical — “Trinity,” “person,” “essence,” “one ousia in three hypostaseis” are the product of three centuries of hard, often bitter labor, and no single verse states the doctrine whole. But the reality those words name was not imported; it was born in Jewish worship. The New Testament does not soften Jewish monotheism to make room for Jesus — it sharpens it (“there is no God but one”), and it is from inside that uncompromising monotheism that the earliest believers were already praying to Jesus and singing to him as though he were that God — what Hurtado calls an unprecedented innovation within Jewish monotheism, not a drift away from it, in place within the first two decades. So the development ran the opposite way from the objection: the worship came first and the word came later. The later labor was a faithful drawing-out of what the data already held, not the invention of something new — Nicaea did not vote Jesus into godhood; it rejected the genuinely new idea, Arius's created Son, by finding language for what congregations had been doing since before Pliny reported them to Trajan around AD 112, more than a century and a half before Constantine was born. That the finished vocabulary is philosophical no more makes the doctrine pagan than naming a disease in Latin makes the patient's condition Roman.
