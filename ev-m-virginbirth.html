The Virgin Birth and Isaiah 7:14

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and stay clear and calm in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

The virgin conception of Jesus is attested by Matthew and Luke in accounts that look genuinely independent: they have different genealogies, different annunciation scenes (Matthew follows Joseph; Luke follows Mary), and largely different episodes, yet they agree on the core — Jesus was conceived by the Holy Spirit and born of Mary, a virgin betrothed to Joseph, in Bethlehem. Two independent witnesses to the same striking claim is a strong historical signal.

The claim also carries a heavy "embarrassment" cost and no obvious motive to invent. A virgin conception exposed Jesus to the charge of illegitimacy — a slur that surfaces in later polemic (Celsus's "Panthera" story) and may be shadowed in John 8:41 ("we were not born of fornication"). Communities do not usually fabricate their own scandal. Nor did Jewish messianic expectation require a virgin-born deliverer, and monotheistic authors deliberately avoided the pagan god-impregnation myths, so neither tradition supplies a ready template.

Honest scope: Matthew cites Isaiah 7:14, where the Hebrew almah ("young woman") is debated and the prophecy had a near-term horizon in King Ahaz's day. The best reading is pattern-fulfilment — Matthew reads Israel's story as recapitulated and surpassed in the Messiah — not a claim that the Hebrew word by itself means "virgin." The historical argument here is about the early, independent, counter-productive-to-invent profile of the tradition, which fits the confession better than its rivals — real weight, not proof.

Before you start · key terms

Eight words this argument leans on

Learn these first — the objections below turn on them, and most weak versions of the argument come from blurring one of them.

It is not a claim about Mary’s perpetual virginity, nor (in the New Testament texts themselves) about a painless or miraculous delivery, nor about the immaculate conception of Mary herself — distinct doctrines, held by some Christian traditions and not others, that should not be confused with the New Testament assertion itself.

‘Almah and betulahThe Hebrew word Isaiah uses is ‘almah, which means a young woman of marriageable age. It does not strictly mean “virgin”; Hebrew’s closer term is betulah. An ‘almah was typically presumed unmarried and so presumably a virgin, but the term’s focus is youth, not sexual status.

A virginal conception is a woman conceiving with no consort, divine or human, by the non-sexual creative act of God’s Spirit.
The argument, formally

Two independent witnesses to a claim nobody benefits from inventing.

P1

Matthew and Luke attest the virgin conception through visibly independent accounts — different genealogies, annunciations (Joseph's vs Mary's), and episodes, sharing only the core: conceived by the Spirit, born of a virgin betrothed to Joseph, in Bethlehem.

P2

The claim carries heavy embarrassment cost — it exposed Jesus to the charge of illegitimacy (a slur the polemic duly made) — and answered no existing expectation: a fabricating church had easier options.

∴ C

Therefore an early, independently attested tradition that embarrassed its own tellers — though that shared stream need not reach all the way back to the event itself — fits the confession better than its rivals: real weight, not proof. Isaiah 7:14 is a window, not the proof. And the virgin birth is true: Jesus, truly God and truly man, was conceived by the Holy Spirit and born of the Virgin Mary.

Historians weight material that creates problems for its tellers. 'No human father' invited the exact slander we find in later polemic (Celsus's Panthera story) and even shadowed in John 8:41 ('WE were not born of fornication'). Defending Premise 1

Matthew and Luke attest the virgin conception through visibly independent accounts — different genealogies, annunciations (Joseph’s vs Mary’s), and episodes, sharing only the core: conceived by the Spirit, born of a virgin betrothed to Joseph, in Bethlehem.

Precision check: the claim is the virginal conception — that Mary conceived Jesus while a virgin, without a human father, by the creative power of God’s Spirit. It is not a claim about Mary’s perpetual virginity, nor (in the New Testament texts themselves) about a painless or miraculous delivery, nor about the immaculate conception of Mary herself — distinct doctrines, held by some Christian traditions and not others, that should not be confused with the New Testament assertion itself. And the case is modest: the virgin birth is a creedal affirmation resting on the testimony of two Gospels. It is not a public event open to investigation the way the crucifixion is. It is held by faith, on the evidence of Matthew and Luke, with reasons — not demonstrated like a theorem.

Convergence amid independence The strongest positive consideration

In Matthew 1:18–25, Mary is “found to be with child from the Holy Spirit” before she and Joseph “came together,” and an angel reassures Joseph in a dream. In Luke 1:26–38, the angel Gabriel tells Mary she will conceive, and she objects, “How will this be, since I am a virgin?” — to which the answer is, “The Holy Spirit will come upon you.” The two accounts are widely regarded by scholars as literarily independent: they share almost no wording, sequence their events differently, and cannot easily be derived one from the other. Yet across that independence they converge on a striking cluster of agreements — virginal conception, the name Jesus given by angelic command, birth at Bethlehem, descent from David, and the role of the Holy Spirit. If Matthew and Luke had collaborated, their agreements would prove little. If they had simply copied a common source, we would expect verbal overlap. Instead we find two writers, working from different angles — Matthew largely through Joseph’s experience, Luke largely through Mary’s — who nonetheless land on the same astonishing core. Independent witnesses agreeing on a core is the kind of thing historians take seriously, even when they dispute the details. Raymond Brown, whose The Birth of the Messiah remains the landmark critical study, treats the virginal conception as one of the few genuine points of agreement between the two infancy traditions.

Early, not late Received, not argued

The doctrine is not a medieval accretion. Ignatius of Antioch, writing around AD 110, already lists “the virginity of Mary and her child-bearing” among the “mysteries to be cried aloud.” Within roughly a generation of the Gospels, the virginal conception is simply assumed, not argued for — suggesting it was received tradition rather than fresh invention.

Objection — the silence: Paul, Mark and John say nothing Hardest

Here the objection bites hardest. Our earliest Christian writer, Paul, never mentions the virgin birth; he says Jesus was “born of woman, born under the law” (Galatians 4:4) and “descended from David according to the flesh” (Romans 1:3) — phrases that neither affirm nor require a virginal conception. Mark, the earliest Gospel, has no infancy narrative at all. John, the latest, opens with a cosmic prologue and never mentions Mary’s virginity. So the doctrine is attested in only two of the New Testament’s voices. The legendary-development thesis — that the virginal conception grew up in the decades between Paul and Matthew/Luke — is the objection’s strongest form, and Raymond Brown lays it out in full, concluding that the historicity of the virginal conception cannot be settled by historical-critical methods alone.

Reply

The honest concession: attestation is limited to two Gospels, and silence in Paul and Mark is a real datum, not a trick. The measured reply is twofold. First, silence is not denial — Paul wrote occasional letters to churches, not biographies, and had no obvious occasion to discuss Jesus’ conception; Mark begins his story at the Jordan as a matter of literary design. Nor is the silence total: Mark alone calls Jesus “the son of Mary” (Mark 6:3) rather than the son of Joseph, and in John his opponents retort, “We were not born of sexual immorality” (John 8:41) — hints, not proofs, but consistent with questions about his origin already in circulation. Second, the silence cuts less far than it seems precisely because the two writers who did tell the birth story did so independently: their agreement pushes the tradition back behind both Gospels into an earlier, shared stream — though in fairness that shared stream need not reach all the way back to the event itself. The silence is a real datum, and an honest reader will feel its weight — but silence is not denial, and two independent witnesses to a private family matter push the tradition earlier, not later. What the argument from silence cannot do is convert an early, independently attested conviction into a late invention.

Objection — the internal tensions

Matthew and Luke do not merely differ; in places they pull against each other. Their genealogies of Jesus diverge sharply — Matthew traces Joseph’s line through David’s son Solomon to a father named Jacob; Luke runs through David’s son Nathan to a father named Heli. Their itineraries differ: Matthew has a flight to Egypt; Luke has a return to Nazareth with no Egypt at all.

Reply

Proposed harmonizations exist — that Luke records Mary’s line, or that levirate marriage explains the two fathers — but they are reconstructions, not statements the texts themselves make, and a fair reader should label them contested. What the tensions do not touch, however, is the shared core. Two accounts that disagree on route and lineage yet agree on virginal conception, Bethlehem, and Davidic descent look less like one fabricated story than like two independent memories of the same extraordinary claim, each elaborated in its own way.

The room: Conservative scholars (e.g., the contributors gathered in studies defending the doctrine’s historicity) hold that the convergence and the embarrassment criterion make a virginal conception the best explanation of the data. Critical scholars such as Brown judge that historical method alone cannot settle the question; others, more skeptical, judge it a relatively late confessional development. No purely historical method can adjudicate a private, miraculous event attested by two sources a generation or two after the fact. That is the real limit of the evidence, and it should be stated without flinching.
Defending Premise 2

The claim carries heavy embarrassment cost — it exposed Jesus to the charge of illegitimacy (a slur the polemic duly made) — and answered no existing expectation: a fabricating church had easier options.

Precision check: the embarrassment is not evidence that the conception was virginal, but that something about Jesus’ origin was, from very early, in need of explanation. And the pagan-parallels reply is a negative argument — it shows the borrowing thesis is weak, not that the conception occurred.

The criterion of embarrassment Suggestive

A pregnancy before the couple “came together” carried, in first-century Judaism, the unavoidable shadow of illegitimacy. Matthew’s narrative shows Joseph “resolved to divorce her quietly” (Matthew 1:19) — a detail no early Christian eager to defend Jesus’ honor would invent gratuitously. Hostile traditions did in fact seize on the opening: by the second century, charges that Jesus was born of an irregular union circulated, preserved in the pagan critic Celsus and echoed in later rabbinic material. A church fabricating a flattering origin story would have had easier options than one that handed its opponents this weapon.

Honest limit: the skeptic will reply that the rumor may simply be true, and the virgin birth a pious cover story — a view revived, in a variant of her own, by Jane Schaberg, who reads the infancy narratives themselves as quietly preserving an illegitimate conception. But the explicit charge of an irregular union surfaces in our sources only after Matthew, reads most naturally as a hostile answer to the Christian claim rather than independent memory, and a community inventing a cover would hardly have told a story that kept the scandal in view. Schaberg’s variant also runs against the texts’ plain sense: Matthew places the conception “from the Holy Spirit” before Joseph and Mary “came together” (Matthew 1:18–20), and Luke’s Mary asks how she can conceive “since I am a virgin” (Luke 1:34–35).

Not the pagan move Holds up well

The pagan tales, where they involve miraculous birth at all, are overwhelmingly stories of divine impregnation: a god, in some bodily or quasi-bodily form, unites with a mortal woman, who then is no longer a virgin. None of these is a virginal conception — a woman conceiving with no consort, divine or human, by the non-sexual creative act of God’s Spirit. The Jewish, fiercely monotheistic setting makes a borrowed divine-consort myth especially unlikely; the last thing a Palestinian Jewish community would import is a god siring children on women. The Gospel accounts are studiedly different: no intercourse, no demigod; in Luke the Spirit overshadows (Luke 1:35) as the glory-cloud overshadowed the tabernacle (Exodus 40:35). The grammar is Jewish-creational, not Olympian.

The conviction came first, not the text Isaiah 7:14 is a window

Matthew alone ties the conception to a text, quoting Isaiah 7:14 (Matthew 1:22–23). In its original setting, Isaiah 7 is a word to King Ahaz of Judah around 734 BC; the Hebrew word Isaiah uses is ‘almah, which means a young woman of marriageable age. What Matthew quotes is not the Hebrew but the Septuagint — the pre-Christian Greek translation made by Jews, which rendered ‘almah with the Greek parthenos, “virgin.” Matthew did not engineer that rendering; he inherited it. On this reading the conviction came first — the church already believed Jesus was virginally conceived — and the text was seen, in hindsight, to illumine it. Isaiah 7:14 is best treated as a window, not a proof.

Honest limit: this is a contested, largely typological case, not a knockdown prediction, and it should be presented as such. As a standalone argument for the virgin birth, Isaiah 7:14 does not work, and the case should not be hung on it. Not that Jesus was not virgin-born: the Virgin Birth is a creedal essential (“born of the Virgin Mary”), grounded in the explicit narratives of Matthew 1 and Luke 1 — it does not stand or fall on the translation of ‘almah. Among Jewish interpreters, the reading of Isaiah 7:14 as a near-term sign to Ahaz, with ‘almah meaning simply “young woman,” is the mainstream and longstanding view, and Christians should state that reading accurately rather than caricature it.

Objection — the translation: Matthew built a doctrine on a Greek mistranslation

Stated at its best (and it has Jewish and critical defenders): Matthew built a doctrine on a Greek mistranslation; the Hebrew never spoke of a virgin; the whole edifice is a linguistic accident.

Reply

I concede its factual kernel, not its inference. Isaiah’s ‘almah is not betulah, and the verse is not a slam-dunk prediction. The reply is narrower than apologists sometimes wish: the virgin birth does not derive from Isaiah 7:14. The earliest tradition rests on the events Matthew and Luke report; Isaiah is cited to interpret, not to generate, the claim. Strip the verse away entirely and Luke — who never quotes it — still reports a virginal conception. So the objection wounds a bad argument, not the doctrine itself.

Objection — the claim is borrowed pagan mythology

Critics in the history-of-religions tradition long argued that the virgin birth is borrowed mythology — Mithras, Dionysus, the divine paternity of Augustus, and others. This must be taken seriously, not waved away.

Reply

The reply turns on a disanalogy that holds up well. Dionysus is the offspring of Zeus and the mortal Semele. The Augustus legend has the god Apollo as a physical agent. Mithras, in the Roman cult, is born from a rock, with no woman in the scene at all. None of these is a virginal conception. The candid scholarly verdict, even among many non-confessional historians, is that the alleged parallels are too loose, and in several cases too late, to ground a derivation. That said, this is a negative argument — it shows the borrowing thesis is weak, not that the conception occurred.

The room: On the pagan “parallels” as divine impregnation rather than virginal conception, the essay cites Raymond Brown and J. Gresham Machen. Schaberg presses the illegitimacy variant; Jewish interpreters hold the near-term reading of Isaiah 7:14 as mainstream. What it does and does not establish: the virgin birth is not a public, multiply-corroborated event like the crucifixion under Pilate. It is the testimony of two Gospels, early and independent, agreeing on a core that ran against the church’s social interest. Those features give it real evidential weight; they do not amount to proof. The early church did not think the virginal conception proved Jesus’ divinity; rather, having come to believe he was God-with-us, it saw in the manner of his coming a fitting sign that the new thing God did in Christ was, from the very first cell, God’s own initiative and not merely ours. Jesus was conceived by the Holy Spirit and born of the Virgin Mary — truly God and truly man, born of a woman, in a body.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: the silence — Paul, Mark and John say nothing about it. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"Almah means young woman, not virgin — the doctrine rests on a mistranslation" Common

Strongest form

Isaiah's Hebrew is almah — young woman. The virgin (parthenos) appears only in the Greek translation Matthew used. The whole doctrine is a translation artifact.

Reply

I concede its factual kernel: 'almah is not betulah, and the verse is not a slam-dunk prediction. Two answers. Linguistic: 'almah is nowhere used of a married woman, though the term's focus is youth, not sexual status — and it was Jewish translators, two centuries before Christianity, who chose parthenos for Isaiah 7:14; Matthew inherited a Jewish reading, not a Christian trick. Logical: the belief did not come from the verse — Luke narrates the virgin conception without ever citing Isaiah. The event generated the proof-text's use, not the reverse.

"Mark, John, and Paul never mention it — because it had not been invented yet" Common

Strongest form

The earliest sources are silent. Silence in Mark and Paul plus presence in the 80s equals legendary growth on schedule.

Reply

Mark begins his story at the Jordan as a matter of literary design, and John at creation; Paul wrote occasional letters to churches, not biographies, and had no obvious occasion to discuss Jesus' conception. The honest concession: attestation is limited to two Gospels, and silence in Paul and Mark is a real datum, not a trick — but silence is not denial. Nor is the silence total: Mark alone calls Jesus 'the son of Mary' (Mark 6:3) rather than the son of Joseph. Meanwhile the two sources that do cover the birth attest it independently — and John 8:41's 'WE were not born of fornication' suggests the unusual-birth rumor was already in the air — a hint, not a proof.

"Matthew rips Isaiah 7:14 from context — the sign was for Ahaz, fulfilled in his day" Trickier

Strongest form

Read Isaiah 7: the child is a sign to king Ahaz about two enemy kings — fulfilled within years (Isaiah 8). Matthew's use is proof-texting malpractice.

Reply

Grant the near fulfilment — and keep reading: the Immanuel child swells beyond any Ahaz-era boy into chapter 9's son called Mighty God, Prince of Peace, on David's throne forever. Isaiah builds a pattern with an unfilled apex. Matthew's 'fulfil' (pleroo) is pattern-completion — the same way he uses Hosea 11:1 — standard Jewish typological exegesis, not context-ripping. The question is whether the pattern's apex arrived; the dictionary was never the battlefield.

"It is copied from pagan divine-birth stories — Perseus, Alexander, Augustus" Trickier

Strongest form

Heroes got divine births as standard PR — Plato, Alexander, Augustus. Christians gave their hero the standard upgrade.

Reply

Compare the texts: those stories feature gods in disguise impregnating women — serpents, showers of gold, bedroom visitations — producing demigods. The Gospel accounts have no union at all; a virgin conceives by the creative overshadowing of the Spirit, in narratives saturated with Septuagint idiom and Torah piety, written by and for people who found pagan god-trysts blasphemous. The 'upgrade' theory requires Jewish authors borrowing the one motif their monotheism most despised — while their actual story shares nothing with it but the word 'birth.' This is a negative argument — it shows the borrowing thesis is weak, not that the conception occurred.

"The genealogies contradict — and both trace JOSEPH, which a virgin birth makes irrelevant" Hard

Strongest form

Matthew and Luke give incompatible family trees, both through Joseph — incoherent if Joseph contributed nothing. The infancy material is confused construction.

Reply

The genealogies serve legal status, not biology: in Jewish law a father's acknowledgment (Matthew shows Joseph naming the child) confers full lineage rights — Davidic sonship runs through legal adoption, deliberately. Proposed harmonizations exist — the oldest, going back to Julius Africanus in the third century, appeals to levirate marriage; the most common modern proposal holds that Luke gives Mary's line — but they are reconstructions, not statements the texts themselves make, and a fair reader should label them contested. Divergent independent records arguing toward the same Davidic claim are evidence the claim mattered early — not that it was fabricated, which would have produced one tidy list.

"An untestable private miracle — even in principle, no one could verify it" Hard

Strongest form

Every other argument appeals to public evidence. This one rests on what only Mary could know. Unverifiable then, unverifiable now — why include it in a case at all?

Reply

Honest — and the argument's claim is calibrated to that: not 'publicly provable like the empty tomb,' but 'a tradition whose earliness, independence, and embarrassment-cost make invention the worse hypothesis.' Historical method weighs testimony it cannot replay all the time. And the claim does not float alone: for anyone persuaded on other grounds that God raised Jesus from the dead, a miraculous beginning is no great leap.

The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

Conservative scholars hold that the convergence and the embarrassment criterion make a virginal conception the best explanation of the data. Critical scholars such as Brown judge that historical method alone cannot settle the question; others, more skeptical, judge it a relatively late confessional development. No purely historical method can adjudicate a private, miraculous event attested by two sources a generation or two after the fact. That is the real limit of the evidence, and it should be stated without flinching.

A fairness most defenders skip: Among Jewish interpreters, the reading of Isaiah 7:14 as a near-term sign to Ahaz, with ‘almah meaning simply “young woman,” is the mainstream and longstanding view, and Christians should state that reading accurately rather than caricature it. The silence of Paul, Mark and John is a real datum, not a trick. And the proposed harmonizations of the two genealogies are reconstructions, not statements the texts themselves make; a fair reader should label them contested.

The bounded verdict: The virgin birth is not a public, multiply-corroborated event like the crucifixion under Pilate. It is the testimony of two Gospels, early and independent, agreeing on a core that ran against the church’s social interest. Those features give it real evidential weight; they do not amount to proof. The case clears the mistranslation and borrowing charges, and it cannot, by historical method alone, prove a private miracle. But an early, independently attested tradition that embarrassed its own tellers fits the confession better than its rivals — and for anyone persuaded on other grounds that God raised Jesus from the dead, a miraculous beginning is no great leap. So our answer is yes: Jesus, truly God and truly man, was conceived by the Holy Spirit and born of the Virgin Mary.

    Critical scholar
    "You call the illegitimacy charge embarrassing — but maybe the rumor was simply true. Read closely, the infancy narratives themselves quietly preserve an illegitimate conception, and the virgin birth is the pious cover story."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    That is the skeptic’s standard reply to the embarrassment argument — a view revived, in a variant of her own, by Jane Schaberg. But the explicit charge of an irregular union surfaces in our sources only after Matthew, and it reads most naturally as a hostile answer to the Christian claim rather than independent memory; a community inventing a cover would hardly have told a story that kept the scandal in view. Schaberg’s variant also runs against the texts’ plain sense: Matthew places the conception “from the Holy Spirit” before Joseph and Mary “came together” (Matthew 1:18–20), and Luke’s Mary asks how she can conceive “since I am a virgin” (Luke 1:34–35). The embarrassment still shows only that Jesus’ origin needed explaining from very early; the case for the virginal conception rests on the two independent accounts.
