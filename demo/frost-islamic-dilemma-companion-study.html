Featured club
The Qur’an speaks warmly of the Torah (Tawrat) and the Gospel (Injil) — calling them revelation from God, “guidance and light,” and telling Jews and Christians to follow them. Yet it also contradicts them on central points, from the crucifixion of Jesus to the very fatherhood of God. Christopher Frost works through the Qur’an itself — not from Christian premises — to lay out that tension and ask what it means. This club walks the book part by part, then helps you talk it over, thoughtfully and kindly.

Finish the study · Mastery check

Can you make the case — kindly?

One capstone for the whole book — eight prompts that walk through the argument, in two modes. Flashcards: read a prompt, answer it in your head (or out loud to the group), then tap to reveal a model answer drawn from Frost's argument. Self-rate quiz: take the same eight prompts one at a time, rate how well you answered each, and get an honest score at the end. If you can walk through all eight — graciously — you can have the conversation.

01 · The argument
What is the “Islamic Dilemma”?
Tap to reveal →
The Qur’an affirms the Torah and Gospel as true, God-given revelation — yet it contradicts them on central points. So, the book argues, the same divine author cannot stand behind both, which puts real pressure on the Qur’an’s own claim. It’s built from the Qur’an itself, and offered as a probability, not a proof.
02 · The fork
Why is it a “dilemma”?
Tap to reveal →
A Muslim faces two hard options. Affirm that the Qur’an endorses the earlier Scriptures — and face the contradictions. Or deny it — and contradict the Qur’an’s own repeated words. Either way there is a cost to pay.
03 · The affirmation
How does the Qur’an speak of the Torah and Gospel?
Tap to reveal →
As genuine revelation from God. It calls itself their “confirmer” (musaddiq), names them “guidance and light,” commands the People of the Book to follow them (Q 5:43–47), and makes belief in the earlier “Books” a pillar of faith. Not one stray verse — a sustained theme across dozens.
04 · Which Bible?
Which Torah and Gospel does the Qur’an mean?
Tap to reveal →
The book argues it means the Scriptures Jews and Christians actually held in the seventh century — substantially the same as ours. The Qur’an tells people to consult their own Torah (Q 3:93), addresses the real Jews and Christians of the day, and the manuscripts (down to the Ethiopian Gospels of Muhammad’s own era) show the text hadn’t changed enough to matter.
05 · The corruption reply
“But the Bible was corrupted” — does that hold?
Tap to reveal →
Frost argues these verses chiefly describe twisting a text’s meaning or concealing it — rather than rewriting the words (and you can only conceal a Scripture you actually have). And a universal, coordinated corruption, across rival communities in many languages with no surviving trace, is very hard to credit historically. He adds a careful point: even if it had been changed, the Qur’an still called it true.
06 · The divergences
Where do the Qur’an and the Bible actually differ?
Tap to reveal →
On the things that matter most: the Qur’an is traditionally read as denying Jesus’ crucifixion (Q 4:157), denies he is the divine Son, and rejects God as “Father” — while the Gospels affirm all three. (Islam still affirms much about Jesus — virgin-born, Messiah, prophet — but it is a different portrait; the divergence over his divine identity is real, not manufactured.)
07 · The tone
How should a Christian actually use this?
Tap to reveal →
As an honest question, not a weapon (1 Peter 3:15). State the Muslim view at its strongest and most charitable, name what Muslims genuinely affirm about Jesus, keep the verdict modest (“this is a real tension”), and invite a friend to look again — not to win a debate.
08 · The 60-second version
The whole thing, for a friend over coffee?
Tap to reveal →
The Qur’an calls the Torah and Gospel true, God-given Scripture and tells people to follow them — yet it contradicts them on Jesus’ cross, his divine identity, and God as Father. Since the Qur’an also affirmed those very Scriptures, it’s hard to see the same God behind both. It’s a genuine tension, argued from the Qur’an itself — worth raising with a Muslim friend as a sincere question, and worth knowing both books well enough to raise it well.
