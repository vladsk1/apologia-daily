Preview
What happened after Jesus was crucified? Andrew Loke sets out what he argues is a complete list of the kinds of explanation for the early Christians’ claim that Jesus appeared to them alive, and for what became of his body — and then examines each rival in turn, starting from the early witness list Paul passes on in 1 Corinthians 15, drawing on history, psychology, memory science and philosophy. His conclusion is that the resurrection is the best explanation — the one left standing. This club walks the book chapter by chapter, then helps you talk it over, carefully and kindly.

01 · The method
How does Loke’s argument work?
Tap to reveal →
By elimination over an exhaustive list. He sorts every possible explanation into categories — for the appearance claims, six naturalistic categories (seven counting the resurrection); for the body, eight (nine counting the resurrection) — then argues that each naturalistic category can reasonably be excluded. Because the list covers every kind of answer, a “new” theory has to fall into one of these categories — or a combination of them, which he examines in chapter 7.
02 · The steps
What are the steps from the evidence to “Jesus rose”?
Tap to reveal →
(I) People claimed to have seen the risen Jesus shortly after his crucifixion. (II) They had some kind of experiences. (III) What they experienced was caused by something outside their minds. (IV) That something was the same Jesus who died on the cross. (V) Therefore Jesus rose. Establishing steps I–IV needs no appeal to the supernatural. Whether it was God who raised him — which Loke argues in chapter 8 — is a further philosophical and theological question; history can establish the event while leaving its cause to philosophy and theology.
03 · The claims
Why think the witness list in 1 Corinthians 15 is not a later legend?
Tap to reveal →
It is a tradition Paul says he received and passed on, in a letter written around AD 55, and every surviving manuscript contains it. (Loke argues the list is an earlier tradition Paul received. The further point that many scholars date Paul’s receiving it to within a few years of the crucifixion is our note, not his claim in chapter 2.) Paul names Cephas and James, says most of the five hundred were still living, and some in Corinth openly doubted that the dead are raised. A false list, Loke argues, would have been easy to expose and costly to Paul.
04 · Sincerity
What does the disciples’ suffering actually prove?
Tap to reveal →
Not that they were right — Loke grants that people of many faiths suffer for what they believe. It shows that at least some of them, perhaps all, were sincere: they were willing to suffer, and, as he argues, a group of people would not willingly suffer and die for what they knew was false. What sets them apart is that they were in a position to know whether they were telling the truth about what they saw — and together with their willingness to suffer, Loke argues, that rules out deliberate invention. Whether a sincere experience could still have been mistaken is the question the next chapters take up. (He is candid that how many apostles were actually martyred is debated; his point rests on willingness, not on a body count.)
