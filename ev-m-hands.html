The HANDS Framework

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and stay clear and calm in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

The HANDS framework, from Robert Bowman and J. Ed Komoszewski's book Putting Jesus in His Place, organizes the New Testament's case for the deity of Christ into five categories that, the authors argue, belong to God alone. HANDS is an acronym: the Honors due to God (worship, prayer, doxology), the Attributes of God (eternity, omnipresence, unchangeableness), the Names of God (YHWH/kyrios, theos, "the First and the Last"), the Deeds of God (creating, sustaining, forgiving, judging, saving), and the Seat of God's throne.

The argument is that the New Testament deliberately attributes all five to Jesus. He is worshipped and prayed to; he is called eternal and unchanging; he bears the divine name in passages that quote YHWH texts; he creates and sustains all things, forgives sins, and judges the world; and he is seated on God's own throne (Revelation 22:1–3; cf. Hebrews 1:3). Bauckham's thesis is that Second Temple Jews defined their one God not chiefly by abstract attributes but by identity — he alone created all things, he alone rules all things, he alone bears the divine name, he alone is worshiped; that reading is widely influential, though the divine identity category is itself debated.

Honest scope: any single line can be debated, and the HANDS framework is not a single mathematical proof of the Trinity. What it does establish is that the New Testament presents Jesus of Nazareth as sharing the honors, attributes, names, deeds, and throne of the one God of Israel. So the verdict this evidence supports is plain: Jesus is God — fully God and fully man, distinct from the Father yet one God. Its strength is cumulative: five independent lines, each hard to square with a merely human Jesus, converging on one conclusion — that the New Testament identifies Jesus within the unique identity of the one God.

The argument, formally

Five fingers, one grip: Jesus shares what God alone is.

P1

Bowman and Komoszewski argue the New Testament presents Jesus as sharing five things that belong to God alone: the Honors due God (worship, prayer, doxology), the Attributes of God (eternity, omnipresence, unchangeableness), the Names of God (YHWH/kyrios, God, the First and the Last), the Deeds God does (creating, sustaining, forgiving, judging, saving), and the Seat of God's throne; even if name and authority could be delegated, the worship and the work of creation could not.

P2

The New Testament — across its earliest and latest documents alike — deliberately attributes all five to Jesus: he is worshipped, eternal, named with the divine Name, creator and final judge, and seated on God's own throne.

∴ C

Therefore the New Testament places Jesus within the unique identity of the one God: Jesus is God — fully God and fully man.

The framework (Bowman & Komoszewski) is cumulative by design: no single HANDS finger, pressed alone, will compel a determined skeptic; five independent lines converging is a case that must be answered whole.

The key idea — identity, not just titles.

Bauckham’s thesis is that Second Temple Jews defined their one God not chiefly by abstract attributes but by identity: he alone created all things, he alone rules all things, he alone bears the divine name, he alone is worshiped — a reading widely influential, though the divine identity category is itself debated. HANDS does not collect proof-texts; it audits Jesus against that checklist.

Defending Premise 1

Bowman and Komoszewski argue the New Testament presents Jesus as sharing five things that belong to God alone: the Honors due God (worship, prayer, doxology), the Attributes of God (eternity, omnipresence, unchangeableness), the Names of God (YHWH/kyrios, God, the First and the Last), the Deeds God does (creating, sustaining, forgiving, judging, saving), and the Seat of God’s throne; even if name and authority could be delegated, the worship and the work of creation could not.

Precision check: The HANDS framework is not a single mathematical proof of the Trinity, and it should not be sold as one. It does not settle by itself every question of metaphysics that the fourth-century councils would later define; nor can it force assent from someone committed to a unitarian reading of every passage. What it does establish is narrower: that the New Testament — read in its own first-century Jewish context — presents Jesus of Nazareth as sharing the honors, attributes, names, deeds, and throne of the one God of Israel, and does so early, pervasively, and on purpose. The gospel needs him to be, and joyfully confesses him to be, fully God and fully man.

Bauckham’s divine-identity test Widely influential · debated

Bauckham’s thesis is that Second Temple Jews defined their one God not chiefly by abstract attributes but by identity — by who God is and what marks him out: he alone created all things, he alone rules all things, he alone bears the divine name, he alone is worshiped. The New Testament writers, Bauckham argues, deliberately include Jesus within that unique identity. HANDS is, in effect, a layperson’s organization of the same evidence Bauckham marshals.

Honest limit: The “divine identity” reading associated with Bauckham is widely influential, though the divine identity category is itself debated: Hurtado reads the same data as a pattern of devotion, James F. McGrath disputes the category outright, and Dunn continued to press a more cautious line to the end. It would be triumphalist to claim the matter is closed.

Agency has a ceiling: worship The load-bearing line

No shaliach in Jewish thought ever received the cultic worship — offered to God, alongside God — that the New Testament gives Jesus; agency conferred authority, not that adoration. An agent represents the sender; he does not receive the sender’s worship. That is exactly why the angel in Revelation — himself God’s messenger — refuses the worship the Lamb accepts.

Agency has a ceiling: creation Strong

An agent does not do the sender’s unshareable work of creating all things (John 1:3; Col. 1:16). Grant, for argument’s sake, that God’s name and authority could be entrusted to a supreme agent; the worship still could not be, and neither could the work of creation.

Objection — the agency reading (Jehovah’s Witnesses and biblical Unitarians) Hardest

The most serious challenge comes from those who affirm the Bible but deny that Jesus is God by nature. Their key tool is the Jewish principle of agency, the shaliach: “a man’s agent is as the man himself.” An ambassador is honored, obeyed, and received as though he were the king — without being the king. On this reading, every HANDS line is the dignity of a supreme agent. Jesus is honored (John 5:23) precisely because the Father gave him judgment; he does God’s deeds because God works through him; he sits at God’s right hand as the exalted Messiah of Psalm 110, the man at God’s side, not God himself. The agent does the sender’s work and bears the sender’s authority; that is what agency means. This is the steelman, and it must be met on its merits: much New Testament language about Jesus’ being “given,” “sent,” and “appointed” does fit it naturally.

Reply

It is true that the New Testament does use the language of sending and giving. But agency has a ceiling, and the HANDS evidence crashes through it. No shaliach in Jewish thought ever received the cultic worship — offered to God, alongside God — that the New Testament gives Jesus; agency conferred authority, not that adoration. An agent represents the sender; he does not receive the sender’s worship, or do the sender’s unshareable work of creating all things (John 1:3; Col. 1:16). Grant, for argument’s sake, that God’s name and authority could be entrusted to a supreme agent; the worship still could not be, and neither could the work of creation. So the agency reading does not hold.

Objection — functional, not ontological (Cullmann, Dunn)

A more academic worry, rooted in Oscar Cullmann’s influential The Christology of the New Testament (1959), is that the New Testament speaks functionally — about what Jesus does and the role he plays in salvation — not ontologically, about his eternal nature. To say Jesus exercises divine functions, the objection runs, is not yet to say he is divine in essence; the metaphysics of Nicaea is a later (if legitimate) development. James D. G. Dunn pressed a version of this in Christology in the Making (1980), arguing that Paul’s Wisdom language need not imply Christ’s personal pre-existence, and that full pre-existence appears clearly only in John.

Reply

The distinction is real, and the honest concession is that one cannot read Chalcedon’s metaphysics straight off the page of Mark. But — and here the framework’s cumulative logic matters — the functions the New Testament assigns Jesus are functions Jewish monotheism reserved to God’s being. Creating the world, ruling from the one throne, receiving worship, bearing the Name: on Bauckham’s reading, itself debated, these were not job descriptions a creature could be hired for but identity markers; and even if name and authority could be delegated, the worship and the work of creation could not. To do what only God does, as God’s equal recipient of worship, is already to be implicated in who God is. The function presupposes the identity.

The room: Robert M. Bowman Jr. and J. Ed Komoszewski coined the HANDS acronym (Putting Jesus in His Place, 2007); the underlying logic belongs to “divine identity” Christology, whose leading exponent is Richard Bauckham. Larry Hurtado reached a complementary conclusion from a different angle. James F. McGrath disputes the divine identity category outright, and Dunn pressed a more cautious line. Jehovah’s Witnesses and biblical Unitarians press the agency reading; Cullmann stands behind the functional objection.
Defending Premise 2

The New Testament — across its earliest and latest documents alike — deliberately attributes all five to Jesus: he is worshipped, eternal, named with the divine Name, creator and final judge, and seated on God’s own throne.

Precision check: These are not, taken singly, knock-down proofs — no single HANDS finger, pressed alone, will compel a determined skeptic. The claim is cumulative and convergent: the case does not rest on one verse or one line, but on the way five independent strands — honors, attributes, names, deeds, seat — all point in the same direction across documents written by different authors in different decades.

H — Honors Strong

In John 5:23 Jesus says the Father has given all judgment to the Son “that all may honor the Son, just as they honor the Father.” The standard of honor is not like God’s but identical to it — the same honor, willed by the Father, while the persons stay distinct (the Father sends, the Son is sent). In Revelation 5:13–14 every creature sings “to him who sits on the throne and to the Lamb” the same blessing and honor, even though the same book twice has an angel refuse worship with the command “Worship God!” (Rev. 19:10; 22:9). The Lamb receives what the angel forbids. The first Christians prayed to Jesus: Stephen dies calling out, “Lord Jesus, receive my spirit” (Acts 7:59). Reporting to the emperor around AD 112, the pagan governor Pliny records that the Christians he interrogated sang “a hymn to Christ, as to a god” (Pliny, Letters 10.96) — hostile testimony confirming from outside that this devotion was early, not a late invention.

Honest limit: A gesture of homage was not itself the line: Israelites could bow before a king as well as before the LORD — 1 Chronicles 29:20 uses a single verb of prostration for both. What Jewish monotheism guarded jealously for God was not the posture of homage but the substance of worship — sacrifice, invocation, prayer.

A — Attributes Cumulative, not a proof

The New Testament ascribes to Jesus attributes that belong to God by nature. He is eternal: “In the beginning was the Word… and the Word was God” (John 1:1). He is the upholder of all things: “in him all things hold together” (Col. 1:17); he “upholds the universe by the word of his power” (Heb. 1:3). John even reports that Jesus “knew all people and… knew what was in man” (John 2:24–25). And Paul says it flatly: in Christ “the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily” (Col. 2:9).

Honest limit: These are not, taken singly, knock-down proofs — but cumulatively they describe one who stands on the Creator’s side of the Creator–creature line.

N — Names Strong · individual texts contested

First, the title Kyrios (“Lord”). The Greek Old Testament used Kyrios to render the divine name YHWH, and New Testament writers repeatedly take such YHWH texts and apply them to Jesus (Phil. 2:10–11 with Isa. 45:23; Rom. 10:13 with Joel 2:32). Peter does the same with the reverence Isaiah reserves for God alone: “in your hearts honor Christ the Lord as holy” (1 Pet. 3:15, the reading of the earliest manuscripts) reworks Isaiah 8:13, where it is “the LORD of hosts” whom Israel must “honor… as holy.” Second, “I am.” In John 8:58 Jesus says, “Before Abraham was, I am” — and his hearers pick up stones, the response to blasphemy. Third, the outright title “God”: Thomas’s confession “My Lord and my God!” (John 20:28), which Jesus accepts; and Titus 2:13, “our great God and Savior Jesus Christ.” Jesus also takes “the First and the Last,” Isaiah’s exclusive self-title for YHWH (Rev. 1:17).

Honest limit: Individual proof-texts remain debated: whether Titus 2:13 and Romans 9:5 call Jesus “God” turns on Greek grammar; whether Philippians 2 quotes YHWH specifically, or “Lord” more loosely, is disputed. As for John 8:58, even on the New World Translation’s milder “I have been” rendering, the reaction — an immediate attempt to stone him (John 8:59) — is hard to square with a tame statement of pre-existence; and where John does state the ground of such an attempt, it is the charge that “you, being a man, make yourself God” (John 10:33).

1 Corinthians 8:6 — the Shema split, not demoted Influential · genuinely contested

In 1 Corinthians 8:6 Paul appears to rework the Shema itself — “for us there is one God, the Father… and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things” — a reading, strongly defended by Bauckham and Wright, that sets Jesus within Israel’s confession of the one God, not beside it. Here “one Lord” is the divine Name, not a demotion; the Son is included within the one God, not ranked below him.

Honest limit: That reading is influential, arguably dominant, and genuinely contested; whether 1 Corinthians 8:6 folds Jesus into the Shema or instead sets a distinct “Lord” beside the one God is pressed by critics such as McGrath.

D — Deeds Strong

Jesus does what only God does. He creates: “All things were made through him” (John 1:3); “by him all things were created” (Col. 1:16). He gives life and judges: “the Son gives life to whom he will… the Father… has given all judgment to the Son” (John 5:21–22) — and the Old Testament insists God alone gives life and judges the earth. He sustains the universe by his word (Heb. 1:3) and forgives sins in his own person (Mark 2:5–7).

S — Seat Supporting

The Old Testament verse the New Testament quotes or alludes to most often is Psalm 110:1: “The LORD says to my Lord, ‘Sit at my right hand.’” To sit on God’s throne is to share God’s rule. Revelation pushes furthest: there is one throne “of God and of the Lamb” (Rev. 22:1, 3) — a single seat, shared. The point is not merely that Jesus is honored at God’s side but that Revelation refuses to picture two thrones: from the one throne the single river of life flows, and before it the redeemed render undivided worship (Rev. 22:1–3).

Honest limit: A viceroy may be seated beside a king, and by the king’s grant may even share his rule; what no viceroy receives is that worship.

Early and everywhere Strong

Philippians 2 is widely regarded as a pre-Pauline hymn — older than the letter, within twenty-odd years of the crucifixion — and it already applies an exclusive-worship text of YHWH to Jesus. The Aramaic prayer Marana tha, “Our Lord, come!” (1 Cor. 16:22), is a fossil of devotion in the original language of the Palestinian church. Hurtado’s whole argument is that the highest devotion is early, not late: a “binitarian” pattern of worship — devotion offered to Jesus alongside God — erupted astonishingly early, within the first couple of decades, and among Jews. The pattern is the floor of the tradition, not its ceiling.

Honest limit: The pre-Pauline dating of the Philippians hymn is itself debated — Gordon Fee argues Paul composed the hymn — but the worship-of-Jesus datum stands either way.

Why five strands are harder to dismiss than one Cumulative

The strength of a convergent case is that its strands cannot be neutralized all at once. Dispute one finger — a contested grammar here, a milder rendering there — and four remain, each now demanding its own separate explanation. The one unifying alternative — that all five are the dignity of a supreme agent — has already run aground on the worship the Lamb receives, which agency never conferred. A single ambiguity is cheap to absorb; a different ad hoc account for each of five independent strands is not.

Objection — Ehrman: the divine figure’s rank developed

Bart Ehrman, in How Jesus Became God (HarperOne, 2014), argues that the historical Jesus did not claim to be God and was not worshiped as God in his lifetime. On his account Christology developed — but what developed was the divine figure’s rank, not the date of pre-existence itself. The earliest confession, he holds, was one of exaltation (God raised the man Jesus to heavenly status); yet Ehrman also finds a pre-existent Christ already in material older than Paul, a figure he takes to have been conceived at first as a subordinate divine being — God’s chief angel, or a divinity of a lesser order — whose elevation to the co-equal God confessed at Nicaea in 325 is the later step. On this view the loftiest HANDS texts — “I am,” Thomas’s confession, the shared throne — are the church’s later theology read back onto Jesus, not history.

Reply

The strongest evidence is the one thing his developmental thesis cannot easily accommodate: the earliest worship. Philippians 2 already applies an exclusive-worship text of YHWH to Jesus (the pre-Pauline dating is itself debated — Gordon Fee argues Paul composed the hymn — but the worship-of-Jesus datum stands either way), and the Aramaic prayer Marana tha is a fossil of devotion in the original language of the Palestinian church. Hurtado’s whole argument is that the highest devotion is early, not late — and Bauckham insists the inclusion of Jesus in the unique divine identity occurs across the New Testament writings, not just John. Wisdom is praised; Enoch is exalted; but no Jewish writer took the creed of divine uniqueness and wrote a second figure into it. Paul did — on the reading of 1 Corinthians 8:6 that is influential, arguably dominant, and genuinely contested. So at the point that matters, we judge that the developmental thesis does not hold: on that reading, which we take to be the right one, the earliest devotion already placed Jesus inside the one God’s identity, not beneath it as a lesser divinity.

The room: Hurtado (devotion to Jesus erupted early) and Bauckham (inclusion in the divine identity across the New Testament, not just John) defend the pattern; Pliny supplies hostile, outside testimony to the worship by AD 112. A minority of scholars still favor a more developmental or adoptionist account of the earliest period; Ehrman’s developmental thesis is answered above; Dunn maintained that some Pauline texts are read too maximally; McGrath presses 1 Corinthians 8:6. Fee argues Paul composed the Philippians hymn. The matter is not closed.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: the agency reading — a supreme emissary could bear all five without being God. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"Worship just means homage — people bowed to kings too" Common

Strongest form

Proskuneo is used of bowing to human authorities throughout the ancient world. Honors prove etiquette, not deity.

Reply

A gesture of homage was not itself the line: Israelites could bow before a king as well as before the LORD (1 Chronicles 29:20). What Jewish monotheism guarded jealously for God was not the posture of homage but the substance of worship — sacrifice, invocation, prayer. That is exactly why the angel in Revelation — himself God’s messenger — refuses the worship the Lamb accepts. Context is the test — and the NT runs the test for us: Peter refuses Cornelius’s worship ('I too am a man'), and Revelation's angel refuses it twice ('worship God!'). The risen Jesus accepts it, in heaven, from every creature, alongside the One on the throne. The same documents that police the line for men and angels watch Jesus stand on the far side of it.

"An exalted agent can carry the Name and the throne — Judaism allowed it" Trickier

Strongest form

Texts like the Exagoge put Moses on God's throne; angels bear the Name in Exodus 23. Agency, not identity, explains your five lines.

Reply

Each line alone, perhaps — that is why the case is cumulative. An agent represents the sender; he does not receive the sender’s worship, or do the sender’s unshareable work of creating all things (John 1:3; Col. 1:16). Grant, for argument’s sake, that God’s name and authority could be entrusted to a supreme agent; the worship still could not be, and neither could the work of creation.

"Each letter has disputed verses — a chain of weak links is weak" Trickier

Strongest form

Granville Sharp constructions, textual variants at John 1:18, contested punctuation in Romans 9:5 — every pillar wobbles when pushed. Five wobbly pillars are not a fortress.

Reply

The framework was built for exactly this objection: no letter rests on one verse. Strike Romans 9:5 and the Names still have Romans 10:13's YHWH-citation; strike a variant and 'the First and the Last' remains. Five independent lines, each with multiple supports across multiple authors, is the structure of a cumulative case — the rebuttal must explain the CONVERGENCE, and 'every author independently wobbled in the same direction' is not an explanation.

"The Seat disproves it — sitting at God’s right hand means NOT being God" Common

Strongest form

Psalm 110 has the LORD speaking TO David's lord and seating him beside himself. Two figures, one God, one honoured guest.

Reply

Two persons, certainly — that is Trinitarianism, not a problem for it. The question is what the seat MEANS: a viceroy may be seated beside a king, and by the king’s grant may even share his rule; what no viceroy receives is that worship. Revelation pictures one throne of God and of the Lamb (Rev 22:1–3), before which the redeemed render undivided worship.

"This is theology imposed on texts — a clever acronym is not exegesis" Hard

Strongest form

HANDS is a mnemonic marketing device. Arrange any figure's honorifics into categories and you can manufacture divinity — the framework creates the pattern it claims to find.

Reply

Run the falsification test: apply HANDS to anyone else in the literature. Even if name and authority could be delegated to an agent, the worship and the work of creation could not. The categories are not invented for Jesus; on Bauckham’s reading, itself debated, they are identity markers (Hurtado reads the same data as a pattern of devotion, and James F. McGrath disputes the category). A framework that only one figure in the entire corpus satisfies is not manufacturing a pattern — it is reporting one.

"Then the NT contradicts itself — the same Jesus grows, prays, dies, and does not know the hour" Hard

Strongest form

Your five lines collide with five counter-lines: ignorance, fatigue, prayer, death. Honest reading says the NT is conflicted about Jesus, and you have curated one half.

Reply

The NT itself holds both halves ON PURPOSE — often in the same breath: the Creator of all things 'became flesh'; the one in the form of God 'emptied himself'; the upholder of the universe 'made purification for sins' (Hebrews 1:3 — one verse, both truths). That is not editorial confusion; it is the doctrine of the incarnation, stated before any council named it. The counter-lines describe what the Son took on; the HANDS lines describe what he never ceased to be — and a reading that keeps every text beats a reading that must delete half of them.

“An exalted agent could carry all five” — the shaliach reading Hard

Strongest form

In the Hebrew Bible an emissary bears the sender’s name and authority — the angel of the LORD speaks as God, Moses is made “like God to Pharaoh” (Exodus 7:1) — without being the LORD. Jewish law put it as the shaliach principle: a man’s agent is as the man himself. So Jesus is God’s supreme agent, given the divine name, the divine works, and a relative honour owed to the one who perfectly represents God — while remaining a distinct and subordinate being. And notice how much of your own evidence fits: the New Testament constantly says he was sent, given, appointed. That is agent language.

Reply

This is the best objection on the board, and the agency motif really is there — the sent/given/appointed language fits it naturally, and that should be granted. But it breaks at one point. A gesture of homage was never the line: Israelites bowed before a king and before the LORD with the same verb (1 Chronicles 29:20). What Jewish monotheism guarded for God alone was the substance of worship — sacrifice, invocation, prayer. Angels, prophets and kings could be honoured; none of them could be prayed to. And in 1 Corinthians 16:22 Paul, writing Greek to a Greek city, leaves one Aramaic phrase untranslated: Marana tha — “Our Lord, come!” That is prayer, addressed to Jesus, originating among the earliest Aramaic-speaking believers in Judea. A figure you worship and pray to, while confessing in the same breath that God is one, has already outrun the category of supreme agent. Nor does an agent do the sender’s unshareable work of creating all things (John 1:3; Col. 1:16). The agency category explains the sending; it breaks at the altar.
