The Uniqueness of the Resurrection Claim

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and stay clear and calm in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

A common objection holds that the resurrection of Jesus was simply borrowed from pagan "dying-and-rising god" myths, or that it evolved naturally out of Jewish hope. This argument turns that around: the resurrection claim cannot be explained away as the predictable product of its Jewish environment, nor as a Jewish edition of a pagan myth.

The pagan parallels do not fit. Figures like Osiris belong to seasonal, mythic-time fertility cycles; none is a named, datable man bodily raised within history. The Jewish background does not fit either. First-century Jewish hope expected one general resurrection of all the righteous at the end of history — not a single individual raised in the middle of it, and certainly not a crucified Messiah, since crucifixion marked someone as under God's curse (Deuteronomy 21:23). Nobody, the historian N. T. Wright argues, expected one person to rise ahead of everyone else.

Honest scope: this argument shows the resurrection claim was not borrowed; on its own it does not prove the resurrection happened — that case is built from the early creed of 1 Corinthians 15:3-7, the empty tomb, and the appearances. What it does establish is that the proclamation cannot be dismissed as a predictable borrowing. Uniqueness is not truth: novel false beliefs arise all the time.

The argument, formally

A mutation that cries out for explanation.

P1

If the resurrection story were borrowed or evolved, it should be the predictable product of its supposed sources — pagan dying-rising myths or Jewish resurrection hope.

P2

The first Christians did not abandon the Jewish hope; they radically modified it — the resurrection everyone reserved for the last day had happened to one man, in advance. And the pagan parallels are gods bound to the seasonal cycle, mythic and timeless, not historical and datable.

∴ C

Therefore the resurrection claim cannot be explained away as a predictable product of Judaism or a Jewish edition of a pagan myth. Uniqueness is not truth; the positive case rests on the early creed, the appearances and the empty tomb — and our answer is the one the first witnesses gave: Jesus of Nazareth was raised bodily from the dead.

This argument turns the sceptic's favourite weapon: the very strangeness that makes the claim 'unbelievable' is what sinks the borrowing explanation — though uniqueness is not truth.

The key concept — a mutation within Jewish hope.

Historians of religion track how beliefs mutate from precursors. Wright's point is that the first Christians did not abandon the Jewish hope; they radically modified it. They claimed that the resurrection of the dead — an event everyone agreed was reserved for the last day — had happened to one man, in advance, in the middle of history. The mutation is so specific, and so unmotivated by prior expectation, that it cries out for explanation.

Defending Premise 1

If the resurrection story were borrowed or evolved, it should be the predictable product of its supposed sources — pagan dying-rising myths or Jewish resurrection hope.

Precision check: uniqueness is not truth. That an idea is unprecedented does not make it correct; novel false beliefs arise all the time. The claim is narrower: the resurrection claim cannot be explained away as the predictable product of its Jewish environment, nor as a Jewish edition of a pagan myth, nor as one more tale of a hero taken up to the gods. And what the claim is, as it surfaces in the earliest sources — the pre-Pauline formula of 1 Corinthians 15:3–7, dated by most scholars to within a few years of the crucifixion — is concrete: Jesus “died,” “was buried,” “was raised on the third day,” and “appeared.” The Greek word translated “raised,” egēgertai, is a perfect passive that, in its Jewish setting, meant the reanimation and transformation of a corpse.

The observation is accurate — the inference is not Concession first

The most popular reduction of Easter is that it is a Jewish version of a pan-Mediterranean myth: the god who dies and rises — Osiris in Egypt, Adonis in Syria, Tammuz (Dumuzi) in Mesopotamia, Dionysus in Greece — reborn each year with the crops, celebrated in the mystery cults. The classic statement is Sir James Frazer’s The Golden Bough (first edition 1890); in our own day the thesis has been revived by the mythicist Richard Carrier. The observation behind it is accurate as far as it goes. There are ancient stories of gods who descend into death and return; the cults did celebrate them; and Christianity did spread in precisely that religious environment. To wave the observation away is intellectually cheap; to grant the inference is another matter.

The strongest critics are historians of religion, not apologists Standard reference treatment

The decisive blow came from Jonathan Z. Smith, the eminent (and religiously skeptical) University of Chicago scholar. In his entry “Dying and Rising Gods” for the Encyclopedia of Religion (1987), Smith concluded that the category is “largely a misnomer based on imaginative reconstructions and exceedingly late or highly ambiguous texts.” Examined closely, he argued, the alleged cases collapse into two different groups: gods who disappear but do not truly die, and gods who die but do not truly return. There is, Smith wrote, no unambiguous instance in the history of religions of a dying and rising deity.

Honest limit: the fullest modern treatment is Tryggve N. D. Mettinger’s The Riddle of Resurrection (2001). Mettinger, a Swedish Hebrew Bible scholar, deliberately set out to test Smith — and here we must concede what should be conceded. Against Smith’s near-total skepticism, Mettinger concluded that some genuine dying-and-rising figures did exist in pre-Christian times: Dumuzi, Baal, and Melqart he judged to be dying-and-rising gods, with Adonis and Eshmun as probable further cases. This is an important concession; the question is not closed, though Mettinger reached his verdict against what remains the prevailing view.

Mettinger’s own verdict on the comparison with Jesus From the scholar the mythicists lean on

“There is, as far as I am aware, no prima facie evidence that the death and resurrection of Jesus is a mythological construct, drawing on the myths and rites of the dying and rising gods of the surrounding world. While studied with profit against the background of Jewish resurrection belief, the faith in the death and resurrection of Jesus retains its unique character in the history of religions.” The reasons he gives include these: the pagan figures are gods bound to the seasonal cycle, their “deaths” are mythic and timeless rather than historical and datable, and there is nothing corresponding to the Christian linkage of resurrection with a once-for-all atoning death.

Objection — “But Adonis and Attis came first”

The dying-and-rising objection deserves its strongest form, and that is not the internet version with its virgin births and December birthdays. Even Mettinger concluded that some genuine dying-and-rising figures did exist in pre-Christian times. Tammuz was mourned at the very gate of Jerusalem’s temple, where Ezekiel saw “women weeping for Tammuz” (Ezekiel 8:14). At Byblos, on Lucian’s report, the rites of Adonis moved from mourning to a day on which his image was “brought out into the light” and “addressed as if alive.” The spring festival of Cybele set a Day of Blood (March 24) beside a Day of Joy (March 25) — a sequence many readers have taken for a pagan Good Friday and Easter in honour of her consort Attis. Osiris was murdered, dismembered, and made whole again. So, the objector concludes, the shape of the Easter story — death, lament, third-day rejoicing — was already on the first Christians’ doorstep, and they had only to borrow it.

Reply

The answer turns on two questions the parallel never asks: when is each rising first attested, and what, exactly, rises? Adonis. As Jonathan Z. Smith puts it, “There is no suggestion of Adonis rising.” “Only late texts, largely influenced by or written by Christians, claim that there is a subsequent day of celebration for Adonis having been raised from the dead.” The earliest of them, Lucian’s second-century account, is ambiguous: addressing a statue “as if alive” was what any image-using cult of the Mediterranean did, and Lucian adds that the women then cut their hair as a sign of mourning. The older Greek story, in which Adonis is decreed to spend part of the year above and part below, is in Smith’s words a “tradition of bilocation” that “has no suggestion of death and rebirth”—a seasonal alternation of the kind met below with Dumuzi. Every text that has Adonis raised from the dead, in other words, was written after the gospel was already being preached. Mettinger takes these late witnesses more seriously than Smith does; yet even he, his reviewer notes, “resists a conclusive argument on the pre-Roman antiquity of the Near Eastern Adonis’s resurrection.” Attis. In the old Phrygian and Lydian versions Attis is killed and “in neither case,” Smith writes, “is there any question of his returning to life.” As for the Day of Joy, Smith notes that it was a late addition to the festival and that within the cult it “celebrates Cybele”; the one ancient text that connects it with Attis at all is a late-antique biography by the Neoplatonist Damascius, reporting a dream. Smith’s verdict: “Neither myth nor ritual offers any warrant for classifying Attis as a dying and rising deity.” Attis is not among the figures even Mettinger accepts. Osiris. Here the story is early, but the event is different in kind: in Smith’s words, “he did not return to his former mode of existence but rather journeyed to the underworld, where he became the powerful lord of the dead.” Tammuz (Dumuzi). Honesty requires saying that the dating argument does not dispose of this one. Dumuzi is far older than Christianity, and he stands on Mettinger’s list. In the Sumerian Descent of Inanna he is dragged to the underworld as the goddess’s substitute, and a later-recovered portion of the text arranges for him to spend half the year below while his sister takes his place for the other half. But look at what his “rising” is: Smith reads this as the opposite of triumph over death — “The myth emphasizes the inalterable power of the realm of the dead, not triumph over it. No one ascends from the land of the dead unless someone takes his or her place.” The same holds for Baal and Melqart: their descent and return are tied, on Mettinger’s own account, to the seasons, and belong to gods in mythic time, not to a man in a known year. And to a Jew the Tammuz rite was not raw material but a scandal: Ezekiel is shown the women weeping for Tammuz as one of the “abominations” committed at the LORD’s own house (Ezekiel 8:13–15).

So the objection fails, and it fails on the specifics. Where the parallel sounds closest — Adonis and Attis — it is late. Where the figure is genuinely old — Dumuzi, Baal, Melqart — the “rising” is loose: a seasonal alternation of a god in mythic time, not a man leaving a grave. And where the story is both old and vivid — Osiris — it is unlike: the god does not come back to this world at all, but reigns over the dead. None of this denies Mettinger’s finding that some ancient gods were believed to die and return; it denies that any of them is the source of Easter, which is precisely what Mettinger himself denied. The objection does not hold.

Objection — the cumulative “atmosphere” of syncretism

The mythicist can press: granting that no single pagan story matches, perhaps Christianity assembled its claim from fragments — a death here, a return there, a communal meal from the mysteries — and the very datedness of Jesus’ story is the Jewish historicizing contribution. Carrier’s case rests not on one clean parallel but on a cumulative atmosphere of syncretism, and he marshals Mettinger’s concessions to argue the raw materials were available.

Reply

The reply is twofold. First, Carrier leans on Mettinger while Mettinger himself explicitly denied that the materials were used this way — a tension Bart Ehrman, himself an agnostic New Testament scholar, has pressed, noting that the relevant evidence is ambiguous and does not bear the weight mythicism places on it. Second, the charge of “parallelomania” — Samuel Sandmel’s term for the scholarly habit of treating superficial similarity as genetic dependence — applies with full force: a death and a return are not a resurrection unless the specific contours match, and they do not. The environment was syncretistic, and that much is accurate; the burden, though, is to show borrowing rather than coincidence, and that burden remains unmet.

The room: Frazer built the pattern and Carrier has revived it; Jonathan Z. Smith (religiously skeptical) dismantled it; Mettinger, who set out to test Smith, conceded some genuine cases yet denied they explain Easter — no friend of Christian apologetics, and the very scholar the mythicists lean on. The biblical scholar Mark S. Smith went further, titling his 1998 survey “The Death of ‘Dying and Rising Gods’ in the Biblical World” — an obituary Mettinger contests for the category itself, but not for the borrowing thesis.
Defending Premise 2

The first Christians did not abandon the Jewish hope; they radically modified it — the resurrection everyone reserved for the last day had happened to one man, in advance. And the pagan parallels are gods bound to the seasonal cycle, mythic and timeless, not historical and datable.

Precision check: the specific claim under study is a bodily rising, distinguished from a merely spiritual survival or exaltation-of-the-soul reading — it narrows what “resurrection” asserts, not who Christ is. This is not a ghost and not a metaphor. Paul insists the body is “raised” (1 Corinthians 15:42–44) — changed, glorified, no longer subject to death, but continuous with the body that died. The premise establishes that the claim is unusual, not that it is true; what remains contested is not whether the resurrection claim is unusual — that is broadly granted — but what best explains its origin.

Jewish resurrection hope: bodily, collective, future Widely accepted

The Anglican scholar N. T. Wright, in his massive study The Resurrection of the Son of God (2003), surveys the entire spectrum of ancient belief about life after death and arrives at a careful definition: resurrection is “life after life-after-death.” By Jesus’ day this hope had developed, especially among the Pharisees, from seeds in Isaiah 26:19, Daniel 12:2, and Ezekiel 37’s vision of the dry bones. Crucially, Wright observes, when ancient Jews spoke of resurrection they meant something bodily, collective, and future: it would happen to all the righteous together, at the end of the age, as part of God’s restoration of the world. What no one pictured was a single man rising, alone, in the middle of ordinary history. There was no slot in the Jewish imagination for it — not the raising of a corpse that would die again (as Lazarus was raised), nor the loose rumour that a prophet like John the Baptist had “come back,” but a single man passing through death into deathless, transformed life ahead of everyone else. The first Christians did not abandon the Jewish hope; they radically modified it. They claimed that the resurrection of the dead — an event everyone agreed was reserved for the last day — had happened to one man, in advance, in the middle of history. The mutation is so specific, and so unmotivated by prior expectation, that it cries out for explanation.

Honest limit: Wright’s account is widely accepted even by those who reject his conclusions, though critics such as the historian James G. Crossley have argued that Wright overstates the historical security of the empty tomb — a genuinely contested claim.

A crucified Messiah was a contradiction in terms Supporting

Deuteronomy 21:23 — hanged on a tree means cursed by God. A crucified Messiah was not an awkward sell; it was a contradiction in terms, which is why Paul calls it a stumbling block to Jews (1 Corinthians 1:23; cf. Galatians 3:13). You do not invent your own refutation.

Bodily and datable, not mythic and timeless Decisive for the comparison

Two features anchor the claim in this world rather than a mythic elsewhere. First, it is tied to an empty tomb. All four Gospels report it, and — a detail many historians find telling — women are the first witnesses, which in a culture that discounted female testimony is an awkward thing to invent. Second, the risen Jesus is portrayed as physical: he eats fish (Luke 24:42–43), invites touch (John 20:27), yet also passes through doors and is transformed. A myth that recurs every spring with the vegetation is a different kind of thing from a claim about a named man under a named governor (Pontius Pilate) in a known city in a particular year. Tammuz returns with the grain every year; Jesus is claimed to have risen once, on a particular morning, never to die again.

Honest limit: honesty requires flagging up front that the empty tomb is the more disputed half of the case — more contested than the appearances — and that the women-witnesses argument, while suggestive, is itself debated (some note that women were the expected ones to tend a grave). The point here is narrower: the claim is of the bodily, this-worldly kind, whatever one finally concludes about the tomb.

Heroes taken up differ in kind: translation, not resurrection Differs in kind

Romulus does not die and return in his body; he is removed from earth, and no empty tomb is at issue because there was no body to begin with — the storm simply takes him. This is translation, the same pattern as Enoch or Elijah, not resurrection. Wright underscores the distinction: pagan antiquity had plenty of room for souls surviving, for heroes divinized, for ghosts appearing — and precisely because it had those categories, it had no need to invent resurrection and generally regarded the idea of a body returning from the grave as undesirable or impossible. In Aeschylus’s Eumenides, the god Apollo declares that once the dust has drawn up a dead man’s blood there is “no resurrection” — the very word, anastasis, the New Testament would later use, here flatly denied. The Apollonius case is weaker even than Romulus’s: by Philostratus’s own admission, the memoir of Apollonius’s companion Damis—his principal source, and the only one written by a professed eyewitness—breaks off before Apollonius dies, and Philostratus falls back on frankly contradictory “stories” about his end.

Honest limit — direction of influence: where a resemblance to the Christian story does surface in later pagan writing, the classicist Glen Bowersock has argued that the debt may run the other way. His is a specific and debated scholarly thesis, not a consensus; but it is enough to show that the direction of influence in these late parallels cannot simply be presumed.

Objection — the visionary reduction (Lüdemann) Hardest

The more serious objection grants the disciples’ sincerity and explains it psychologically. Drawing on Leon Festinger’s cognitive-dissonance study When Prophecy Fails (1956), critics argue that a group whose messianic hopes were shattered by crucifixion would, rather than abandon belief, reinterpret and intensify it. The New Testament scholar Gerd Lüdemann developed the fullest version: Peter, wracked by grief and guilt, experienced a vision of Jesus; this triggered a “chain reaction” of further visions through the community. At its strongest, the objection presses that 1 Corinthians 15 bundles quite diverse experiences under a single formulaic list, and that the two hardest cases are individually explicable — Paul’s as a conversion vision, James’s as a reconciliation vision born of grief for a brother — so that no one mechanism need cover them all.

Reply

This account at least takes the disciples’ sincerity seriously rather than alleging fraud. But the replies are substantial. Gary Habermas and William Lane Craig have argued that grief hallucinations are private and individual, ill-suited to explain the multiple, group, and even hostile appearances (Paul, James) reported in 1 Corinthians 15. Moreover, Lüdemann’s framework presupposes that the disciples already held a category into which a risen-yet-individual messiah could fit — the very thing Wright shows they lacked. Even granting that Paul and James can be handled one at a time, Wright’s missing-category point still blocks Lüdemann’s “chain reaction”: a community with no slot for a single man risen ahead of the last day is an odd seedbed for visions of exactly that; and the bodily, tangible phenomenology the sources report — eating, being touched — together with the empty tomb resists a clean visionary reduction. Of the naturalistic reconstructions, the visionary theory is the least weak — but it still does not account for the group and hostile appearances, nor supply the category the disciples are shown to have lacked; it is not the best explanation of the full set of data.

Objection — the Gospels as “historicized myth”

The most sophisticated recent form of the borrowing case no longer claims that Christians copied any particular god. The scholar of ancient religion M. David Litwa argues that the Gospels were written and received not as myths but as historical records, and that the evangelists deliberately cast mythic material about Jesus in the form of historical narrative, the form ancient readers found most believable. This form of the case concedes something important: the Gospels present themselves, and were read, as history. The question is not about genre but about origin — whether the stories were myth first and history only in form.

Reply

That is precisely the question the earliest evidence can test, and the earliest evidence is not the Gospels. The resurrection claim is already a fixed formula in the creed Paul received, 1 Corinthians 15:3–7, dated by most scholars to within a few years of the crucifixion, with named witnesses who were still alive to be asked. A story shaped to look like history within the lifetime of the people it names is a much harder thing to produce than one written up generations later. Litwa’s comparison shows that ancient writers could dress myth as history; it does not show that this is what happened here, and the date of the earliest testimony tells against it.

The room: Wright (resurrection as bodily, collective, future — widely accepted even by those who reject his conclusions) holds the Jewish half; Crossley presses the empty tomb. Lüdemann and Festinger’s dissonance model press from the psychological side; Habermas and Craig answer. What this does not prove: this essay does not prove the resurrection on its own. It clears away the borrowing explanation; the positive case rests on the early creed, the appearances, and the empty tomb, argued elsewhere in this library.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: the visionary reduction — Lüdemann’s grief-and-guilt “chain reaction,” which grants the disciples’ sincerity. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"Zeitgeist — dying-rising gods were everywhere" Common

Strongest form

Horus, Mithras, Dionysus, Osiris — born of virgins, dead three days, risen. Christianity is mythology with a Jewish accent.

Reply

Concede what should be conceded: Mettinger's careful audit found that some genuine dying-and-rising figures did exist before Christianity — Dumuzi, Baal, Melqart. The category is not empty and we should not pretend it is. What fails is the derivation. The popular list dissolves on inspection: Horus is not born of a virgin nor raised; Roman Mithras is born from a rock and never dies; Osiris is reassembled to rule the dead, not returned to life. These lists trace to 19th-century compilations and internet documentaries, not to the texts — which is why, after Jonathan Z. Smith's 1987 treatment, the simple dying-and-rising-god derivation is now widely abandoned. Specifics, on request, one god at a time.

"Judaism had exceptions — Elijah rose to heaven, Lazarus was raised" Common

Strongest form

Your own scriptures have individuals beating death before the end. The category was not closed.

Reply

Distinguish three things the sources distinguish: escape (Elijah taken, never died), resuscitation (Lazarus, returned to mortal life, died again), and resurrection (raised to immortal, glorified bodily life — the end-time event). The first two existed; the claim about Jesus is the third — 'the firstfruits' of the final harvest, arrived early for one man. That category had no precedent, which is the point.

"2 Maccabees shows martyrs expecting bodily resurrection — the hope was personal already" Trickier

Strongest form

The seven brothers die expecting God to restore their very bodies. Individual bodily hope predates Jesus — your closed category was open.

Reply

Read when they expect it: 'the King of the universe will raise us up to an everlasting renewal of life' (2 Macc 7:9, NRSV) — at the end, with all the righteous. Maccabees confirms exactly the frame the argument states: bodily, yes; individual-in-the-middle-of-history, never. No Maccabean martyr's followers claimed he had already been raised. The claim about Jesus is something else: not the raising of a corpse that would die again, nor the loose rumour that a prophet like John the Baptist had "come back," but a single man passing through death into deathless, transformed life ahead of everyone else.

"Desperation invents — cognitive dissonance theory explains it" Trickier

Strongest form

Festinger showed failed-prophecy groups double down with new claims. Shattered disciples resolving dissonance would generate exactly this innovation.

Reply

Festinger's groups doubled down on existing frameworks — recalculated dates, spiritualised the prophecy. The available Jewish moves were exactly those: he is exalted in heaven, vindicated at the end, or find a new messiah (as followers of other slain claimants did — and those movements died or moved on). Dissonance theory predicts the conventional swerve; what happened was an unprecedented claim — bodily, now, tomb empty (the empty tomb is the more disputed half of the case) — and costly. Theories that predict the wrong output do not explain the data.

"Unique just means we lost the sources — absence of parallels is not evidence" Hard

Strongest form

Antiquity is mostly lost. Maybe the parent traditions existed and perished. You are arguing from silence.

Reply

The survey is not of random survivals but of the two best-documented religious environments of the era — second-Temple Judaism (vast surviving literature: Qumran, apocalyptic, rabbinic roots) and Greco-Roman cult (whose myths we possess precisely because they were everywhere). In both, the conceptual grammar itself — not just examples — runs against the claim. And the burden cuts the other way: the borrowing theory asserts parents; asserting invisible ones is the argument from silence.

"Originality proves creativity, not truth — every founder innovates" Hard

Strongest form

Joseph Smith was original too. Unprecedented claims are what new religions are made of; strangeness is marketing, not evidence.

Reply

Innovators innovate along the grain of their audience — Smith gave restorationist America new scripture in King James idiom. These innovators went against their own grain: proclaiming, as Torah-observant Jews in Jerusalem, a Torah-cursed Messiah bodily raised ahead of schedule — the least marketable claim available, at the price of floggings and execution, anchored to a tomb (the empty tomb is the more disputed half of the case). Creativity optimises for acceptance; this claim cut against the grain of its own soil. Uniqueness is not truth — but a mutation so specific, and so unmotivated by prior expectation, cries out for explanation.
