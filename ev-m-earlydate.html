The Early Date of the New Testament

The deep dive tells you what the argument is. This track makes you able to use it — reconstruct it from memory, answer its hardest objections, and hold the line in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

When was the New Testament written, and is it early enough to be trusted? The argument is that a document written within the lifetime of eyewitnesses is too early to be a developed legend that could pass unchallenged. The strongest evidence is not the narrative Gospels but what lies beneath them: in 1 Corinthians 15:3–7 Paul quotes a fixed creed about Jesus' death, burial and resurrection appearances that scholars across the spectrum — including skeptics — date to within just a few years of the crucifixion. The resurrection proclamation is therefore at the foundation, not a late addition.

Other clues point the same way. Paul's letters were written in the AD 50s. Acts never reports the deaths of Paul, Peter or James, nor the destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70 — events a later historian would have had every reason to mention — which suggests Luke wrote in the early 60s and his Gospel earlier still. By ancient standards even the conventional dating keeps the Gospels within living memory, when hostile witnesses in Jerusalem could in principle have refuted fabrication.

The honest scope: an early date rules out slow, undetected legendary development but not sincere error or deliberate fabrication — the harder questions of honesty, accuracy, and the resurrection itself are taken up on their own terms. Most scholars date Mark around AD 70 and the others later; this argument does not depend on the earliest dating, because the creed and letters the great majority of scholars, skeptics included, accept already anchor the central claims in the 30s and 50s — and the truth of the central claim is argued on its own evidence, where this Library defends the inference that Jesus rose bodily from the dead.

Before you start · key terms

Eight words this argument leans on

Learn these first — the objections below turn on them, and most weak versions of the argument come from blurring one of them.

The eyewitness windowA document produced within the lifetime of eyewitnesses is too early to be a fully developed legend that could circulate unchallenged. The conclusion is modest: it tells us the material is early, not that it is accurate.
The two-generation ruleThe Oxford Roman historian A. N. Critics such as Richard Carrier note, accurately, that this was an observation drawn from a particular body of Greek material, not a demonstrated law of folklore; the premise needs only the comparative point that more time and more distance make undetected legend easier.

The undisputed Pauline lettersRomans, the Corinthian correspondence, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians and Philemon are dated by near-universal agreement to roughly AD 48–60. They already presuppose the central narrative of cross and resurrection — which establishes early attestation, how soon the claims are on record, not by itself that their content is true.
The silence of ActsActs ends with Paul under house arrest in Rome and never reports the deaths of Paul, Peter or James the brother of Jesus, the Neronian persecution, or the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70. On this reading Luke wrote Acts in the early 60s — but it is genuinely an argument from silence, only as strong as the certainty that the author would have mentioned the missing event.
Vaticinium ex eventuA “prophecy after the fact.” The mainstream view reads Jesus’s prediction of Jerusalem’s destruction in Mark 13 as most simply explained by an Evangelist writing after AD 70 and shaping the prophecy in light of what had occurred. It is not special pleading as a method, but the inference is far less decisive than it sounds.
The latest possible dateA document cannot be later than the first author who quotes it, and 1 Clement, Ignatius and Polycarp already treat New Testament writings as known and authoritative. What this shows is a ceiling, not a floor: it rules out composition centuries after Jesus, but it does not decide between the mainstream dates and the earlier ones.
Graeco-Roman biography (bios)The finding, from a scholar who set out to disprove it, that the Gospels were composed in the genre of ancient biography, a literary form whose purpose is to convey historical information about a real person. That is a claim about genre, not a guarantee of accuracy: ancient bioi still selected and shaped their material around a theme.
The argument, formally

Three lines. The whole weight rests on the two premises.

P1

A document written within the lifetime of eyewitnesses is too early to be a developed legend that could pass unchallenged.

P2

The core New Testament material falls inside that eyewitness window — and its earliest layers, Paul's undisputed letters of the 50s and the 1 Corinthians 15 creed, fall startlingly early.

∴ C

Therefore, the New Testament is too early to be legend and must be assessed as first-generation testimony.

The logic is valid — if both premises are true, the conclusion follows. So objections come in two families: attacks on P1 (whether an early date rules out legend) or P2 (whether the books really are that early), and attacks on the further step from the conclusion to what it is taken to show. Knowing which premise an objection targets is half the battle.

Then the conceptual step — what does an early date buy you?

Reasoning from the conclusion: if the books are early, hostile witnesses and living eyewitnesses were still around to refute fabrication — which dramatically constrains how much invention is possible.

What follows — and what doesn’t

Don’t read the conclusion — reason to it

Working only from what the argument has established: what does each piece of evidence rule out, and how far does the inference reach? Reason each line out, then reveal.

Paul quotes a creed he “received,” and the tradition must predate his Jerusalem visit three years after his conversion — so its content dates…?
within a few years of the crucifixionPaul’s own conversion is usually placed within a few years of the crucifixion, and in Galatians 1:18–19 he meets Cephas and James — two of the named witnesses in the creed — three years later. The tradition he relays must predate that meeting. That is why the dating crosses ideological lines: even Gerd Lüdemann, who rejects a bodily resurrection, dates its elements to “the first two years after the crucifixion… not later than three years.”

Defending Premise 1

A document written within the lifetime of eyewitnesses is too early to be a developed legend that could pass unchallenged.

Precision check: the conclusion is modest — it tells us the material is early, not that it is accurate. An early date rules out one explanation, slow, undetected legendary development; it does not rule out sincere error or deliberate fabrication, because people can and do invent things quickly. Early does not mean true.

Legends need time and distance Supporting

The classic statement comes from the Oxford Roman historian A. N. Sherwin-White, who turned the historian’s eye on the Gospels. Working from how legends actually behave in the ancient sources — he used Herodotus as his test case — Sherwin-White argued that even two full generations was too short an interval for legend to wipe out a solid historical core. For the Gospels the interval is shorter still. Where living memory remains, an invented account does not float free; it collides with people who were present.

Honest limit: The skeptic Richard Carrier and others note that Sherwin-White’s “two-generation rule” was an observation drawn from a particular body of Greek material, not a demonstrated law of folklore — and that much is accurate; rapid legendary embellishment is not impossible, and the rate at which stories mutate varies enormously. Premise One does not need an iron law. It needs only the comparative point that more time and more distance make undetected legend easier.

Hostile witnesses were a live constraint Supporting

According to Acts, the earliest preaching of the resurrection happened in Jerusalem, weeks after the crucifixion, to crowds that included the very authorities who had engineered Jesus’s death. Claims about an empty tomb or public events could in principle have been falsified on the spot. Early dating keeps that adversarial check in force, which is exactly what a slow-growing legend needs to escape.

The window is unusually tight Comparative

The New Testament window is unusually tight by the standards of ancient history — where our best surviving biographies of Alexander the Great, by Arrian and Plutarch, were written some four centuries after him and are still mined for a historical core.

Objection — “Early doesn’t mean accurate — people lie fast too”

Conspiracy theories form within days. An early date rules out slow legend but not deliberate fabrication, so it proves nothing about whether the events happened.

Reply

Correct that early dating targets legend, not lying — and that’s the right division of labor. This argument removes one whole class of explanation (gradual mythologizing) so what remains is honest report, sincere error, or deliberate fabrication. The harder questions of honesty, accuracy, and the resurrection itself are taken up on their own terms; sincere error is weighed where this Library defends the inference that Jesus rose bodily from the dead. Each argument carries its own part of the load.

The room: Sherwin-White (even two generations is too short for legend to erase a historical core); Carrier and others press that his rule is an observation from particular Greek material, not a demonstrated law of folklore.
Defending Premise 2

The core New Testament material falls inside that eyewitness window — and its earliest layers, Paul’s undisputed letters of the 50s and the 1 Corinthians 15 creed, fall startlingly early.

Precision check: the second premise does not require the most aggressive possible dating of the four Gospels. The strongest version of the case rests on the layers that the great majority of scholars — believer and skeptic alike — already grant to be early: Paul’s undisputed letters of the 50s, and the pre-Pauline tradition embedded inside them. The case is firm for the central proclamation, and a contested but defensible position for the narrative Gospels.

The creed behind 1 Corinthians 15 Firmest ground

In 1 Corinthians 15:3–7, writing in the mid-50s, Paul hands on what he himself had “received”: that Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures, and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the Twelve, then to more than five hundred, to James, and to all the apostles. The technical language of “delivering” and “receiving” tradition, the non-Pauline vocabulary, the rhythmic structure, and the Aramaic name Cephas all mark this out as a pre-formed oral creed that Paul is quoting, not composing. Paul’s own conversion is usually placed within a few years of the crucifixion, and in Galatians 1:18–19 he describes going up to Jerusalem to meet Cephas (Peter) and James — two of the named witnesses in the creed — three years later. The German New Testament scholar Gerd Lüdemann — who emphatically does not believe Jesus rose bodily and explains the appearances as visions — nonetheless concluded that “the elements in the tradition are to be dated to the first two years after the crucifixion… not later than three years.”

Honest limit: Gary Habermas, who has surveyed the literature most exhaustively, reports that a wide range of scholars across the spectrum who address the question place the formula within the first few years — though his survey should be cited with care, since it is a count of published positions assembled by one researcher rather than a randomized poll. And the creed does not date the Gospels.

Paul: a checkable link back to the eyewitnesses Strong · narrowly read

Three years after his conversion Paul lodged for a fortnight with Cephas — the first name in the creed. Fourteen years later he went up to Jerusalem again and deliberately “set before” its leaders — James, Cephas, and John, the reputed “pillars” — the gospel he had been preaching (Galatians 2:2). What it shows is narrower and still weighty: that the message Paul preached independently was set before the leading men of the Jerusalem church, those who had known Jesus, and was endorsed by them rather than corrected. The line from the events to the creed runs through named people who had access to one another and to the events.

Honest limit: We should not overstate what this shows: Paul is at pains to say that the gospel he preached was “not man’s gospel” but came “through a revelation of Jesus Christ” (Galatians 1:11–12), so this is not a report of Paul being taught the tradition by Peter, nor of the eyewitnesses auditing his memory against theirs. The dispute in Galatians 2 was about the terms on which Gentiles could be included, not a line-by-line audit of the tradition.

Paul’s undisputed letters Near-universal

Paul’s undisputed letters — Romans, the Corinthian correspondence, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians, Philemon — are dated by near-universal agreement to roughly AD 48–60. They are first-generation documents, written by a man who knew the original apostles, and they already presuppose the central narrative of cross and resurrection. That places the load-bearing claims well inside living memory.

Honest limit: This establishes early attestation — how soon the claims are on record — not, by itself, that their content is true, which is argued on separate grounds.

The silence of Acts Suggestive · contested

Acts ends abruptly, with Paul under house arrest in Rome. It does not report the martyrdom of Paul, nor of Peter, nor the death of James the brother of Jesus (AD 62, recorded by Josephus), nor the Neronian persecution, nor — most conspicuous of all — the destruction of Jerusalem and its Temple in AD 70. Luke, who narrates the martyrdom of Stephen at length and records that of James son of Zebedee (Acts 12:2), and who returns again and again to the theme of the Temple, would have had every reason to mention these catastrophes had they already happened. On this reading, Luke wrote Acts in the early 60s, which places his Gospel (and Mark, one of its sources) earlier still.

Honest limit: The argument from silence in Acts is genuinely an argument from silence, and such arguments are only as strong as the certainty that the author would have mentioned the missing event — a judgment about which reasonable scholars differ. Luke may have ended Acts where his narrative purpose was complete, with the gospel reaching Rome.

Seccombe’s further considerations Cumulative · contested

David Seccombe has pressed two considerations that do not turn on what Acts fails to mention. The first is political climate: from the outbreak of the Jewish revolt in AD 66, an intense anti-Jewish mood settled over the Roman world, and in that atmosphere the opening chapters of Luke read as dangerous material to own or circulate. The second concerns purpose: Acts devotes whole chapters (10–11 and 15) to arguing that Gentiles may be received without circumcision — a debate whose very shape presupposes a Jewish church in the position of strength. After 70, with Judea crushed and the church increasingly Gentile, that argument had—on Seccombe’s reading—largely lost its occasion.

Honest limit: Both points are contested: scholars who date Luke–Acts later read the same features as compatible with a post-70 setting, and Seccombe himself presents his case as a set of further, cumulative reasons rather than a proof.

Patristic testimony Corroboration

The early church remembered who wrote the Gospels and traced them to the apostolic circle. The earliest such testimony comes from Papias of Hierapolis (writing c. AD 110–130), preserved by the church historian Eusebius. Papias reports, on the authority of “the Elder,” that Mark became Peter’s “interpreter” and wrote down what Peter recounted, though “not in order.” Later writers — Irenaeus around AD 180 — name all four Evangelists. This is external corroboration that the church tied its Gospels to eyewitnesses and their associates, not to anonymous late authors.

The latest possible date A ceiling, not a floor

A document cannot be later than the first author who quotes it. 1 Clement, commonly dated around AD 95–96, tells its readers “Take up the epistle of the blessed Paul the Apostle”; Ignatius of Antioch echoes 1 Corinthians 1:20 and repeats wording found only in Matthew 3:15; Polycarp of Smyrna quotes 1 Peter and cites 1 Corinthians 6:2 “as Paul teaches.”

Honest limit: These dates need care: Kirsopp Lake judged it safest to place 1 Clement “between 75 and 110 A.D.,” Thomas Herron has argued for about AD 70 and Laurence Welborn for a window reaching as late as 140; Timothy Barnes has argued that Ignatius wrote in the 140s. Even on the latest of these proposals, Paul’s letters and 1 Peter were already circulating and being cited as authorities, and Matthew was very probably known, by the first half of the second century. This shows a ceiling, not a floor: it rules out composition two centuries after Jesus, but it does not decide between the mainstream dates and the earlier ones. Like every argument here, it shows that the writings existed and were valued early, not that what they say is true.

Objection — as Ehrman would press it: you have rescued the creed, not the Gospels Hardest

You have rescued the early creed, granted, but you have not rescued the early Gospels, and it is the Gospels that contain the miracles, the empty tomb, the detailed narratives — so the gap you most needed to close, you have left open.

Reply

The first half is accurate: the creed does not date the Gospels. The second is not, because the argument never promised to close that gap. What the creed establishes is that the explanatory core — a crucified, buried, and risen Jesus, as the creed proclaims him, attested by named witnesses — is too early to be slow legend. The narrative details are a further question, addressed by other lines of evidence, including the finding that the Gospels were composed in the recognizable genre of Graeco-Roman biography (bios). That is a claim about genre, not a guarantee of accuracy: ancient bioi still selected and shaped their material around a theme.

Objection — “The Gospels were written 40–70 years later”

The standard consensus, articulated by Bart Ehrman among many others, dates Mark to around AD 70, Matthew and Luke to roughly 80–85, and John to 90–95. Matthew and Luke used Mark as a source, which requires Mark to have circulated first, and the decisive point is Mark 13, where Jesus predicts the encirclement and destruction of Jerusalem — most simply explained, on this view, by an Evangelist writing after AD 70 (vaticinium ex eventu). E. P. Sanders judged the Gospels probably composed between 70 and 90. Luke 21:20, with its talk of Jerusalem “surrounded by armies,” reads to many scholars as more specific than Mark—suggesting an author writing with the siege in view.

Reply

The vaticinium ex eventu reading is not special pleading as a method, and it cannot be refuted simply by insisting that Jesus could predict the future. But the inference is far less decisive than it sounds. The prediction in Mark 13 is conspicuously unspecific about the manner of Jerusalem’s fall — it says nothing of the fire that gutted the Temple, the months-long siege, or the legions of Titus. This is why E. P. Sanders, for all his post-70 dating of the Gospels, judged Mark’s prediction of the Temple’s destruction “probably pre-70.” The discourse’s most vivid imagery is drawn from Scripture, not from the events of 70, and even Luke’s “surrounded by armies” is the stock language of any siege. Nor does a prediction of the Temple’s fall require foreknowledge to be early: Josephus reports another Jesus, son of Ananias, crying woe on the city and the sanctuary from AD 62, years before the war. Most importantly, the early-dating case for the central claims does not stand or fall with Mark’s date at all: even on Ehrman’s own chronology, the creed of 1 Corinthians 15 and Paul’s letters carry the death, burial, and resurrection proclamation back into the 30s and 50s. Whether Mark is 65 or 72 changes little here.

Objection — “Was Acts written in the second century?”

Richard I. Pervo’s Dating Acts (2006) places Acts around AD 115 on three main grounds: that Luke used Josephus’s Antiquities (completed in AD 93/94), and that some of Acts’ historical tangles—above all Gamaliel’s mention of the rebels Theudas and Judas the Galilean (Acts 5:36–37)—are best explained as misreadings of Josephus; that the author knew a collection of Paul’s letters; and that Acts shares vocabulary, themes and outlook with writings he places in the early second century. Joseph B. Tyson argues that Acts and the canonical form of Luke were completed around 120–125 as a response to Marcion. It is a minority position, but one argued in detail by specialists in Acts.

Reply

Our verdict is that the case for a second-century date is not made. Its central link to Josephus is disputed at its strongest point. The real difficulty is that Gamaliel, speaking in the 30s, sets Theudas before Judas, whereas Josephus’s Theudas rose a decade or more later. It is a recognised historical problem. However it is resolved, Josephus’s sequence does not explain it. As Jonathan Bernier has argued, using a place where Luke diverges from Josephus to prove that Luke used Josephus exposes how weak the hypothesis is; the overlap is public Judean history that both men could know independently. The question is not closed — Steve Mason, a leading Josephus specialist, thinks Luke probably did know Josephus’s work — but even if he did, that would place Acts after the mid-90s, not establish a date around 115. Its argument from Paul’s letters is at least as well explained by an author who knew Paul, and the themes Tyson reads as anti-Marcionite already have a first-century occasion in the disputes over Gentile inclusion. The evidence leaves Acts where most scholars place it: in the first century. The live question is whether Acts was written in the early 60s or in the decades after 70.

The room: On the dating of the four Gospels, the mainstream consensus — Mark c. 70, the others in the 80s and 90s — remains the majority view, and the pre-70 dating championed by Robinson and Hemer, and restated more recently by Alexander Mittelstaedt (2006), Karl Armstrong (2017), and David Seccombe (2020), is a respected minority position, not the field’s settled judgment; a centrist commentator like Craig Keener, no partisan of the early date, still places Luke–Acts in the early 70s, with the 60s and 80s among the live possibilities. But on the dating of the earliest tradition — the 1 Corinthians 15 creed and the undisputed Pauline letters — there is broad agreement across the ideological spectrum, skeptics included, that the material is remarkably early.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

This is what separates someone who knows the Early Date of the NT from someone who can defend it. Each gets its best form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: Ehrman’s — you have rescued the creed, not the Gospels, and the Gospels carry the miracles. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"The Gospels were written 40–70 years later" Common

Strongest form

Even on the mainstream dating, Mark comes around AD 70 and John near the century's end. Decades are plenty of time for embellishment to creep in before anyone wrote.

Reply

By ancient standards the gap is unusually tight — our best biographies of Alexander the Great were written some four centuries after him and are still mined for a historical core. More importantly, the gap to the source material is far shorter: Paul's letters (50s) and the creed he cites (within a few years) carry the central claims well inside living memory, long before the narrative Gospels were finalized.

"Jesus predicts the Temple's fall, so it was written after AD 70" Trickier

Strongest form

Mark 13 has Jesus foretelling the destruction of the Temple in detail. The simplest explanation is that the author wrote after 70 and put the prophecy in his mouth — so the Gospels are post-70.

Reply

The post-70 reading is the majority view and isn't special pleading, but it is far less decisive than it sounds. The prediction in Mark 13 is conspicuously unspecific about the manner of Jerusalem's fall — it says nothing of the fire that gutted the Temple, the months-long siege, or the legions of Titus; even Luke's "surrounded by armies" is the stock language of any siege, and C. H. Dodd argued that Luke's picture is drawn from the Old Testament accounts of Jerusalem's fall in 587 BC rather than from Titus's campaign. E. P. Sanders, who judged the Gospels probably composed between 70 and 90, also judged Mark's prediction of the Temple's destruction "probably pre-70," since a prophecy written after the event would likely have mentioned the fire. Nor does a prediction of the Temple's fall require foreknowledge to be early: Josephus reports another Jesus, son of Ananias, crying woe on the city and the sanctuary from AD 62, years before the war (Jewish War 6.300–309). And the early-dating case for the central claims does not stand or fall with Mark's date at all: the creed of 1 Corinthians 15 and Paul's letters carry the death, burial, and resurrection proclamation back into the 30s and 50s.

Strongest form

Reply

Correct that early dating targets legend, not lying — and that's the right division of labor. This argument removes one whole class of explanation (gradual mythologizing) so what remains is honest report, sincere error, or deliberate fabrication. The harder questions of honesty, accuracy, and the resurrection itself are taken up on their own terms; sincere error is weighed where this Library defends the inference that Jesus rose bodily from the dead. Each argument carries its own part of the load.

"The argument from silence in Acts is weak" Hard

Strongest form

Authors omit things for many reasons. Luke may simply have ended Acts where his narrative purpose was complete; you can't date a book from what it fails to mention.

Reply

An argument from silence is only strong when the omission is one the author would almost certainly have included. Luke narrates the martyrdom of Stephen at length and records that of James son of Zebedee (Acts 12:2), yet stops with Paul under house arrest and never reports Paul's death, the Neronian persecution, or Jerusalem's fall — events central to his themes. It isn't any single silence but the convergence of several expected-yet-absent events that points to composition in the early 60s — though this remains an argument from silence, and Luke may have ended where his narrative purpose was complete.

"Most scholars date the Gospels later than you do" Hard

Strongest form

The mainstream consensus puts Mark around 70 and the others in the 80s–90s. Appealing to a minority early dating is special pleading against the experts.

Reply

The argument doesn't depend on the earliest possible dating of the narrative Gospels. Even the standard consensus places them within living memory of eyewitnesses — and concedes that Paul's letters and the 1 Corinthians 15 creed, which carry the central claims, are demonstrably early. Whether Mark is 65 or 72, the resurrection proclamation is anchored in the 30s. The case stands on the earliest layers the great majority of scholars, skeptics included, accept.

The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

On the dating of the four Gospels, the mainstream consensus — articulated by Bart Ehrman among many others: Mark c. 70, Matthew and Luke c. 80–85, John c. 90–95 — remains the majority view, and E. P. Sanders judged the Gospels probably composed between 70 and 90. The pre-70 dating championed by John A. T. Robinson and Colin Hemer, and restated more recently by Alexander Mittelstaedt, Karl Armstrong and David Seccombe, is a respected minority position, not the field’s settled judgment; a centrist commentator like Craig Keener, no partisan of the early date, still places Luke–Acts in the early 70s, with the 60s and 80s among the live possibilities. From the other side, a smaller group, the fullest statement is Richard Pervo’s, with Joseph Tyson supplying a setting, places Acts in the early second century. But on the dating of the earliest tradition — the 1 Corinthians 15 creed and the undisputed Pauline letters — there is broad agreement across the ideological spectrum, skeptics included, that the material is remarkably early.

A fairness most defenders skip: Honest defenders must grant the observations: the vaticinium ex eventu reading is not special pleading as a method, and the correspondence between Jesus’s words and the events of 70 is real; the argument from silence in Acts is genuinely an argument from silence, and Luke may have ended Acts where his narrative purpose was complete; Sherwin-White’s “two-generation rule” is an observation, not a demonstrated law of folklore; Habermas’s survey is a count of published positions assembled by one researcher, not a randomized poll; and the creed does not date the Gospels. None of that carries the critic’s inference. Mark 13 is conspicuously unspecific about the manner of Jerusalem’s fall, a convergence of expected-yet-absent events is more telling than any single omission, and the early-dating case for the central claims does not stand or fall with Mark’s date at all.

The bounded verdict: The foundational Christian claims about Jesus’s death and resurrection appearances are not the residue of generations of mythmaking; they are demonstrably early, fixed in creedal form within a few years of the events, named to specific witnesses, and circulated while hostile parties in Jerusalem could have objected. Even on the mainstream (later) dating, the Gospels fall within living memory, and the “legend had time to grow” hypothesis, in its strong form, does not fit the evidence. The pre-70 dating of the narrative Gospels remains a serious, defensible, but genuinely contested position — not a settled result. And early does not mean true: the argument clears the ground and hands the central claims over to be assessed as first-generation testimony, where the resurrection itself is argued on its own evidence — and this Library defends the inference that Jesus rose bodily from the dead.

    "The gap is real — but by ancient standards it is unusually tight. Biographies of Alexander written some four centuries after him are still mined for a historical core. Legends need time and distance to grow unchallenged, and here there was neither. So the question isn't 'did myth have time to form?' but 'were these witnesses telling the truth?' — and that's a fairer place to start."

    Gold gate · the unseen objection

    One you weren’t coached on

    Everything above rehearsed objections the page showed you. Here is the real test of mastery: an objection we deliberately did not drill. Reason it out cold from what the argument established, then reveal how it actually handles it. An honest “nailed it” is the first half of Gold.

    SR
    Skeptical reader
    "You call Paul a checkable link back to the eyewitnesses. But Paul insists the gospel he preached was “not man’s gospel” and came “through a revelation of Jesus Christ” (Galatians 1:11–12). By his own account Peter did not teach it to him — so the chain you are building through Jerusalem is not there."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    Paul does say that, and we should not overstate what the Jerusalem contacts show: this is not a report of Paul being taught the tradition by Peter, nor of the eyewitnesses auditing his memory against theirs. What it shows is narrower and still weighty. Three years after his conversion Paul lodged for a fortnight with Cephas, the first name in the creed; fourteen years later he deliberately “set before” James, Cephas and John the gospel he had been preaching (Galatians 2:2), and they “added nothing” to it and gave him “the right hand of fellowship” (2:6, 9) — requiring nothing further of him and recognizing his gospel as one with their own. The dispute there was about the terms on which Gentiles could be included, not a line-by-line audit of the tradition. So the message Paul preached independently was set before the leading men of the Jerusalem church, those who had known Jesus, and was endorsed by them rather than corrected. The line from the events to the creed does not vanish into anonymous hands; it runs through named people who had access to one another and to the events. So the objection corrects an overstatement, but the link stands in its narrower form.
