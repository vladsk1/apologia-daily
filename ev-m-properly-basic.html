Is Belief in God Properly Basic?

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and locate exactly where its weight rests.

The argument in brief

In 1876 the mathematician William Kingdon Clifford drew a principle that still sets the terms of the debate: “It is wrong always, everywhere, and for anyone, to believe anything upon insufficient evidence.” The answer developed over the last half-century by the philosopher Alvin Plantinga and his allies, usually called Reformed epistemology, is that a believer who has never worked through an argument for God need not be negligent at all. Belief in God, they argue, can be properly basic: rationally held, and possibly even knowledge, without being inferred from any argument.

One thing should be said at the outset. Reformed epistemology is not an argument that God exists. It is a reply to one particular charge, namely that belief in God is irrational unless it rests on argument. As a reply to that charge it is, in our judgment, strong. As a proof of God it was never intended, and should never be presented as one. It answers the classical form of the evidentialist objection by exposing the classical foundationalism behind it as too strict and, on Plantinga’s argument, self-defeating; broader evidentialisms survive, but no longer demand an argument. On Plantinga’s account of warrant, it removes the option of dismissing belief as irrational while declining to argue it is untrue. It does not tell an inquirer which God is real, and its general argument reaches only the rationality of belief in a personal Creator, not yet the triune God of the creeds. It is one strand in a cumulative case; for the specifically Christian claim, the decisive question is historical, set out in the minimal facts argument for the resurrection.

Before you start · key terms

Nine terms this argument turns on

Learn these first — above all basic, but not groundless and defeater.

Evidentialist objectionThe claim that belief in God is rationally acceptable only if there is sufficient evidence or argument for it.
Classical foundationalismThe view that a belief is properly basic only if it is self-evident, incorrigible (I cannot be mistaken about it), or, in the older ancient and medieval version, evident to the senses. Everything else must be supported, ultimately, by beliefs of those kinds.
Basic beliefA belief a person does not hold on the basis of other beliefs. My belief that there is a tree outside the window is basic: I do not reason my way to it from my visual sensations, I just see the tree.
Properly basicA belief is properly basic when it is rightly held in that way: when holding it without an argument involves no intellectual fault, and (in Plantinga’s later work) when it can amount to knowledge.
Basic, but not groundlessA perceptual belief is basic, but it is not groundless; it is formed in a characteristic experience. The experience is not a premise in an argument. It is the circumstance in which the belief arises, and it is what makes the belief appropriate.
DefeaterNot a proof that the belief is false, but a reason that undercuts your grounds for it. A defeater can itself be defeated — a defeater-defeater — and the original belief can stand again.
WarrantThe property that turns true belief into knowledge. Roughly: a belief has warrant when it is produced by cognitive faculties that are functioning properly, in an environment suited to them, according to a design plan successfully aimed at truth.
De facto / de jureA de facto objection says the belief is false. A de jure objection says the belief is irrational, unjustified, or unwarranted whether or not it is true.
FideismPlantinga takes fideism to be “exclusive or basic reliance upon faith alone, accompanied by a consequent disparagement of reason.” Reformed epistemology does not disparage reason.
The argument, carefully

Three lines — a reply to one charge, not a proof of God.

P1

Some beliefs are rational without being inferred from any argument — belief in other minds, memory, and perception, held in the right circumstances by properly working faculties and open to defeat.

P2

Belief in God can be formed in that same way — basic, but not groundless — in response to experiences of creation, conscience, and Scripture.

∴ C

Therefore belief in God can be rational without being based on an argument — though this does not prove that God exists.

Reformed epistemology is not an argument that God exists. It is a reply to one particular charge, namely that belief in God is irrational unless it rests on argument. It answers the classical form of the evidentialist objection by exposing the classical foundationalism behind it as too strict and, on Plantinga’s argument, self-defeating; broader evidentialisms survive, but no longer demand an argument. On Plantinga’s account of warrant, it removes the option of dismissing belief as irrational while declining to argue it is untrue.

From the charge to the reply

Honestly stated: Reformed epistemology does not prove that God exists. It does not tell an inquirer which God is real, or whether the Christian account is true rather than a rival one; its central conditional is explicitly silent on that question. Even granted in full, its general argument reaches only the rationality of belief in a personal Creator, not yet the triune God of the creeds; it does not by itself establish the Trinity, the incarnation, or the resurrection. Plantinga’s extended model shows how such beliefs could be warranted if they are true, but whether they are true has to be argued on other grounds. For the specifically Christian claim, the decisive question is historical: did Jesus of Nazareth rise from the dead? That case is set out in our essay on the minimal facts argument for the resurrection.

Defending Premise 1

Some beliefs are rational without being inferred from any argument — belief in other minds, memory, and perception, held in the right circumstances by properly working faculties and open to defeat.

Precision check: the claim is not that the beliefs are groundless — the most common misunderstanding of Reformed epistemology is that “properly basic” means “groundless”: believed for no reason at all, by sheer will. Plantinga insisted on the opposite. And a properly basic belief is not an unrevisable one: basic beliefs can be defeated.

Classical foundationalism condemns far too much Very widely accepted

Consider the beliefs that there are other people with minds like yours, that the world existed five minutes ago, that your chair still exists when you leave the room. None of these is self-evident or incorrigible, and none has been successfully derived from beliefs that are; the early modern attempts to build them up from the bare foundations are widely judged to have failed. If classical foundationalism were true, nearly everything you believe about the past, the physical world, and other people would be irrational. That is not a discovery about your beliefs; it is a reason to doubt the theory.

Honest narrowing: in our judgment few epistemologists today defend the strict version Plantinga attacked — and the critique of classical foundationalism does not reach every form of evidentialism.

The criterion fails its own test Plantinga’s argument

Classical foundationalism is itself a belief: the belief that a proposition is properly basic only if it is self-evident, incorrigible, or evident to the senses. Is that belief self-evident? Plainly not, since a great many thoughtful philosophers reject it. Is it incorrigible, or evident to the senses? No. Has anyone shown that it follows from beliefs that are? Plantinga argued that no one has, and that it is hard to see how anyone could. So by its own standard, the classical foundationalist ought not to accept classical foundationalism. Plantinga’s term for this was self-referential incoherence: the criterion fails the test it sets for everyone else.

Other minds, and Reid Parity

Plantinga had been circling this idea since his first book, God and Other Minds (1967). There he found each of the classic arguments for God, taken alone, less than conclusive, and found that the classic argument for other minds (the argument from analogy, by which I infer from your behavior that you have thoughts like mine) fared no better. Yet no one thinks belief in other minds is irrational. Plantinga’s conclusion was that if belief in other minds is rational despite the weakness of the arguments for it, then the weakness of the theistic arguments does not, by itself, make belief in God irrational. Behind this lies an older voice: the eighteenth-century Scottish philosopher Thomas Reid, who argued that perception, memory, and testimony are trustworthy sources of belief in their own right, needing no certificate from reasoning.

Basic, but not groundless — and open to defeat Built into the premise

A perceptual belief is basic, but it is not groundless; it is formed in a characteristic experience. I believe there is a tree because I am, as philosophers sometimes put it, appeared to in a tree-like way. The experience is not a premise in an argument. It is the circumstance in which the belief arises, and it is what makes the belief appropriate. Suppose you look across a field and see what appears to be a sheep, and you form the basic belief that there is a sheep in the field. Then the farmer tells you that he owns a dog that is often mistaken for a sheep. You now have a defeater: not a proof that there is no sheep, but a reason that undercuts your grounds for thinking there is. If the farmer then adds that the dog is away at the vet this week, you have a defeater for your defeater, and your original belief can stand again.

Objection — Refuting classical foundationalism does not refute evidentialism

Contemporary evidentialists such as Earl Conee and Richard Feldman defend a broader view on which a person’s evidence includes her experiences, not just other beliefs, so that a belief can be supported by how things seem to her without being inferred from a premise.

Reply

Honesty requires granting the qualification: refuting classical foundationalism does not refute every form of evidentialism. On a view like that, the gap between the evidentialist and the Reformed epistemologist narrows considerably, and much of the dispute turns into a quarrel about what counts as evidence. But it is worth noticing what has happened. The original challenge said that belief in God needs an argument. Once evidence is widened to include experience, that demand has, in effect, been withdrawn.

The room: Clifford’s principle still sets the terms of the debate; William James replied in “The Will to Believe” (1896), and Antony Flew proposed that the burden of proof lies entirely on the believer. The critique of classical foundationalism has been very widely accepted. Contemporary evidentialists such as Conee and Feldman hold a broader view — and, as internalist epistemologists, reject the externalist account of warrant on which the later version of Plantinga’s view depends.
Defending Premise 2

Belief in God can be formed in that same way — basic, but not groundless — in response to experiences of creation, conscience, and Scripture.

Precision check: strictly speaking, the beliefs that are properly basic are not the bare proposition “God exists” but more particular beliefs formed in particular circumstances. Each of these entails that God exists, so belief that God exists comes with them, with no theistic argument needed. And the claim is a reply to the charge of irrationality — not an argument that God exists.

Particular beliefs, formed in particular circumstances Plantinga’s proposal

Plantinga proposed that belief in God works the same way as perception. The properly basic beliefs are God made all this, formed while looking at the starry heavens or a flower; God disapproves of what I have done, formed in the experience of guilt; God forgives me, formed in the experience of confession; God is speaking to me, formed while reading Scripture. The pattern will sound familiar to readers of Scripture: “The heavens declare the glory of God” (Psalm 19:1); “the invisible things of him from the creation of the world are clearly seen, being understood by the things that are made” (Romans 1:20).

Honest limit: it overlaps heavily with the argument from religious experience (Swinburne treats an apparent experience of God as evidence; Alston, Plantinga’s close ally, an Episcopalian rather than a Calvinist, offers an account of Christian practice) — but its distinctive emphasis is that the experience need not function as a premise at all.

The de jure question depends on the de facto question Strong · on Plantinga’s account of warrant

A believer who has reflected carefully and still finds her belief compelling violates no intellectual duty, so she is not unjustified in that sense. The serious de jure question is whether her belief has warrant. But whether it has warrant depends on what produced it and whether those processes are aimed at truth. If God exists and has made human beings to know him, it is quite likely that he has given them faculties by which they can come to believe in him, and in that case belief in God, produced by those faculties, would very likely have warrant. If God does not exist, belief in God is probably produced by something like wish-fulfilment, and probably lacks warrant. So the de jure question is not independent of the de facto question. To show that Christian belief is unwarranted, the critic has to show, or at least give reason to think, that it is false.

Honest narrowing: that is the project’s main payoff: on that account, it removes the option of dismissing belief as irrational while declining to argue that it is untrue. Internalist epistemologists reject the externalist account of warrant on which it depends.

The Aquinas/Calvin model Conditional

To show how belief in God could have warrant, Plantinga offered his Aquinas/Calvin model. Plantinga’s model proposes that God has created human beings with a faculty, which, following Calvin, he calls the sensus divinitatis (the sense of divinity). In a wide range of circumstances (the glory of the night sky, the experience of danger or guilt or gratitude) it produces beliefs about God directly, without inference. If the model is true, those beliefs are properly basic and can have warrant, and when they are held firmly enough and are true, they can amount to knowledge. Plantinga does not claim to have shown that the model is true. He claims that it is possible, that there are no good objections to it that do not presuppose that Christianity is false, and that if Christianity is true, then the model, or something close to it, is very likely true as well.

Honest limit: whether there is a sensus divinitatis philosophy cannot settle; on Plantinga’s own account it stands or falls with theism. On Plantinga’s account, sin has cognitive as well as moral effects, so that the natural awareness of God can be dimmed, distorted, or suppressed — a model of human nature, not a verdict on your neighbor. It is not that unbelievers are stupid, dishonest, or irrational, and not that anyone can diagnose a particular person’s unbelief as a moral failure. The model is offered to show how belief could be warranted, not to pass judgment on those who do not believe. How far sin’s damage reaches is a matter on which Reformed, Catholic, and Orthodox theologians have long disagreed, and the essay does not adjudicate it.

Objection — Why not any belief at all? (the Great Pumpkin)

If belief in God can be properly basic, without argument, why not any belief at all? The objection’s force is that Reformed epistemology seems to open the gate to any belief at all: astrology, the Great Pumpkin, or whatever anyone happens to feel strongly about.

Reply

Plantinga’s reply had two parts. First, rejecting classical foundationalism does not mean having no standards at all: “criteria for proper basicality must be reached from below rather than above.” Second, there is a relevant difference between belief in God and belief in the Great Pumpkin: the Reformed epistemologist can hold with Calvin that God has implanted in human beings a natural tendency to see his hand in the world, whereas, in Plantinga’s words, there is “no Great Pumpkin and no natural tendency to accept beliefs about the Great Pumpkin.” Notice, though, that the second reply depends on the truth of theism. Whether there really is a natural tendency to believe in God, aimed at truth, is part of what is in dispute.

Objection — It only shows that if Christianity is true, it’s warranted

The A/C model shows, at most, that if Christianity is true, Christian belief is probably warranted. But the naturalist can produce the mirror image: if naturalism is true, religious belief is probably produced by mechanisms not aimed at truth, and so probably lacks warrant. Everything turns on which worldview is true, and on that question the A/C model is silent.

Reply

The description should be granted in full, because it is what Plantinga set out to do; the inference that the project is therefore empty should not be. He was explicit that Warranted Christian Belief does not argue that Christian belief is true. But the conditional is not trivial: it answers a whole family of objections by arguing that each depends, in the end, on the claim that God does not exist. After Plantinga, a critic who wants to say that belief in God lacks warrant must, in most cases, argue that it is false. It also means that Reformed epistemology must not be overstated by its friends: it does not show that God exists, only that the question that really matters is the question of truth.

The room: Plantinga and Nicholas Wolterstorff edited Faith and Rationality (1983); the label “Reformed epistemology” stuck because its two leading founders came from the Reformed (Calvinist) tradition, though its core claim does not require taking sides in the debates between Reformed, Catholic, and Orthodox Christians. The First Vatican Council taught in 1870 that God “can be known with certainty from the consideration of created things, by the natural power of human reason”; Thomas Aquinas held that God’s existence can be demonstrated; in the East, John of Damascus taught that “the knowledge of God’s existence has been implanted by Him in all by nature,” and two chapters later he set out arguments for God’s existence from change and from the order of the world. Some Reformed theologians, notably Karl Barth, have been sharply critical of natural theology. Many on both sides now treat properly basic belief and natural theology as complementary ways of describing how God makes himself known; where real differences remain, the essay does not adjudicate them. Critics such as Fales, Quinn, and Hick represent serious, sustained dissent.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Six objections, stated at full strength, not knocked down as straw. The most discussed challenge is flagged below.

The most discussed challenge (in our reading of the literature): Michael Martin argued that any community could begin “from below” with its own examples; Keith DeRose sharpened it: a model on which a belief would be warranted if it were true is available to the adherents of almost any belief system. The essay grants that the defense cannot tell an outsider which worldview is true. The deep dive puts it at full strength and answers it honestly — take it with you, not just the reply below.

“Why not the Great Pumpkin?” Trickier

Strongest form

If belief in God can be properly basic, without argument, why not any belief at all? Why couldn’t Linus, in the Peanuts comic strip, hold as properly basic his belief that the Great Pumpkin returns every Halloween? If “it’s basic for me” is a sufficient answer, then anything goes.

Reply

Plantinga’s reply had two parts. First, rejecting classical foundationalism does not mean having no standards at all. We do not need an all-purpose criterion of proper basicality, worked out in advance, to know that some beliefs are properly basic and others are not. Instead, “criteria for proper basicality must be reached from below rather than above”: we begin from clear examples (the remembered morning coffee, the perceived tree, the friend’s evident sadness) and test proposed criteria against them. Second, there is a relevant difference between belief in God and belief in the Great Pumpkin. The Reformed epistemologist can hold with Calvin that God has implanted in human beings a natural tendency to see his hand in the world, whereas, in Plantinga’s words, there is “no Great Pumpkin and no natural tendency to accept beliefs about the Great Pumpkin.” Notice, though, that the second reply depends on the truth of theism. Whether there really is a natural tendency to believe in God, aimed at truth, is part of what is in dispute.

“The Son of Great Pumpkin” (Martin, DeRose) Hard

Strongest form

The objection in its most serious form was developed by the philosopher Michael Martin and sharpened by Keith DeRose of Yale. Martin argued that Plantinga’s method could be used by any community. Voodoo practitioners, for example, could begin “from below” with their own examples of properly basic belief, build criteria around them, and declare their beliefs rational in exactly the way Plantinga declares his. DeRose’s version is sharper still: the real worry is that Plantinga’s central strategy, giving a model on which a belief would be warranted if it were true, is available to the adherents of almost any belief system. If nearly any worldview can produce such a model for itself, then producing one does nothing to distinguish Christianity from its rivals, even the most implausible of them.

Reply

Plantinga’s reply was, in substance, that the existence of a parallel model for some other belief does not show that this other belief is warranted. It shows only that, for that belief too, the question of warrant cannot be settled apart from the question of truth, which is exactly his thesis. And not every belief system can produce such a model. Some worldviews, if true, would give their adherents reason to distrust their own cognitive faculties; that is the point of his evolutionary argument against naturalism, a contested argument which our essay on the argument from reason treats in detail. Let the critic have the last objection plainly: even granting all that, the de jure defense does not tell an outsider which of the many belief systems with such models is true. Our reply is that this is correct, and it should be said without embarrassment. The defense is a shield, not a sword. It answers the charge that belief in God is irrational regardless of whether God exists; it was never meant to show that Christianity rather than its rivals is true. The critic has not found a hidden flaw; he has located the defense’s stated boundary. Beyond it the conversation moves to the de facto question.

“Religious diversity is a defeater” Hard

Strongest form

Suppose the believer grants that her belief in God was properly basic when she formed it. She then learns that millions of thoughtful, sincere people in other traditions hold, with equal conviction and by what seem to them equally direct means, beliefs incompatible with hers. Can she, aware of all this, go on believing without arbitrariness? John Hick argued that the facts of diversity are best explained by taking the great traditions as culturally conditioned and, in his view, comparably authentic responses to a single ultimate Reality. Philip Quinn argued, more modestly, that reflection on diversity should lead believers toward “thinner” theologies, holding fewer distinctive claims, and with less confidence.

Reply

Plantinga’s central points were these. Holding one’s own religious beliefs to be true, and incompatible beliefs false, is not in itself arrogant or irrational; it is simply what believing something involves. Some form of exclusivism is also inescapable: the pluralist believes that his own view is correct and that the many believers who reject it are mistaken. If disagreement among sincere, intelligent people automatically defeated belief, it would defeat the pluralist’s position too. We think that reply holds as far as it goes (pluralists reply that their view is a second-order hypothesis, not a rival creed; the exchange continues), and we add an honest concession. Diversity does reduce what basic religious belief can do as public evidence. The believer’s own warrant may be untouched, but her neighbor in another tradition can say the same about his, and neither can hand over the experience for inspection. The traditions offer a shared report of encountering something, not a shared object, and they do not show that the traditions met the same God under different names. Diversity is therefore a reason to go beyond basic belief to the public evidence, and in particular to the claim that God has made himself known in a particular, historical person (John 1:18).

“The problem of evil is a defeater” Hard

Strongest form

The reality of suffering, much of it horrifying and apparently pointless, is the hardest thing a believer faces, and no one should pretend to a tidy explanation of it. William Rowe gave the objection its most influential modern form: there appear to be instances of intense suffering that an all-powerful, all-knowing, wholly good God could have prevented without losing any greater good, so it is reasonable to believe there are such instances, and therefore reasonable to believe that no such God exists. Applied here, the objection says: even if belief in God was properly basic for the believer, once she becomes aware of the evidential argument from evil, it serves as a defeater.

Reply

Plantinga’s reply is that whether a belief is defeated depends on how strong its grounds are compared with the force of the objection. A belief with a high degree of warrant can withstand an argument whose premises are less certain than the belief itself. And the crucial premise in Rowe’s argument, the step from we cannot see a reason that would justify God in permitting this to there probably is no such reason, is precisely the one many philosophers dispute. This is a contested reply, not a knock-down one, and our essay on the problem of evil treats the debate at length. Something else should be said, which philosophy alone does not say. The Christian believes that the eternal Son of God took our human nature, suffered, and was crucified, and that in raising him from the dead God showed that suffering will not have the last word. That does not dissolve the mystery of why particular evils are permitted. It does mean that her grounds for trusting God include a story in which God, in the person of the Son made man, entered our suffering rather than standing apart from it.

“This is just fideism — a conversation-stopper” Trickier

Strongest form

Reformed epistemology, critics say, is fideism in academic dress: a way of saying “I just believe” and refusing to engage. If belief in God is properly basic, the believer can simply announce that she needs no reasons and end the discussion. That looks like a refusal of the very intellectual accountability Clifford was trying to defend.

Reply

The charge has a point about a possible misuse, but it misdescribes the view. Plantinga takes fideism to be “exclusive or basic reliance upon faith alone, accompanied by a consequent disparagement of reason.” Reformed epistemology does not disparage reason. It holds that belief in God, on the A/C model, is produced by a cognitive faculty working as it should, in the same broad category as memory and perception, which are paradigm sources of rational belief. It holds that basic beliefs are defeasible and must answer serious objections. The misuse, however, is real, and the believer should own it. “It’s properly basic for me” can be used to close a conversation that ought to stay open, which is a failure of charity and of witness. Scripture asks believers to “be ready always to give an answer to every man that asketh you a reason of the hope that is in you, with meekness and fear” (1 Peter 3:15). Reformed epistemology and 1 Peter 3:15 fit together. The first says that the believer’s faith does not wait on arguments in order to be rational. The second says that when someone asks, she should be ready to offer reasons anyway, as a service to the one who asks.

“It only shows that if Christianity is true, it’s warranted” Hard

Strongest form

The A/C model shows, at most, that if Christianity is true, Christian belief is probably warranted. But the naturalist can produce the mirror image: if naturalism is true, religious belief is probably produced by mechanisms not aimed at truth, and so probably lacks warrant. Everything turns on which worldview is true, and on that question the A/C model is silent. Evan Fales, in a detailed critical study of Warranted Christian Belief, frames the central question as whether Christian beliefs are produced by defective belief-forming mechanisms, which is to say that the de facto question has returned and is still open.

Reply

The description should be granted in full, because it is what Plantinga set out to do; the inference that the project is therefore empty should not be. He was explicit that Warranted Christian Belief does not argue that Christian belief is true; its target is the claim that Christian belief is unacceptable whether or not it is true. But the conditional is not trivial. It answers a whole family of objections (the familiar complaint that belief in God is irrational, childish, or intellectually irresponsible, regardless of whether God exists) by arguing that each depends, in the end, on the claim that God does not exist. After Plantinga, a critic who wants to say that belief in God lacks warrant must, in most cases, argue that it is false. It also means that Reformed epistemology must not be overstated by its friends: it does not show that God exists, only that the question that really matters is the question of truth.

The honest state of the scholarship

Where the argument really stands

The critique of classical foundationalism has been very widely accepted; in our judgment few epistemologists today defend the strict version Plantinga attacked. Reformed epistemology has become a mainstream position in the philosophy of religion, with its own literature of refinements and objections. But the debate has not been won. The Great Pumpkin family of objections, in Martin’s and DeRose’s forms, remains, in our reading of the literature, the most discussed challenge, and critics such as Fales, Quinn, and Hick represent serious, sustained dissent. Internalist epistemologists, including evidentialists such as Conee and Feldman, reject the externalist account of warrant on which the later version of the view depends.

The contested hinges: whether there is a sensus divinitatis philosophy cannot settle; on Plantinga’s own account it stands or falls with theism. Whether religious diversity reduces the warrant of basic belief, and by how much, is disputed, as is whether warrant should be understood externally or internally. What is far less contested is the narrower point the movement was built to make: that the demand that every rational belief in God be based on argument rested on a theory of rationality too strict to survive (it would condemn memory, perception, and belief in other minds) and, many argue, unable to meet its own standard.

The bounded verdict, stated plainly: Reformed epistemology does establish that belief in God can be rational, and on Plantinga’s account can be warranted and even knowledge, without being based on argument. It answers the classical form of the evidentialist objection by exposing the classical foundationalism behind it as too strict and, on Plantinga’s argument, self-defeating; broader evidentialisms survive, but no longer demand an argument. It does not prove that God exists. It does not tell an inquirer which God is real, or whether the Christian account is true rather than a rival one. Even granted in full, its general argument reaches only the rationality of belief in a personal Creator, not yet the triune God of the creeds. That is why it is best understood as one strand in a cumulative case. For the specifically Christian claim, the decisive question is historical: did Jesus of Nazareth rise from the dead? It is set out in our essay on the minimal facts argument for the resurrection.

    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    On Plantinga’s account the strength of that warrant is not hers to declare; it depends on whether her faculties are in fact working as they should, and a believer who is wrong about that has no protection. Defeasibility is real, not nominal. Critics reply that this safeguard operates out of the believer’s sight, which is part of why the internalist objection remains live. Internalist epistemologists, including evidentialists such as Conee and Feldman, reject the externalist account of warrant on which the later version of the view depends; whether warrant should be understood externally or internally is disputed. The believer is not required to prove God before being entitled to believe, but she is required to take real objections seriously when they come.
