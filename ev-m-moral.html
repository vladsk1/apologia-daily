The Moral Argument

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and stay clear and kind when it gets personal.

The argument in brief

The moral argument for God runs in two steps: if God does not exist, then objective moral values and duties do not exist; but objective moral values and duties do exist; therefore God exists. The second premise is, if anything, common ground between the theist and the secular moral realist: philosophers such as Erik Wielenberg agree wholeheartedly that some acts are objectively evil. The interesting fight is over the first: granting that morality is objective, what grounds it?

An important clarification, because this argument is easy to caricature: it is not the claim that atheists cannot be moral. Plenty of atheists live admirably and know right from wrong. The claim is narrower — it asks where genuinely binding moral obligations come from. If morality is only an evolved feeling or a social agreement, then it describes what we happen to prefer, not what we truly ought to do; it would have no authority over someone who simply disagreed. Real duties seem to require a foundation beyond human opinion — a transcendent moral lawgiver.

In fairness, atheist philosophers have offered grounds for objective morality without God, and that debate is live. But the believer can point out that our deepest conviction — that torturing the innocent for fun is really wrong, everywhere, for everyone — fits more naturally in a universe grounded in a good God than in one of blind matter. Note the scope: this argument reaches a moral Lawgiver who grounds our duties — not yet the Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Identifying that being with the God revealed in Christ rests on revelation and on the historical case for the resurrection.

Before you start · key terms

Eight terms this argument turns on

Learn these first — above all the ontology/epistemology distinction, which is where the argument is most often caricatured.

ObjectiveIndependent of human opinion. Not that moral views vary across cultures (they do) — that beneath the variation some things are really, mind-independently wrong.
Values vs. dutiesCraig’s distinction: values concern what is good or bad; duties concern what is right or wrong — what we are obligated to do. The argument is about both, but the sharp end is duties.
Ontology vs. epistemologyOntology: what makes moral facts true at all. Epistemology: who can know and follow the law — anyone, atheist included. The argument is about ontology; confusing the two is the classic caricature.
Properly basicA belief accepted directly, as a deliverance of experience, not from a prior argument (Plantinga). That torturing a child for fun is wrong is held this way — and rightly.
Euthyphro dilemmaPlato’s trap: is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good? The ancient objection the argument must answer.
Modified divine command theoryRobert Adams’s view and the project’s scholarly anchor: moral wrongness is being contrary to the commands of a loving God; values are grounded in God’s own nature as the Good.
Secular moral realismThe view (Wielenberg, Shafer-Landau, Enoch) that objective moral facts exist with no God. It grants premise 2 and denies premise 1 — the live opposition.
Transcendent moral groundThe most the argument reaches: a personal source whose nature is the Good — a moral lawgiver, not yet the triune God of Christian worship.
The argument, formally

Three lines — and the whole case turns on the first premise.

P1

If God does not exist, objective moral values and duties do not exist.

P2

Objective moral values and duties do exist.

∴ C

Therefore, God exists.

Valid as it stands: accept both premises and the conclusion follows. P2 is, if anything, common ground between the theist and the secular moral realist; the interesting fight is over P1 — granting that morality is objective, what grounds it? Know which move your interlocutor is making.

From the moral law to a Lawgiver

Don’t read the conclusion — reason to it

Start from the intuition everyone shares, then follow it down — and note exactly how far it reaches, and how far it does not.

You say the Holocaust was really, objectively wrong — not merely out of fashion. Is that a claim about human opinion, or about reality?

Honestly stated: if it succeeds the argument reaches a transcendent ground of objective morality — a lawgiver whose nature is the Good. That is a great deal, but not yet the full God of Christian worship. The inference to personhood is a further step (though not arbitrary: duties are owed to someone, and the Good is best understood as a person’s nature). Identifying this lawgiver with the triune God revealed in Jesus Christ requires the rest of the case — historical and revelatory. It is one strand in a cumulative case, held with confidence rather than certainty. And a standing caution: the argument never claims atheists cannot be moral — it asks what makes morality real, for believer and unbeliever alike.

Defending Premise 1

If God does not exist, objective moral values and duties do not exist.

Precision check: the claim is not that atheists cannot be good, cannot know right from wrong, or lack moral sincerity. They plainly can and do — often putting believers to shame. The claim is about ontology: about what, at the deepest level, makes moral facts true. An atheist can read the moral law as fluently as anyone; the question is who, if anyone, wrote it. The claim is that objective moral duties — real obligations binding independently of human opinion — need a foundation beyond human preference; without God there is only opinion.

The ontological claim The contested premise

If humans are accidental byproducts of a mindless process, our moral sense is a survival mechanism, not a perception of real obligations. A universe of pure matter contains no oughts woven into it — only what is. The claim is about ontology: what, at the deepest level, makes moral facts true — and blind matter offers no answer.

Honest narrowing: read it correctly — the claim is about ontology (what makes moral facts true), never that atheists cannot be good or know right from wrong (they plainly can, often putting believers to shame). And it is genuinely contested: secular moral realists argue objective morality needs no God at all (see Wielenberg below).

The theistic ground: values in God’s nature, duties in his commands Strong reply · not cost-free

A leading theistic answer comes in two layers. Values are grounded in God’s own nature: God is not a being who happens to be good but Goodness itself — the standard, “the Good” in person, after which all finite goods are patterned. Duties arise from the commands that flow from that nature. This is the heart of Adams’s modified divine command theory: moral wrongness consists in being contrary to the commands of a loving God — not the commands of just any conceivable deity. In Finite and Infinite Goods he developed the value side: finite things are good insofar as they resemble the infinite Good, which is God. On this picture, the moral order is neither an accident nor an invention but a reflection of the character of the One who is.

Honest limit: this is a strong reply, and it is the standard move among theists. But it is not cost-free, and honesty requires saying so (see the Euthyphro objection below).

The is/ought firewall Signpost · thin conclusion

Does the natural world, on its own, give us any reason to protect the weak at all? That natural selection describes what tends to happen, the strong outlasting the weak, is a fact about biology. David Hume named the gap: no catalogue of what is the case, however complete, entails by itself a single claim about what we ought to do. Nature can tell us what survives; it is silent on what deserves to. To read any command out of the bare process is to appeal to a standard the process itself does not contain.

Nearly everyone, believer and atheist alike, holds with deep conviction that the weak do have a claim on the strong — that the child, the disabled, and the dying are owed care precisely when they can offer nothing in return. Yet that conviction cannot be read off nature, because nature, left to itself, points the other way. It requires a standard above the natural order — part of what premise 1 asserts: real duties, binding on us regardless of what survival would counsel, need a ground beyond the world of natural fact.

Honest limit: here the argument leans less on a contested intuition than on the firewall between fact and value that Hume made explicit. The secular realist grants this much — duties cannot be read off nature; whether they can then stand as brute, impersonal facts rather than rest on a Person is the question the Wielenberg objection below takes up. What it points to is still thin — a moral order that transcends nature, not yet the God of the creeds — but it is a real signpost, and it points up.

Objection — The Euthyphro dilemma

Plato’s ancient trap, translated to monotheism: Is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good? Grab the first — good because commanded — and morality looks arbitrary: had God commanded gratuitous cruelty, cruelty would be good, which seems monstrous. Grab the second — commanded because good — and goodness is a standard above God, to which even He answers; God becomes a moral middleman, and the atheist’s question returns: why not skip God and go straight to the standard?

Reply

The standard theistic reply, sharpened by Adams and others, is that the dilemma is false because it omits a third option. God neither invents the good by fiat nor consults a standard outside Himself. God’s own necessary nature is the Good. His commands are not arbitrary, because they necessarily express His character — He cannot command gratuitous cruelty, not because a higher law forbids it, but because such a command would contradict what He essentially is. And the good is not independent of God, because it just is God’s nature. The dilemma assumes the standard must be either God’s will or something external; the theist locates it in God’s being.

The reply is not cost-free. Critics such as Wes Morriston press that “God’s nature is the Good” may quietly relocate rather than dissolve the dilemma: why is God’s nature good? If the answer is “because being loving, just, and so on is good,” then goodness seems definable apart from God after all; if the answer is “because it is God’s nature,” the charge of arbitrariness creeps back in. The theist can respond that “good” and “God’s nature” are necessarily, not contingently, identical — like “water” and “H₂O” — so the question “why is God’s nature good?” is as confused as “why is water H₂O?” The exchange continues; it is live, not settled — but the original dilemma, as a forced choice between an arbitrary God and a standard above Him, does not hold: the good is neither invented by God nor above Him.

Objection — Wielenberg: godless normative realism Hardest

Erik Wielenberg (Robust Ethics, 2014) argues that objective moral truths exist as brute, necessary, non-natural facts — they require no foundation outside themselves, God included. That “torturing the innocent for fun is wrong” is simply a necessary truth, true in every possible world, full stop. Steelmanned: the theist says, “moral values are grounded in God’s nature, and that’s where explanation stops.” Wielenberg says, “moral values are grounded in necessary moral facts, and that’s where explanation stops.” Both bottom out in something brute and unexplained. If the theist may help himself to God as a necessary stopping point, why may the atheist not help himself to moral facts as necessary stopping points? Indeed, Wielenberg argues, his view is more economical — one fewer entity.

Reply

On a bare count of entities Wielenberg’s ledger is shorter — a point apologists too rarely concede — but that is the wrong ledger: a single personal ground (God) that unifies values, duties, moral knowledge, and the obligatory force of morality may explain more than a sprawling plurality of free-floating ethical facts — and explanatory power, not bare entity-counting, is the right measure. More pointedly, it is hard to see how an impersonal, causally inert fact could generate a genuine obligation or accountability. Duties seem to be owed to someone; commands require a commander. Wielenberg replies that obligations can be grounded in the value of persons without a commander. What the theist claims is not that Wielenberg’s view is incoherent but that it is less explanatorily satisfying: it asks us to accept obligations that float, owed to no one.

The room: the modern form is associated with William Lane Craig, and its scholarly anchor is Robert Adams’s modified divine command theory; Kant is an ancestor (with a caveat — he denied God is the ground of obligation). The live opposition grants P2 and denies P1: the secular realists Wielenberg, Shafer-Landau and Enoch. Wes Morriston presses the Euthyphro reply.
Defending Premise 2

Objective moral values and duties do exist.

Precision check: Craig draws a useful distinction. Values concern what is good or bad; duties concern what is right or wrong — what we are obligated to do. And by objective he means, precisely, independent of human opinion. The claim is not that moral views vary across cultures (they do), but that beneath the variation some things are really, mind-independently wrong.

Reflective intuition Common ground, widely granted

Most people, on reflection, are far more certain that torturing a child for fun is wrong than they are of any philosophical theory that might be marshaled to deny it. This is the method of reflective intuition: just as we trust our sensory experience of the physical world absent a defeater, we are within our rights to trust our moral experience absent a defeater. We apprehend the wrongness of the Holocaust the way we apprehend the reality of the past — not by argument but by a basic, non-inferential moral perception.

Properly basic belief, and the burden on the skeptic Strong

Philosophers have a name for a belief held this way: properly basic — accepted not on the basis of some prior argument but directly, as a deliverance of experience, the way we accept the reality of other minds or of the past. This is the epistemology of Alvin Plantinga’s “reformed” tradition, and it reframes the burden of proof. As the atheist philosopher Louise Antony put it in her debate with Craig, any argument for moral skepticism will have to be built out of premises less obvious than the moral truths it is trying to deny — so the skeptic’s footing is always less secure than the conviction he means to unseat. One would have to be more certain of a clever theory than of the wrongness of torturing a child, and few honest people are.

The crooked line: evil named from the inside Illustration

C. S. Lewis, looking back on his years as an atheist, noticed that his strongest argument against God — that the universe is cruel and unjust — had quietly assumed the very thing it denied: “a man does not call a line crooked unless he has some idea of a straight line.” To condemn the world as unjust is already to measure it against a real standard of justice that does not bend to anyone’s opinion. Few have pressed that point more unforgettably than Rachael Denhollander. Facing Larry Nassar at his 2018 sentencing, she said she could name what he had done evil “because the straight line exists” — “not measured based on your perception or anyone else’s perception” — and on that same ground she both asked the court for its fullest justice and offered him forgiveness. Neither the condemnation nor the mercy would have meant anything if the wrong had not been objectively, mind-independently real.

Shared by secular moral realists Common ground

This is not a uniquely religious claim. It is shared by a large camp of secular moral realists — philosophers like Erik Wielenberg, Russ Shafer-Landau, and David Enoch — who agree wholeheartedly that some acts are objectively evil. So premise 2 is, if anything, common ground between the theist and the secular realist. The interesting fight is over premise 1: granting that morality is objective, what grounds it?

Honest narrowing: candor also requires noting that debunking arguments (Sharon Street’s) have at least dented the intuition it rests on.

Objection — Street’s Darwinian dilemma

Sharon Street argues that our moral intuitions were shaped by natural selection to promote reproductive fitness, not to track mind-independent moral truth. If so, the realist faces a dilemma: either deny that evolution shaped our values (empirically untenable) or admit it did, in which case the staggering coincidence that fitness-tracking intuitions also happen to track moral reality cries out for an explanation the realist cannot give. Our confident sense that the Holocaust was evil might be just a well-engineered survival heuristic.

Reply

First — and this is crucial — Street’s argument is not an argument for theism; she is herself an anti-realist, and her dagger is aimed at secular moral realism above all. It therefore wounds Wielenberg at least as badly as it wounds anyone, since his causally inert moral facts cannot have shaped our evolving brains. Second, the theist has a reply ready to hand: a God who designed our cognitive faculties to apprehend the moral order He authored gives a non-coincidental reason why our intuitions track truth. (Wielenberg himself answers Street with a “third-factor” account, on which our faculties both track moral facts and help explain why those facts are instantiated; so the honest claim is not that no secular reply exists, but that a designing mind explains the fit more economically.)

Two further replies are worth adding. First, the debunker’s empirical premise — that our specifically moral beliefs were fixed by natural selection — is shakier than it is usually made to sound. The biologist Jeffrey Schloss, reviewing the evolutionary literature, finds robust accounts of cooperative behaviors but no settled account of our moral beliefs, and least of all of costly altruism toward those who can never repay us. Second, pressed to its limit the debunking strategy consumes itself: if natural selection tuned our faculties for survival rather than truth, that verdict engulfs every belief we hold — including belief in evolutionary naturalism itself. This is the nerve of Plantinga’s evolutionary argument against naturalism — a reply his critics vigorously contest, but one that turns the debunker’s own premise back on him.

Honest limit: candor demands the concession that the theist who leans on this reply has not proved moral realism; he has shown that if realism is true, theism explains our access to it better than naturalism does. The realism itself still rests on the intuition of premise 2 — an intuition the debunker has at least dented.

The room: Louise Antony, an atheist philosopher, pressed the burden-of-skepticism point against Craig. The secular realists Wielenberg, Shafer-Landau and Enoch grant P2 wholeheartedly, which is why it is common ground. Sharon Street, an anti-realist, presses the debunking worry, which wounds secular realism at least as badly as the theist.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

This is where a defender is made. Each of these in its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged below.

The one that matters most: Wielenberg’s — necessary moral facts need no God, and both accounts bottom out somewhere. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"The Euthyphro dilemma" Hard

Strongest form

Is something good because God commands it, or does God command it because it is good? Either morality is arbitrary (God could command cruelty) or there is a standard above God — making God unnecessary.

Reply

A false dilemma. Goodness is grounded in God’s own nature — love, justice, mercy — not in arbitrary commands nor in a standard outside him. God commands in accordance with what he is. So morality is neither arbitrary nor independent of God. The reply is not cost-free: critics such as Wes Morriston press why God’s nature counts as good, and that exchange is live — but the original dilemma, as a forced choice between an arbitrary God and a standard above him, does not hold.

"Evolution explains morality" Common

Strongest form

Cooperation and altruism helped our ancestors survive, so morality is a biological adaptation — fully explained without God.

Reply

Evolution might explain why we have moral beliefs, but not whether any are true. If our sense of ought is just a survival trick, it carries no real authority — which debunks morality rather than grounding it. Sharon Street’s “Darwinian dilemma” presses exactly this, but it is not an argument for theism: it cuts at least as hard against secular realism. (Wielenberg answers with a “third-factor” account, on which our faculties both track moral facts and help explain why those facts are instantiated; the honest claim is not that no secular reply exists, but that a designing mind explains the fit between our faculties and moral truth more economically.)

"Atheists are moral too" Common

Strongest form

Plenty of atheists live good lives. Clearly you do not need God to be moral.

Reply

Completely agreed — and the argument never says otherwise. That is a question of epistemology (who can know and follow the moral law — anyone). The argument is about ontology (what makes it a real law at all). Atheists keep the moral law as well as anyone; the question is what grounds it.

"Morality is just cultural" Trickier

Strongest form

Different societies hold different values, so morality is relative to culture, not objective.

Reply

Disagreement does not disprove truth — cultures disagree about science too, yet facts remain. Much variation is over facts (who counts as a person), not core values; and we rightly judge slavery and genocide wrong across all cultures, which assumes a standard above culture.

"Objective morality can be grounded without God" Hard

Strongest form

Erik Wielenberg (Robust Ethics, 2014): moral truths are brute, necessary, non-natural facts needing no foundation, God included. Both sides bottom out in something necessary — and his ledger has one fewer entity.

Reply

On a bare count of entities his ledger is shorter — a point apologists too rarely concede — but that is the wrong ledger: explanatory power, not entity-counting, is the right measure, and one personal ground unifies values, duties, moral knowledge and the obligatory force of morality. More pointedly, it is hard to see how an impersonal, causally inert fact could generate a genuine obligation: duties seem owed to someone; a command needs a commander. Wielenberg replies that obligations can be grounded in the value of persons; the theist claims not that Wielenberg’s view is incoherent but that it is less explanatorily satisfying.

"What about the evil commanded in the Bible?" Hard

Strongest form

The God said to ground morality is depicted commanding violence — which undercuts the claim that he is the standard of good.

Reply

A real challenge, and one Christians themselves debate. But note it is a different argument — it presupposes an objective standard by which to judge those acts wrong, which is exactly what the moral argument says requires God. It is a question about this God’s character, not about whether objective morality needs a transcendent ground.

The honest state of the scholarship

Where the argument really stands

Among philosophers of religion the moral argument is taken seriously; Robert Adams’s modified divine command theory is its scholarly anchor, and the live battleground is premise 1 — against secular moral realists, Erik Wielenberg above all, who hold that objective moral facts exist as brute, necessary truths needing no God. Premise 2 is, by contrast, common ground: the secular realists grant it wholeheartedly.

The real fork: whether objective morality can stand as brute, impersonal necessities owed to no one, or whether genuine obligation needs a personal ground. Honest thinkers part ways here — naming it is accuracy, not weakness.

The bounded verdict: if it succeeds, the argument reaches a transcendent ground of the Good — a moral lawgiver — not yet the personal, good, triune God of worship. The conviction that some things are really, objectively wrong fits more naturally in a universe grounded in a good God than in one of brute, impersonal fact — but it does not coerce; it invites. That is our judgment, held with confidence rather than certainty — one strand in a cumulative case that rests, above all, on the historical evidence for the resurrection.

    NT
    Naturalist
    "You say caring for the weak needs God to ground it. But that's backwards — compassion is just an evolved instinct: kin selection, reciprocal altruism. Nature explains why we protect the vulnerable perfectly well. No lawgiver required."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    Notice the quiet jump from is to ought. Grant that evolution gave us compassionate feelings — that is a fact about biology. It still does not tell us we ought to act on them, or that the weak are genuinely owed care. In fact nature, left to itself, points the other way: the strong outlast the weak. The most honest naturalist here was Nietzsche, who saw clearly that protecting the weak is “anti-nature” — and drew the chilling inference that the moral argument exists to reject. His error was not the description but the silent leap from what is to what we ought to do. David Hume named the gap: no catalogue of what is the case, however complete, entails by itself a single claim about what we ought to do. So the conviction that the weak really do have a claim on the strong — that the child and the dying are owed care precisely when they can offer nothing in return — cannot be read off nature, because nature points the other way. It needs a standard above the natural order. That is a real signpost — though it points only to a moral order that transcends nature, not yet to the God of the creeds.
