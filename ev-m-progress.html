Where Did the Idea of Progress Come From?

The deep dive tells you what the argument is. This track makes you able to use it — reconstruct it from memory, answer its hardest objections, and hold the line in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

“The arc of the moral universe is long, but it bends toward justice.” Martin Luther King Jr. reached for that line again and again, and it has since been woven into an Oval Office rug and quoted by presidents. Most people who hear it nod, because it says out loud something they already feel in their bones: that history is going somewhere, that the future can be better than the past, and that the direction is up.

Here is the surprising thing: that assumption is not a human universal. The oldest and most widespread pictures of time are cyclical (the Stoics’ endlessly repeating cosmos, the wheel of Fortune) or a downhill slope (Hesiod’s descent from a Golden to an Iron age). What gave the West a line with a destination — a judged road running from creation and exodus through the prophets to a promised kingdom — was the biblical narrative. Even confident secularists like Steven Pinker reach for its shape: swords beaten into plowshares, a “mysterious arc,” the principle that “life is sacred.”

But the scope must be kept honest, and this track keeps it honest throughout. Showing that the idea of progress has biblical roots is a claim about where it came from, not proof that the faith is true — a tradition can seed a good idea and still be wrong about God, and Christians have often stood on the wrong side of the very reforms this hope demands. The genealogy shows the confidence that history bends toward justice is an inheritance still leaning on the fixed point it was given. The case for the faith’s truth rests elsewhere, above all on the resurrection.

Understand it

Not more prose — the load-bearing structure. See the skeleton, then learn to defend each bone.

The argument, formally

Three lines. The whole weight rests on the two premises.

P1

The belief that history has a direction — that it bends toward justice and the future can be deliberately made better than the past — is not a human universal. The dominant ancient pictures of time were the wheel and the downhill slope; the biblical narrative gave the West its distinctive picture of time as a judged line heading toward a goal.

P2

That confidence still depends on a fixed point above history to say which direction counts as forward — a record of where we have gone cannot supply it — and even committed secularists reach for the biblical arrow’s shape while its source is filed off.

∴ C

Therefore the modern idea of progress is a biblical inheritance still leaning on the fixed point it was given — which shows where the hope came from, not (by itself) that Christianity is true.

This is a genealogy, and its conclusion is deliberately bounded: it traces where a load-bearing modern conviction came from, and it stops there. It is not an argument that Christianity is true — roots are not proof.

Then the honest step — what the genealogy does and does not show

Tracing an idea to its source shows where it came from; it never shows the source was right about God. Keep the two apart.

Defending the premises

Why each premise is more plausible than its denial

Support for P1 — the upward arrow is not common sense, it is inherited

The ancient pictures: the wheel and the slope

Hesiod (c. 700 BC) gave the classic downhill slope: humanity descends from a Golden age through Silver and Bronze to his own Iron race, doomed to labour and destined to grow worse. The Stoics taught the cosmos passes through vast cycles that repeat exactly — the same Socrates married to the same wife, endlessly — and the wheel of Fortune simply turned. On these pictures nothing is finally gained, because there is no whole that is moving anywhere.

The Bible gave history a judged line

The Hebrew Scriptures supplied what the ancient world lacked: a story that runs in a straight line and is going somewhere — creation, a call to Abraham, rescue from Egypt, prophets, and a promised kingdom in which the wrongs of history are set right. Crucially the road is judged against a standard ahead of it, not behind: Amos’s “let justice roll down like waters,” Isaiah’s swords beaten into plowshares, the Jubilee’s release of debts and slaves. King’s cadences are Amos and Isaiah in a Sunday suit.

Support for P2 — the hope still needs a fixed point above history

The secular borrowing, in plain sight

Steven Pinker, writing as a thoroughgoing atheist, reaches for the image of swords into plowshares to celebrate disarmament, says declining executions make it “seem as if there really is a mysterious arc bending toward justice,” and states his governing principle as bluntly as any liturgy: “life is sacred.” A committed secularist straining to describe progress cannot help but talk like a prophet. There is nothing dishonest in this — it is what happens when you keep the fruit and let go of the root.

An arc has to bend toward something

Filing off the source leaves a real question exposed. Parker could name what the arc bends toward — a justice fixed by conscience, which for him meant fixed by God. Pinker offers instead the long tables of declining violence and rising health. Those tables are real and this argument disputes not a line of them. But a measurement of where we have gone cannot, by itself, tell us which direction counts as forward; that needs a standard above history. (Whether a secular account can supply one is the moral argument, made elsewhere; the point here is only historical.)

The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

This is what separates someone who knows where the idea of progress came from from someone who can defend the claim. Each gets its best form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: the sharpest sceptic will not deny the genealogy but say it proves nothing about God — a tradition can seed a good idea and still be mistaken in its central claims. That is correct, and this track concedes it in our own voice. The argument’s job is narrower: to show that the confidence history bends toward justice is an inheritance, not a self-evident fact.

“The Greeks and Romans believed in progress too” Trickier

Strongest form

Robert Nisbet, in his History of the Idea of Progress, argued that antiquity already had real ideas of progress; the Greeks and Romans celebrated advances in the arts and sciences and noted that humanity had climbed from rude beginnings. So the idea is not distinctively biblical at all.

Reply

Concede it — Nisbet is right about that, and this track says so: the ancients did have technical and cultural notions of progress. But that is not the idea being traced. What the dominant ancient pictures lacked is the further conviction: that history as a whole is a story with a plot, morally judged, headed toward a goal it has not yet reached. That load-bearing picture — a line with a destination, against the images of the cycle and the decline — came into the West through the biblical narrative. Nisbet himself gives that biblical sense of purposive history a central place.

“This is just Löwith’s secularization thesis, and it’s been refuted” Hardest

Strongest form

Karl Löwith claimed the modern idea of progress is a secularized biblical eschatology — Judeo-Christian hope in worldly dress. But Hans Blumenberg replied at length that progress grows from within history, out of the open-ended experience of science and modern self-assertion, not from any borrowed eschatology. The whole thesis is contested.

Reply

Concede the strong thesis is contested, and do not lean on it: Blumenberg’s rebuttal is serious and this track does not endorse Löwith’s stronger claim. But the point here is narrower, and it survives Blumenberg. What Löwith and Nisbet actually share — and what is not seriously disputed — is that the biblical narrative made a distinctive, load-bearing contribution: time as a line with a moral destination, which the cyclical and declining images did not supply. That narrower, historical claim is enough for everything this argument needs.

“A secular humanist can just believe in progress on their own terms” Common

Strongest form

You do not need the Bible for this. Pinker’s long-run data on declining violence and rising health gives a more objective measure of progress than any transcendent standard — the improvement is empirical, and you can read it off the tables.

Reply

They can, and many do — Pinker’s data is real and worth taking seriously; this track disputes not a line of it. The tension is narrower. An arc has to bend toward something, and a record of where we have gone cannot by itself tell us which direction counts as “forward.” For that you need a fixed point above history to measure against — which is exactly what the idea was born believing in. The observation is not that secular progress is impossible, only that it is living on inherited capital, and owes an account of the fixed point it still assumes.

“Christians were often on the wrong side of the reforms you claim” Trickier

Strongest form

If this arc is really a Christian inheritance, why were Christians so often the ones defending what later Christians would fight to abolish? You cannot claim the reform and disown the record.

Reply

Granted, without softening it: Christians have repeatedly stood on the wrong side of the very reforms this hope demands, reading the same Scriptures. But the point being made is genealogical, not congratulatory. The claim is not that Christians always lived up to the arc — plainly they did not — but that the belief history can bend toward justice is an inheritance rather than a self-evident fact. That the source seeded the idea is compatible with those who carried it failing it, often and badly.

Touch the originals

Where this comes from — primary sources, not our summary

Hesiodc. 700 BC · Works and Days 106–201
The classic downhill slope: humanity descends through the ages of gold, silver, bronze, and the heroes to Hesiod’s own Iron race — the arrow, if there is one, points down, and the task of the wise is memory, not reform.
Amos & IsaiahHebrew prophets · Amos 5:24; Isaiah 2:4
The judged line: “let justice roll down like waters”; nations that “beat their swords into plowshares… and learn war no more.” The prophets indict the present against a justice that lies ahead — the very cadences King preached in Washington.
Theodore Parker1853 · “Of Justice and the Conscience”
The abolitionist minister whose passage King compressed into the famous line — and who was honest that it was a claim of faith, not sight: “I cannot calculate the curve… I can divine it by conscience. But… I am sure it bends towards justice.”
Steven Pinker2018 · Enlightenment Now
The secular borrowing: a committed atheist who reaches for swords into plowshares, a “mysterious arc bending toward justice,” and “life is sacred” — the biblical arrow with the source filed off, resting instead on the long tables of declining violence.
UN General Assembly1948 · Universal Declaration of Human Rights
When the century recoiled from its worst chapters it reached backward for a footing — naming “barbarous acts which have outraged the conscience of mankind.” It states, magnificently, what we must never do again; it says far less, from its own resources, about why.
