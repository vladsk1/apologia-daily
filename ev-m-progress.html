Where Did the Idea of Progress Come From?

The deep dive tells you what the argument is. This track makes you able to use it — reconstruct it from memory, answer its hardest objections, and hold the line in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

“The arc of the moral universe is long, but it bends toward justice.” Martin Luther King Jr. reached for that line again and again, and it has since been woven into an Oval Office rug and quoted by presidents. Most people who hear it nod, because it says out loud something they already feel in their bones: that history is going somewhere, that the future can be better than the past, and that the direction is up.

Here is the surprising thing: that assumption is not a human universal. The oldest and most widespread pictures of time are cyclical (the Stoics’ endlessly repeating cosmos, the wheel of Fortune) or a downhill slope (Hesiod’s descent from a Golden to an Iron age). What gave the West a line with a destination — a judged road running from creation and exodus through the prophets to a promised kingdom — was the biblical narrative. Even confident secularists like Steven Pinker reach for its shape: swords beaten into plowshares, a “mysterious arc,” the principle that “life is sacred.”

But the scope must be kept honest, and this track keeps it honest throughout. Showing that the idea of progress has biblical roots is a claim about where it came from, not proof that the faith is true — a tradition can seed a good idea and still be wrong about God, and Christians have often stood on the wrong side of the very reforms this hope demands. The genealogy shows the confidence that history bends toward justice is an inheritance still leaning on the fixed point it was given. The case for the faith’s truth rests elsewhere, above all on the resurrection.

Understand it

Not more prose — the load-bearing structure. See the skeleton, then learn to defend each bone.

The argument, formally

Three lines. The whole weight rests on the two premises.

P1

The belief that history has a direction — that it bends toward justice and the future can be deliberately made better than the past — is not a human universal. The dominant ancient pictures of time were the wheel and the downhill slope; the biblical narrative gave the West its distinctive picture of time as a judged line heading toward a goal.

P2

That confidence still depends on a fixed point above history to say which direction counts as forward — a record of where we have gone cannot supply it — and even committed secularists reach for the biblical arrow’s shape while its source is filed off.

∴ C

Therefore the modern idea of progress is, in large part, a biblical inheritance still leaning on the fixed point it was given — which shows where the hope came from, not (by itself) that Christianity is true.

This is a genealogy, and its conclusion is deliberately bounded: it traces where a load-bearing modern conviction came from, and it stops there. It is not an argument that Christianity is true — roots are not proof.

Then the honest step — what the genealogy does and does not show

Tracing an idea to its source shows where it came from; it never shows the source was right about God. Keep the two apart.

Defending Premise 1

The belief that history has a direction — that it bends toward justice and the future can be deliberately made better than the past — is not a human universal. The dominant ancient pictures of time were the wheel and the downhill slope; the biblical narrative gave the West its distinctive picture of time as a judged line heading toward a goal.

Precision check: the claim is narrow. None of this is to say the ancients imagined no improvement at all: Greek and Roman writers could celebrate advances in the arts and sciences, and note that humanity had climbed from rude beginnings. What the dominant pictures lacked was the further idea being traced — that history as a whole is a story with a plot, morally judged, headed toward a goal it has not yet reached. What is not seriously disputed is that the biblical narrative gave the West a distinctive and load-bearing picture of time as a line with a destination.

The downhill slope — Hesiod Strong

The Greek poet Hesiod, writing around 700 BC, gave the classic version of the downhill slope. Humanity began in a Golden Age, when people lived like gods, free of toil and sorrow; then came a Silver race, then a Bronze, then the heroes, and finally the Iron race — his own — doomed to labour and grief, and destined to grow worse until the gods abandoned it altogether. Where the golden age lies behind you, the task of the wise is not reform but memory: to recover what has been lost, not to build what has never been.

The wheel — the Stoics and Fortune Strong

The Stoics taught that the cosmos passes through vast cycles, each ending in a universal fire and beginning again — and that when it begins again it repeats exactly: the same Socrates marries the same wife and is tried by the same accusers, endlessly, without remainder. On such a view nothing is ever finally gained or lost, because everything comes round again. The goddess Fortune turned her wheel, lifting the low and casting down the high, and the only wisdom was to expect the turn. There was no destination toward which the whole was moving, because there was no whole that moved.

The Bible gave history a judged line Strong

What the ancient world lacked, the Hebrew Scriptures supplied: a story that runs in a straight line and is going somewhere — creation, a call to Abraham, a rescue from slavery in Egypt, a journey to a promised land, prophets who summon the nation back to its covenant, and a promised kingdom in which the wrongs of history are finally set right. Crucially, that road is judged — measured against a standard that lies ahead of it rather than behind. The prophets do not tell Israel to return to a golden age; they hold up a future in which justice arrives, and indict the present for falling short of it. “But let justice roll down like waters,” thunders Amos, “and righteousness like an ever-flowing stream.” Isaiah sees a day when the nations “shall beat their swords into plowshares, and their spears into pruning hooks.” The law itself builds in the rhythm of reform: every fiftieth year is a Jubilee, when debts are released, Israelite bondservants freed, and land restored.

Honest limit: Augustine, answering philosophers who held that the ages turn in fixed cycles, set against the wheel one unrepeatable event: “For once Christ died for our sins; and, rising from the dead, He dies no more.” The point being made is genealogical; on its own it is no proof that Christianity is true.

Objection — “The Greeks and Romans believed in progress too”

Robert Nisbet, in his History of the Idea of Progress, credited the Greeks and Romans with real ideas of progress of their own; they celebrated advances in the arts and sciences and noted that humanity had climbed from rude beginnings. So the idea is not distinctively biblical at all.

Reply

Concede it: the ancients did have technical and cultural notions of progress. But that is not the idea being traced. What the dominant ancient pictures lacked is the further conviction that history as a whole is a story with a plot, morally judged, headed toward a goal it has not yet reached. That load-bearing picture — a line with a destination, against the images of the cycle and the decline — came into the West through the biblical narrative. Nisbet himself gives that biblical sense of purposive history a central role in the developed idea.

Objection — “This is just Löwith’s secularization thesis, and it’s been refuted” Hardest

Karl Löwith argued that the modern philosophy of progress is a secularized form of biblical eschatology — Judeo-Christian hope in worldly dress. Hans Blumenberg replied that the idea of progress grows from within history, out of the open-ended experience of science and modern self-assertion, not from any borrowed eschatology.

Reply

It is a contested reading, and worth naming as one; the track follows the shared claim of Löwith and Nisbet — that the biblical narrative made a distinctive contribution — without endorsing the stronger secularization thesis. What is not seriously disputed is narrower, and enough for the point here: the biblical narrative gave the West a distinctive and load-bearing picture of time as a line with a destination, which the dominant ancient images of the cycle and the decline did not supply.

The room: Karl Löwith argues the strong secularization thesis; Hans Blumenberg answers that progress grows from within history; Robert Nisbet, unlike Löwith, credits the Greeks and Romans with real ideas of progress of their own, while giving the Judeo-Christian sense of purposive history a central role. The shared, narrower claim is that the biblical narrative made a distinctive contribution.
Defending Premise 2

That confidence still depends on a fixed point above history to say which direction counts as forward — a record of where we have gone cannot supply it — and even committed secularists reach for the biblical arrow’s shape while its source is filed off.

Precision check: the point here is only historical, and narrower than the moral argument. That the moral law needs such a ground is the argument of a companion essay and is not re-run here. The claim is that the confidence that history bends toward justice was born believing in that fixed point, and has not obviously found a replacement for it — not that secular progress is impossible.

The secular borrowing — Pinker’s arc Suggestive

Steven Pinker, whose Enlightenment Now (2018) is perhaps the most confident recent defence of progress, writes as a thoroughgoing atheist and humanist. Explaining the retreat of the death penalty, he writes that it has “made it seem as if there really is a mysterious arc bending toward justice.” That is not a dead idiom; it is Theodore Parker’s image and King’s. One need not claim Pinker is quoting Scripture unawares to notice the shape of it: when a secular humanist reaches for the most resonant way to say that history improves, the cadence that comes to hand is a prophet’s.

Honest limit: some of the echoes are easy to over-read — when he celebrates disarmament as swords beaten into plowshares, or states the principle behind the century’s moral gains as “life is sacred,” he is reaching for phrases so worn into ordinary English that little should be made of them on their own. The argument rests on the single direct echo of the Parker–King “arc.” There is nothing dishonest in this, and nothing to sneer at: it is simply what happens when you keep the fruit and let go of the root.

An arc has to bend toward something Suggestive

Filing off the source leaves a real question exposed. Parker could name what the arc bends toward: a justice fixed by conscience, which for him meant fixed by God. Pinker offers instead something he presents as firmer than conscience — the long tables of declining violence and rising health. Those tables are real, and this track does not dispute a line of them. But a measurement of where we have gone cannot, by itself, tell us which direction counts as forward. For that you need a fixed point standing above history to be measured against — a standard the story cannot generate purely from within itself.

Honest limit: Parker was a radical Unitarian who rejected much of historic Christian doctrine — a reminder that the argument here needs no one to have been orthodox. Whether a secular account can supply the standard from within is the question of a companion essay, not this track.

Progress without a pole star — the twentieth century Suggestive

The word “progress” names a movement but not a destination; it says forward without saying toward what — and a century that believed fervently in progress discovered that “forward” can be made to point almost anywhere. Cut loose from any standard above history, the belief that one is riding the tide of the future can curdle into its own justification. Isaiah Berlin warned precisely against this: the notion that history has a guaranteed direction, he argued, becomes a way of excusing whatever is done in its name, dissolving the moral responsibility of the people who do it.

Where the century turned — the 1948 Declaration Suggestive

The framers of the 1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights did not deduce human dignity from progress or from nature; they asserted it in their first line as “inherent,” and in their second named the pit they meant to climb out of — “barbarous acts which have outraged the conscience of mankind.” It states, with magnificent clarity, what we must never do again; it says far less, from its own resources, about why. Jacques Maritain related a remark made at a meeting of one of UNESCO’s national commissions: champions of violently opposed ideologies had agreed on a list of rights, they said, but only on condition that “no one asks us why.”

Honest limit: the historical observation here is only that the drafters reached for the language of conscience outraged, not for a derivation from progress or from nature. Whether a secular account can supply that why from within is the question of a companion essay, not this track.

Objection — “A secular humanist can just believe in progress on their own terms”

You do not need the Bible for this. Pinker’s long-run data on declining violence and rising health gives a firmer measure of progress than any transcendent standard — the improvement is empirical, and you can read it off the tables.

Reply

They can, and many do — Pinker’s data are real and worth taking seriously. The honest tension is narrower. An arc has to bend toward something, and a record of where we have gone cannot by itself tell us which direction counts as “forward.” For that you need a fixed point above history to measure against — which is exactly what the idea was born believing in. The observation is not that secular progress is impossible, but that it is living on inherited capital, and owes an account of the fixed point it still assumes.

Objection — “The genealogy proves nothing about God”

A tradition can seed a good idea and still be mistaken in its central claims.

Reply

That is correct: that a conviction has biblical roots tells you where it came from, not whether the faith that carried it is right about God, and the case for the faith’s truth is made elsewhere in this Library, resting above all on the resurrection. The belief that history can bend toward justice is an inheritance, not a self-evident fact — and an inheritance still leaning on the fixed point it was given. That even confident atheists keep the hope after discarding the God who grounded it is a small thread worth weighing, though never a proof.

Objection — “Christians were often on the wrong side of the reforms you claim”

If this arc is really a Christian inheritance, why were Christians so often the ones defending what later Christians would fight to abolish?

Reply

Granted, without softening it: Christians have repeatedly been on the wrong side of the very reforms this hope demands — defending what later Christians, reading the same Scriptures, would fight to abolish. The point being made is genealogical, not congratulatory.

The room: Steven Pinker presents the case for progress as an empirical one, built on long-run data rather than on any transcendent standard, and writes explicitly as an atheist and humanist. Theodore Parker, a radical Unitarian, named the standard as a justice fixed by conscience, which for him meant fixed by God. Isaiah Berlin warned against a guaranteed direction to history. Maritain notes that practical agreement on rights, without agreement on their justification, was possible and worth having.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

This is what separates someone who knows where the idea of progress came from from someone who can defend the claim. Each gets its best form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged below.

The one that matters most: the sharpest sceptic will not deny the genealogy but say it proves nothing about God — a tradition can seed a good idea and still be mistaken in its central claims. That is correct, and this track concedes it in our own voice. The argument’s job is narrower: to show that the confidence history bends toward justice is an inheritance, not a self-evident fact.
