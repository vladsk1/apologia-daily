Why a Crucified Messiah? The Scandal of the Cross

The deep dive explains the case. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild from memory why a crucified, cursed Messiah was the one claim no one would invent, why that makes the crucifixion bedrock, and how the New Testament turns the curse into the gospel — then hold it together in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

Every movement leads with its best foot; the earliest Christians led with their worst. At the center of their message was not a teaching or a miracle but an execution — and not a noble one: a Messiah nailed to a Roman cross, the death reserved for slaves and rebels, the death a Jew's own Scriptures called a curse (Deuteronomy 21:23). "We preach Christ crucified," Paul wrote, "a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles" (1 Corinthians 1:23). It was the single fact most likely to kill the movement in its cradle — and they built their gospel on it.

That strange choice cuts two ways. First, it is evidence. The crucifixion is, by wide agreement, the most secure single fact about Jesus — resting first on multiple attestation (reported by non-Christian writers with no motive to flatter, Tacitus and Josephus) and conceded even by determined sceptics (John Dominic Crossan: "as sure as anything historical can ever be"). Converging with that plain attestation is a further consideration: inventors do not sabotage their own inventions, and no one fabricating a Messiah, or gilding a beloved teacher's memory, would choose the detail most damaging to the claim. Saul of Tarsus, who found the claim so intolerable he tried to destroy the movement, is the sharpest illustration. Second, the New Testament does not hide the scandal but centers it ("I decided to know nothing… except Jesus Christ and him crucified," 1 Corinthians 2:2) and answers it: "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us" (Galatians 3:13) — the very verse (Deuteronomy 21:23) that made a crucified Messiah a scandal becomes the key to what the cross accomplished.

Honest scope: the scandal establishes that the crucifixion is authentic bedrock (not invention) and that the earliest Christians met it with a theology of the curse borne — not, by itself, that Jesus rose (the separate resurrection case) or that the theology is true. The criterion of embarrassment is a probability, not a proof, and has its critics (Allison, Rodríguez), so the crucifixion's certainty is anchored on multiple attestation, not on it alone. And how the cross saves — the mechanism of the atonement — has been confessed in more than one orthodox way (penal substitution, Christus Victor, satisfaction, and others); this track states the shared confession ("Christ died for our sins," the curse borne, reconciliation) and crowns none of them. Nor does "became a curse for us" ever mean the divine nature died or that the Father hated the Son.

Before you start · key terms

Eight words this argument leans on

Learn these first — the objections below turn on them, and most weak versions of the argument come from blurring one of them.

The argument, formally

The crucifixion is bedrock, not invention — and the New Testament turns the curse into the gospel.

P1

A crucified Messiah was the single most offensive claim in its world: crucifixion was the most degrading Roman death (Hengel), reserved for slaves and rebels; Deuteronomy 21:23 put a hanged man under God's visible curse, so "crucified Messiah" was nearly a contradiction in terms; and to a Greek it was "folly" (1 Corinthians 1:23).

P2

The crucifixion is therefore historical bedrock — resting first on multiple attestation (Tacitus, Josephus), conceded even by sceptics; and, converging with that, no one inventing a Messiah, and no audience they hoped to win, would choose the detail most damaging to the claim — yet the earliest Christians centered it rather than hiding it ("we preach Christ crucified," 1 Corinthians 1:23; "boast… in the cross," Galatians 6:14), and Saul the persecutor, who found it a blasphemy, became its missionary.

∴ C

Therefore the crucifixion is authentic bedrock, not fabrication, and the New Testament does not evade the scandal but answers it — "Christ… becoming a curse for us" (Galatians 3:13), the curse borne in our place. (This does not by itself prove the resurrection — the separate event that turned scandal into gospel — and it crowns no single theory of how the cross saves.) And we say it with the first Christians. The crucified Jesus is the Messiah; his death was for our sins, the curse borne and God and sinners reconciled; and God raised him. The scandal does not prove that last claim, but it clears the ground for it.

This establishes that the crucifixion is bedrock and that the earliest Christians met it with a theology of the curse borne — not, by itself, that Jesus rose or that the theology is true. The criterion of embarrassment is a tool of probability, not a proof, and has its critics (Allison, Rodríguez), so the crucifixion's certainty rests on multiple attestation, not on it alone. And how the cross saves is confessed in several orthodox ways — penal substitution, Christus Victor, satisfaction, and others — which this track states as the family's internal debate, never crowning one; "became a curse for us" never means the divine nature died or that the Father hated the Son.

Why "it was just an embarrassing invention" is the popular skeptic's move.

A skeptic may say the crucifixion was an awkward fact the first Christians spun into theology after the fact, or that the "criterion of embarrassment" is an unreliable trick. The reply grants that the criterion is a probability, not a proof — and then points out that the crucifixion's certainty does not rest on it: it is stated outright by non-Christian writers with no motive to flatter the movement (Tacitus, Josephus). The scandal is a converging consideration: not that the Christians were embarrassed (they gloried in the cross), but that no one inventing a Messiah would have chosen it.

Defending Premise 1

A crucified Messiah was the single most offensive claim in its world

Precision check: the claim is narrow and firm. It is not that no strand of ancient Judaism ever contemplated a suffering righteous figure. It is that the dominant hope was for a triumphant deliverer, and a crucified Messiah — executed as a criminal, hung under the Deuteronomic curse, by Rome — was the opposite of what that hope expected and, to most first-century Jews, an offense to be resisted rather than a promise to be welcomed.

The most degrading death Rome had Strong

It is hard, at this distance, to feel the scandal. The cross is now jewelry. In the first century it was an instrument of torture so degrading that polite Romans avoided the word. The historian Martin Hengel, in the standard study of the subject, gathered the ancient sources to show that crucifixion was what Josephus called “the most wretched of deaths,” reserved for slaves, violent criminals, and rebels against Rome, deliberately public and deliberately obscene. Cicero told an assembly of the Roman people that the very word “cross” should be far removed not only from the body of a Roman citizen but from his thoughts, his eyes, his ears. To announce that one worshipped a crucified man was, to a cultured Greek or Roman, simply folly — the word Paul himself uses (1 Corinthians 1:23).

A body under God’s curse Strong

For a Jew the offense cut deeper, and touched God directly. The Law was explicit: “if a man has committed a crime punishable by death and he is put to death, and you hang him on a tree… you shall bury him the same day, for a hanged man is cursed by God” (Deuteronomy 21:22–23). A crucified body hanging on a Roman cross was, on the face of the text, a body under the visible curse of God. So “crucified Messiah” was not merely an unhappy phrase; it was very nearly a contradiction in terms. The Messiah was the LORD’s anointed, the son of David who would break the nations and reign — not a condemned criminal dying under God’s own curse at the hands of the pagan empire he was supposed to overthrow. A crucified Messiah looked less like a vindicated king than like a blasphemous joke.

Note: The Hebrew of Deuteronomy 21:22–23 is applied to crucifixion in Second Temple Judaism (cf. the Temple Scroll, 11QTemple 64:6–13) and by Paul in Galatians 3:13.

Objection — “Jews did expect a suffering Messiah”

Isaiah 53 describes a suffering servant; some ancient Jewish texts read it messianically, and later rabbinic tradition speaks of a “Messiah son of Joseph” who suffers and dies. So the premise is wrong: a suffering, even dying, Messiah was a live Jewish category, and the cross fit it rather than scandalizing it.

Reply

Keep the distinction. Ancient Judaism was not monolithic, and this track says so plainly: the Servant of Isaiah 53 was read by some in an individual, even messianic key (the Targum on Isaiah 52:13 names “my servant the Messiah,” even though the Targum itself redirects the Servant’s sufferings to Israel and the nations), and later tradition knew of a suffering “Messiah son of Joseph.” The claim is narrower and firmer: the dominant hope was for a triumphant deliverer, and a crucified Messiah — executed as a criminal, hung under the Deuteronomic curse, by Rome — was the opposite of what that hope expected and, to most first-century Jews, an offense to be resisted rather than a promise to be welcomed. The specific scandal was never “suffering” in the abstract; it was the Roman cross.

The room: Martin Hengel (the standard study of crucifixion, gathering the Greco-Roman sources), Josephus (“the most wretched of deaths”) and Cicero on the Roman revulsion; the Targum on Isaiah 52:13 and the later “Messiah son of Joseph” tradition on the other side of the ledger — this page grants both and holds the narrower claim.
Defending Premise 2

The crucifixion is therefore historical bedrock — multiple attestation, converging with the fact that no one inventing a Messiah would choose the detail most damaging to the claim

Precision check: what the scandal establishes is that the crucifixion is authentic bedrock — too damaging to have been invented, present at the movement’s foundation, believed by people who had every earthly reason to wish it away. What it does not do, by itself, is prove that Jesus rose. The offensiveness of the cross explains why no one would fabricate it; it does not explain why anyone would then celebrate it. The scandal clears the ground; it does not, alone, raise the dead.

Plain attestation The first warrant

This is why the crucifixion is, by wide agreement, the most secure single fact about Jesus of Nazareth. Its bedrock status rests first on plain attestation: it is reported not only by Christians but by non-Christian writers with no motive to flatter the movement — the Roman historian Tacitus, and (in a notice whose core is widely accepted as authentic) Josephus — and even determinedly skeptical scholars grant it, John Dominic Crossan calling it “as sure as anything historical can ever be.”

No one inventing a Messiah would choose it Converging · probability

Inventors do not sabotage their own inventions. A group fabricating a messianic hero from scratch, or gilding the memory of a beloved teacher, has every reason to leave out the detail that most discredits the claim — and crucifixion was that detail. Strictly, the point is not that the crucifixion embarrassed the Christians who preached it — they gloried in it — but that no one setting out to invent a Messiah, and no audience they hoped to win, would ever have chosen this detail; a claim so damaging to its own cause is not the kind a movement fabricates.

Honest limit: that reasoning is a historian’s tool of probability, not a proof, and it has its critics; here it does not stand alone but reinforces the attestation. It is a form of the criterion of embarrassment / dissimilarity, and has itself been criticized as unreliable when used alone (e.g. Dale C. Allison, Rafael Rodríguez), which is why the crucifixion’s certainty is anchored on the attestation, not on the criterion by itself.

They gloried in it, and the persecutor became its missionary Converging

What is most striking is not that the first Christians admitted the cross but that they gloried in it. A movement merely coping with an embarrassing fact would minimize it. Paul does the reverse. “I decided to know nothing among you except Jesus Christ and him crucified” (1 Corinthians 2:2); “far be it from me to boast except in the cross of our Lord Jesus Christ” (Galatians 6:14). The offense was not an awkwardness to be managed on the way to the message; it was the message. People selling a fabricated hero do not stake everything on his most humiliating hour.

The sharpest illustration is a single man. Before he was the apostle Paul, Saul of Tarsus found the crucified-Messiah claim so intolerable that he set out to destroy the people making it (Galatians 1:13–14). He of all people understood the Deuteronomic curse; a cursed Messiah was, to the Pharisee he was, the ultimate blasphemy. That the movement’s most capable enemy became its most relentless missionary — and never once tried to soften the scandal he had once found damning — is a fact demanding explanation, and it is the subject of our essay on Paul’s conversion.

The answer the New Testament gives Scope-limited

The first Christians did not merely concede the curse; they claimed to understand it. Paul writes: “Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us — for it is written, ‘Cursed is everyone who is hanged on a tree’” (Galatians 3:13). The move is audacious: the exact verse (Deuteronomy 21:23) that made a crucified Messiah a scandal becomes the key to what the crucifixion accomplished. The curse was real, Paul grants — and it was borne, willingly, in the place of those who deserved it. The scandal is not denied; it is absorbed and turned inside out.

Honest limit: this is not the endorsement of any one theory of how the cross saves — the church has explained the mechanism in several orthodox ways, and this page does not adjudicate between them. What is said is the shared confession the New Testament states plainly: “Christ died for our sins” (1 Corinthians 15:3), and in that death the curse was borne and God and sinners reconciled. And “becoming a curse for us” does not mean that the Father hated or was divided from the Son, nor that the divine nature could die or be cursed.

Objection — the “no one would invent it” reasoning is circular Hardest

The criterion of embarrassment has been sharply criticized by New Testament scholars themselves (Dale Allison, Rafael Rodríguez): it is easy to abuse, hard to falsify, and often assumes we know what an ancient author would or would not have found embarrassing. “They wouldn’t have invented a crucified Messiah” can look like a just-so story dressed as history.

Reply

Concede the criticism — and notice the argument does not actually rest on the criterion. The crucifixion’s bedrock status rests first on plain multiple attestation: it is reported by non-Christian writers with no motive to flatter the movement (Tacitus; Josephus, in a notice whose core is widely accepted), and granted even by determined sceptics (Crossan: “as sure as anything historical can ever be”). The scandal is a converging consideration, offered as a probability, not a proof. And it avoids the usual circularity charge: the point is not the vague “embarrassing to the tradents” (they gloried in the cross), but the sharper, more falsifiable claim that a detail this damaging to one’s own cause is not what a movement fabricates to win converts.

Objection — proclaiming the cross louder was just cognitive dissonance

This is cognitive dissonance at work: when reality contradicts a deeply held belief, people often re-rationalize the failure and proclaim it harder than before.

Reply

Dissonance theory describes a group preserving an existing belief despite disconfirmation. Dissonance reduction can explain a group doubling down on what it already hoped for. It is poorly suited to explain a group proclaiming a brand-new category — a crucified and cursed Messiah, raised bodily ahead of the last day — contrary to its entire tradition, to describe a man it had given up for lost.

The room: Tacitus and Josephus attest it from outside the movement; John Dominic Crossan, a determinedly skeptical scholar, calls it “as sure as anything historical can ever be” (Gerd Lüdemann and Bart D. Ehrman likewise grant the crucifixion in their own work). Dale C. Allison and Rafael Rodríguez press the criterion of embarrassment hardest — which is why the certainty is anchored on the attestation.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: the charge that the whole argument is circular — that “no one would invent it” is an unfalsifiable just-so story, and the criterion of embarrassment is an unreliable tool. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the reply below.

"Jews did expect a suffering Messiah — so a crucified Messiah was no scandal" Trickier

Strongest form

So the premise is wrong: a suffering, even dying, Messiah was a live Jewish category, and the cross fit it rather than scandalizing it.

Reply

A note of care is owed — and keep the distinction. It is true that ancient Judaism was not monolithic, and this track says so plainly: some read Isaiah 53 in an individual key, and the ben-Joseph tradition (mostly later) knew a suffering messianic figure. But the dominant hope was for a triumphant Davidic deliverer, and the specific scandal was never "suffering" in the abstract — it was crucifixion: a Roman execution of a condemned criminal, a body hung under the Deuteronomic curse (Deuteronomy 21:23). That is the opposite of a conquering king, and it is why a former Pharisee like Paul first found the claim a blasphemy worth persecuting.

"A crucified savior is just borrowed pagan myth" Common

Strongest form

Dying-and-rising gods were everywhere in the ancient world — Osiris, Adonis, Baal. A crucified and vindicated savior is just Christianity dressing an old pagan template in Jewish clothes; there is nothing embarrassing or un-inventable about it.

Reply

This gets the evidence backwards. The simple "dying-and-rising god as source of Christianity" thesis is now widely abandoned, while Tryggve Mettinger, arguing against the prevailing view, has maintained that some such figures genuinely existed — yet found "no prima facie evidence that the death and resurrection of Jesus is a mythological construct, drawing on the myths and rites of the dying and rising gods of the surrounding world" (treated in our essay on the uniqueness of the resurrection claim) — but the decisive point here is narrower: no pagan template involved crucifixion, the Roman slaves' death, or a savior under the curse of the Jewish Law. Those are the exact features that made the Christian claim offensive to Jew and Greek alike. A movement borrowing a flattering myth does not borrow the one detail that repels its whole audience; the scandal is evidence the crucifixion came from history, not from mythology.

"'He became a curse for us' is monstrous — cosmic child abuse" Trickier

Strongest form

The idea that God cursed and killed an innocent to satisfy his own wrath is morally repugnant — an angry Father punishing his Son in our place. However you dress it up, "he became a curse for us" makes God a divine child-abuser.

Reply

This objection targets one crude version of one theory of the atonement, not the shared Christian confession — and it misreads even that. On classical Christian teaching the one who "became a curse for us" is not a third party the Father abuses but God himself, the eternal Son, willingly giving himself: "God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself" (2 Corinthians 5:19). It is self-giving, not child abuse. And "became a curse for us" never means the Father hated the Son, nor that the divine nature could die or be cursed. As to how the cross saves, the church has confessed it in several orthodox ways (Christus Victor and others, not only penal substitution); this track states the shared confession — "Christ died for our sins," the curse borne, God and sinners reconciled — and does not stake the case on any one mechanism.

Strongest form

It is poorly suited to explain a group proclaiming a brand-new category — a crucified and cursed Messiah, raised bodily ahead of the last day — contrary to its entire tradition, to describe a man it had given up for lost.

"Even if the crucifixion is bedrock, that proves nothing about the resurrection" Hard

Strongest form

Grant it all — the crucifixion is historical, and offensive, and the first Christians didn't invent it. So what? A man was executed and his followers found meaning in it. That does nothing to show he rose, or that any of the theology is true.

Reply

Scope is owed: the scandal clears the ground; it does not, alone, raise the dead. What it establishes is that the crucifixion is authentic bedrock and that the earliest Christians met it head-on — not that the theology is true. An honest critic can grant every word here and still ask whether the crucified man stayed dead — which is exactly the question the resurrection evidence, beginning with the creed Paul quotes in 1 Corinthians 15:3–7, answers. What the scandal does settle is decisive against one popular story: the cross was no fabrication, and the offensiveness explains why no one would invent it while leaving open why anyone would then celebrate it — a question the resurrection claim exists to answer.

Martin Hengel, Crucifixion in the Ancient World (Fortress, 1977), gathers the sources: the servile supplicium, the slaves' punishment, reserved for slaves and rebels. Cicero: the very word "cross" should be kept from a citizen's thoughts, his eyes, his ears (Pro Rabirio 5.16). Josephus calls it "the most wretched of deaths" (Jewish War 7.203).
Crossan · Tacitus · Josephus; Allison · Rodríguezthe state of scholarship
The crucifixion is granted across the spectrum: John Dominic Crossan calls it "as sure as anything historical can ever be," and it is attested by non-Christian writers (Tacitus, Annals 15.44; Josephus, Antiquities 18.3.3). The criterion of embarrassment, on which the un-invention argument leans, is itself contested — Dale Allison and Rafael Rodríguez argue it is unreliable used alone — which is why the crucifixion's certainty rests on the multiple attestation, not on the criterion by itself.
The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

The crucifixion is, by wide agreement, the most secure single fact about Jesus of Nazareth. Its bedrock status rests first on plain attestation — Christian and non-Christian (Tacitus, and the widely accepted core of Josephus) — and even determinedly skeptical scholars grant it. The scandal adds a second, converging consideration: a claim so damaging to its own cause is not the kind a movement fabricates.

A fairness most defenders skip: Two cautions are owed. The point about Jewish expectation is easily overstated: some read the Servant of Isaiah 53 in an individual, even messianic key (the Targum on Isaiah 52:13 names “my servant the Messiah,” even though it redirects the Servant’s sufferings to Israel and the nations), and later tradition knew of a suffering “Messiah son of Joseph” — the claim is only that the dominant hope was triumphant. And the un-invention reasoning is a historian’s tool of probability, not a proof; it has been criticized as unreliable when used alone, which is why it reinforces the attestation rather than replacing it.

The bounded verdict: The scandal of the cross is powerful evidence that the crucifixion is historical bedrock — the kind of fact a movement is stuck with, not the kind it invents — and that the earliest Christians met it not with denial but with a developed theology of the curse borne. It does not, on its own, demonstrate that the theology is true, nor that Jesus rose; those rest on further evidence. And we say it with the first Christians: the crucified Jesus is the Messiah; his death was for our sins, the curse borne and God and sinners reconciled; and God raised him. The scandal does not prove that last claim, but it clears the ground for it.

    "Mark doesn’t center the shame — he dresses the execution up as a royal scene: purple robe, crown, acclamation, the title over the cross. Some scholars even hear a Roman triumphal procession in it. That’s spin, the very minimizing you say the first Christians never did."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    Look at what the scene actually is. The soldiers dress Jesus in purple, press a crown of thorns onto his head, and salute him: “Hail, King of the Jews!” (Mark 15:17–18); the charge over the cross repeats the title (15:26). It is a mock coronation meant to humiliate, and Mark lets it stand, for his readers, as truth its mockers did not intend. The proposed triumphal-procession parallel is disputed, and nothing here depends on it; the mockery of the title itself is plain in the text. Mark’s posture is the same as Paul’s, and it is hard to explain as invention or salesmanship: people selling a fabricated hero do not stake everything on his most humiliating hour. People convinced that the most humiliating hour had been reversed — and had turned out to mean something — might.
