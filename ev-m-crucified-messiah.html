Why a Crucified Messiah? The Scandal of the Cross

The deep dive explains the case. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild from memory why a crucified, cursed Messiah was the one claim no one would invent, why that makes the crucifixion bedrock, and how the New Testament turns the curse into the gospel — then hold it together in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

Every movement leads with its best foot; the earliest Christians led with their worst. At the center of their message was not a teaching or a miracle but an execution — and not a noble one: a Messiah nailed to a Roman cross, the death reserved for slaves and rebels, the death a Jew's own Scriptures called a curse (Deuteronomy 21:23). "We preach Christ crucified," Paul wrote, "a stumbling block to Jews and folly to Gentiles" (1 Corinthians 1:23). It was the single fact most likely to kill the movement in its cradle — and they built their gospel on it.

That strange choice cuts two ways. First, it is evidence. The crucifixion is, by wide agreement, the most secure single fact about Jesus — resting first on multiple attestation (reported by non-Christian writers with no motive to flatter, Tacitus and Josephus) and conceded even by determined sceptics (John Dominic Crossan: "as sure as anything historical can ever be"). Converging with that plain attestation is a further consideration: inventors do not sabotage their own inventions, and no one fabricating a Messiah, or gilding a beloved teacher's memory, would choose the detail most damaging to the claim. Saul of Tarsus, who found the claim so intolerable he tried to destroy the movement, is the sharpest illustration. Second, the New Testament does not hide the scandal but centers it ("I decided to know nothing… except Jesus Christ and him crucified," 1 Corinthians 2:2) and answers it: "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us" (Galatians 3:13) — the very verse (Deuteronomy 21:23) that made a crucified Messiah a scandal becomes the key to what the cross accomplished.

Honest scope: the scandal establishes that the crucifixion is authentic bedrock (not invention) and that the earliest Christians met it with a theology of the curse borne — not, by itself, that Jesus rose (the separate resurrection case) or that the theology is true. The criterion of embarrassment is a probability, not a proof, and has its critics (Allison, Rodríguez), so the crucifixion's certainty is anchored on multiple attestation, not on it alone. And how the cross saves — the mechanism of the atonement — has been confessed in more than one orthodox way (penal substitution, Christus Victor, satisfaction, and others); this track states the shared confession ("Christ died for our sins," the curse borne, reconciliation) and crowns none of them. Nor does "became a curse for us" ever mean the divine nature died or that the Father hated the Son.

Understand it

The argument, formally

The crucifixion is bedrock, not invention — and the New Testament turns the curse into the gospel.

P1

A crucified Messiah was the single most offensive claim in its world: crucifixion was the most degrading Roman death (Hengel), reserved for slaves and rebels; Deuteronomy 21:23 put a hanged man under God's visible curse, so "crucified Messiah" was nearly a contradiction in terms; and to a Greek it was "folly" (1 Corinthians 1:23).

P2

The crucifixion is therefore historical bedrock — resting first on multiple attestation (Tacitus, Josephus), conceded even by sceptics; and, converging with that, no one inventing a Messiah, and no audience they hoped to win, would choose the detail most damaging to the claim — yet the earliest Christians centered it rather than hiding it ("we preach Christ crucified," 1 Corinthians 1:23; "boast… in the cross," Galatians 6:14), and Saul the persecutor, who found it a blasphemy, became its missionary.

∴ C

Therefore the crucifixion is authentic bedrock, not fabrication, and the New Testament does not evade the scandal but answers it — "Christ… becoming a curse for us" (Galatians 3:13), the curse borne in our place. (This does not by itself prove the resurrection — the separate event that turned scandal into gospel — and it crowns no single theory of how the cross saves.)

This establishes that the crucifixion is bedrock and that the earliest Christians met it with a theology of the curse borne — not, by itself, that Jesus rose or that the theology is true. Why "it was just an embarrassing invention" is the popular skeptic's move.

A skeptic may say the crucifixion was an awkward fact the first Christians spun into theology after the fact, or that the "criterion of embarrassment" is an unreliable trick. The reply grants that the criterion is a probability, not a proof — and then points out that the crucifixion's certainty does not rest on it: it is stated outright by non-Christian writers with no motive to flatter the movement (Tacitus, Josephus). The scandal is a converging consideration: not that the Christians were embarrassed (they gloried in the cross), but that no one inventing a Messiah would have chosen it.

Defending the premises

Why each premise is more plausible than its denial

Support for P1 — how offensive a crucified Messiah really was

The most degrading death Rome had

It is hard, at this distance, to feel the scandal — the cross is now jewelry. In the first century it was an instrument of torture so shameful that polite Romans avoided the word. The classicist Martin Hengel gathered the ancient sources into one verdict: crucifixion was the most wretched of deaths, reserved for slaves, violent criminals, and rebels, deliberately public and obscene. Cicero told a jury that the very word "cross" should be kept far from a Roman citizen's "thoughts, his eyes, his ears." To announce that one worshipped a crucified man was, to a cultured Greek, simply folly — the word Paul himself uses (1 Corinthians 1:23).

A body under God's curse

For a Jew the offense cut deeper and touched God directly. The Law was explicit: "a hanged man is cursed by God" (Deuteronomy 21:22–23). A crucified body on a Roman cross was, on the face of the text, a body under the visible curse of God — so "crucified Messiah" was nearly a contradiction in terms. The Messiah was to break the nations and reign, not die a condemned criminal's cursed death at the hands of the pagan empire he was meant to overthrow. A note of care: it is not that no strand of Judaism ever imagined a suffering figure — some read Isaiah 53 in an individual key, and later tradition knew of a "Messiah son of Joseph" — but the dominant hope was for a triumphant deliverer, and a crucified, cursed, Roman-executed Messiah was the opposite of what that hope expected.

Support for P2 — why the scandal is evidence, and how the New Testament answers it

Bedrock by attestation, un-invented by counterproductivity

Inventors do not sabotage their inventions. A group fabricating a messianic hero, or gilding a beloved teacher's memory, has every reason to omit the detail that most discredits the claim — and crucifixion was that detail. This is why the crucifixion is, by wide agreement, the most secure single fact about Jesus. Its bedrock status rests first on plain attestation: it is reported by non-Christian writers with no motive to flatter the movement — the Roman historian Tacitus, and (in a notice whose core is widely accepted as authentic) Josephus — and even determined sceptics grant it, John Dominic Crossan calling it "as sure as anything historical can ever be." The scandal adds a converging consideration: strictly, not that the crucifixion embarrassed the Christians who preached it (as the next card shows, they gloried in it) but that no one setting out to invent a Messiah would have chosen it. That reasoning is a tool of probability, not a proof, and has its critics (Allison, Rodríguez); here it reinforces the attestation. Saul the persecutor, who found the claim a blasphemy and became its missionary (Galatians 1:13–14), is the sharpest illustration.

The curse borne — and what the answer does not settle

The first Christians did not merely concede the curse; they claimed to understand it. "Christ redeemed us from the curse of the law by becoming a curse for us — for it is written, 'Cursed is everyone who is hanged on a tree'" (Galatians 3:13). The exact verse (Deuteronomy 21:23) that made a crucified Messiah a scandal becomes the key to what the cross accomplished: the curse was real, and it was borne, willingly, in the place of those who deserved it. The same reversal is read into Isaiah 53 (the Servant "pierced for our transgressions") and the Passover lamb (1 Corinthians 5:7). The honest limit: how the cross saves has been confessed in more than one orthodox way — penal substitution, Christus Victor, satisfaction, recapitulation, moral influence — and this track crowns none of them; it states the shared confession, "Christ died for our sins" (1 Corinthians 15:3), the curse borne, God and sinners reconciled. And "became a curse for us" never means the divine nature died, nor that the Father hated or was divided from the Son.

The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: the charge that the whole argument is circular — that “no one would invent it” is an unfalsifiable just-so story, and the criterion of embarrassment is an unreliable tool. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the reply below.

"The 'no one would invent it' reasoning is circular, and the criterion of embarrassment is unreliable" Hard

Strongest form

The criterion of embarrassment has been sharply criticized by New Testament scholars themselves (Dale Allison, Rafael Rodríguez): it is easy to abuse, hard to falsify, and often assumes we know what an ancient author would or would not have found embarrassing. "They wouldn't have invented a crucified Messiah" can look like a just-so story dressed as history.

Reply

Concede the criticism — and notice the argument does not actually rest on the criterion. The crucifixion's bedrock status rests first on plain multiple attestation: it is reported by non-Christian writers with no motive to flatter the movement (Tacitus; Josephus, in a notice whose core is widely accepted), and granted even by determined sceptics (Crossan: "as sure as anything historical can ever be"). The scandal is a converging consideration, offered as a probability, not a proof. And it avoids the usual circularity charge: the point is not the vague "embarrassing to the tradents" (they gloried in the cross), but the sharper, more falsifiable claim that a detail this damaging to one's own cause is not what a movement fabricates to win converts.

"Jews did expect a suffering Messiah — so a crucified Messiah was no scandal" Trickier

Strongest form

Isaiah 53 describes a suffering servant; some ancient Jewish texts read it messianically, and later rabbinic tradition speaks of a "Messiah son of Joseph" who suffers and dies. So the premise is wrong: a suffering, even dying, Messiah was a live Jewish category, and the cross fit it rather than scandalizing it.

Reply

Concede the real point — and keep the distinction. It is true that ancient Judaism was not monolithic, and this track says so plainly: some read Isaiah 53 in an individual key, and the ben-Joseph tradition (mostly later) knew a suffering messianic figure. But the dominant hope was for a triumphant Davidic deliverer, and the specific scandal was never "suffering" in the abstract — it was crucifixion: a Roman execution of a condemned criminal, a body hung under the Deuteronomic curse (Deuteronomy 21:23). That is the opposite of a conquering king, and it is why a former Pharisee like Paul first found the claim a blasphemy worth persecuting.

"A crucified savior is just borrowed pagan myth" Common

Strongest form

Dying-and-rising gods were everywhere in the ancient world — Osiris, Adonis, Baal. A crucified and vindicated savior is just Christianity dressing an old pagan template in Jewish clothes; there is nothing embarrassing or un-inventable about it.

Reply

This gets the evidence backwards. The alleged parallels are contested and mostly post-Christian (treated in our essay on the uniqueness of the resurrection claim) — but the decisive point here is narrower: no pagan template involved crucifixion, the Roman slaves' death, or a savior under the curse of the Jewish Law. Those are the exact features that made the Christian claim offensive to Jew and Greek alike. A movement borrowing a flattering myth does not borrow the one detail that repels its whole audience; the scandal is evidence the crucifixion came from history, not from mythology.

"'He became a curse for us' is monstrous — cosmic child abuse" Trickier

Strongest form

The idea that God cursed and killed an innocent to satisfy his own wrath is morally repugnant — an angry Father punishing his Son in our place. However you dress it up, "he became a curse for us" makes God a divine child-abuser.

Reply

This objection targets one particular theory of the atonement (a crude version of penal substitution), not the shared Christian confession — and it misreads even that. On classical Christian teaching the one who "became a curse for us" is not a third party the Father abuses but God himself, the eternal Son, willingly giving himself: "God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself" (2 Corinthians 5:19). It is self-giving, not child abuse. And "became a curse for us" never means the Father hated the Son, nor that the divine nature could die or be cursed. As to how the cross saves, the church has confessed it in several orthodox ways (Christus Victor and others, not only penal substitution); this track states the shared confession — "Christ died for our sins," the curse borne, God and sinners reconciled — and does not stake the case on any one mechanism.

"Even if the crucifixion is bedrock, that proves nothing about the resurrection" Hard

Strongest form

Grant it all — the crucifixion is historical, and offensive, and the first Christians didn't invent it. So what? A man was executed and his followers found meaning in it. That does nothing to show he rose, or that any of the theology is true.

Reply

Correct, and the argument concedes it in its own voice: the scandal clears the ground; it does not, alone, raise the dead. What it establishes is that the crucifixion is authentic bedrock and that the earliest Christians met it head-on — not that the theology is true. An honest critic can grant every word here and still ask whether the crucified man stayed dead; that is the separate work of the resurrection case (the empty tomb, the appearances, the hostile-witness conversions), argued on its own evidence. What the scandal does settle is decisive against one popular story: the cross was no fabrication, and the offensiveness explains why no one would invent it while leaving open why anyone would then celebrate it — a question the resurrection claim exists to answer.

    Answer the "just an embarrassing invention" objection

    A seeded scenario from the Debate Arena. Draft your reply, then reveal a model answer to compare.

    SK
    Skeptic
    "This whole 'nobody would invent a crucified Messiah' line is a circular trick. Early Christians clearly weren't embarrassed by the cross — they preached it constantly. And your 'criterion of embarrassment' is exactly the kind of unfalsifiable tool historians have been throwing out. You can't turn an awkward fact into proof of anything just by saying it's awkward."
    One strong way to answer — in a 1 Peter 3:15 tone

    "You're right on two counts, and I'll grant them up front. The criterion of embarrassment really has been criticized — Dale Allison and others say it's overused — and the early Christians really weren't embarrassed; they gloried in the cross. So let me not lean on the criterion the way you're expecting. First, the crucifixion doesn't need it: it's reported by non-Christian writers with no reason to flatter the movement — Tacitus and Josephus — and even a sceptic like John Dominic Crossan calls it 'as sure as anything historical can ever be.' That's plain multiple attestation, not a psychological guess. Second, the point about invention isn't 'it embarrassed them,' it's sharper and more testable: a crucified, cursed Messiah was the single most offensive thing you could preach to a first-century Jew or Greek — the slaves' death, a body under the Deuteronomic curse. Nobody inventing a Messiah, and nobody trying to win that audience, picks that detail. The fact that they built on it instead of quietly dropping it tells you they were stuck with it. Now — does that prove Jesus rose? No, and I won't claim it does; the scandal clears the ground, it doesn't raise the dead. But it does sink the easy story that the crucifixion was a convenient fiction. It's the opposite of convenient — which is exactly why it's bedrock."
