The Full Humanity of Christ

The deep dive explains the doctrine. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild the two-natures confession from memory, meet its hardest objections, and hold the deity and the humanity together in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

The historic confession about Jesus is not that he is God dressed up as a man, nor a blend of the two into something that is neither. It is the harder claim that the eternal Son of God, without ceasing to be God, took to himself a complete human nature — body and rational soul — and was born, lived, and died as a genuine human being. Fully God and fully man: two full natures in one person.

The evidence is woven through the earliest sources. Paul never argues for Christ's humanity; he assumes it — "born of a woman" (Galatians 4:4), "in the likeness of sinful flesh" yet without sin (Romans 8:3), "the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5). Hebrews states why it is not negotiable: he was "made like them, fully human in every way," so that he could represent us, truly die, and sympathize with us. The church confessed exactly this at Chalcedon (451), against Docetism (Christ only seemed human) and Apollinarianism (the divine Word replacing his human mind).

Honest scope: this does not by itself prove the deity of Christ — that case is made in the companion essays, and the two natures stand or fall together. Two questions it deliberately leaves open, where orthodox Christians disagree: whether Christ was able to sin (his real temptation and actual sinlessness are affirmed; the further question is not settled here), and the relation of Jesus' brothers to Mary. What it establishes is modest and immovable: the earliest sources present a genuinely human Jesus — affirmed alongside, never instead of, his full deity.

The argument, formally

Fully God AND fully man — two full natures in one person.

P1

The earliest and best sources present Jesus as a genuine human being — "born of a woman" (Galatians 4:4), sent "in the likeness of sinful flesh" yet without sin (Romans 8:3), "the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5), "made like them, fully human in every way" (Hebrews 2:17).

P2

The same sources give Jesus the worship and the divine name reserved for God alone — so the humanity does not compete with the deity. The church confessed both together against Docetism and Apollinarianism, and sealed it at Chalcedon: "truly God and truly man."

∴ C

Therefore Jesus is fully God AND fully man — two full natures in one person — and only such a Christ can represent us, truly die for us, and sympathize with us.

This does not by itself prove the deity — that case is made elsewhere, and the two natures stand or fall together. Its modest, immovable point: the sources present a genuinely human Jesus, and "fully human" is affirmed alongside, never instead of, "fully God."

Why the humanity was one of the church's first fights.

Among the earliest errors the church faced, teachers denied not that Jesus was God but that he was a man — a bleeding, dying God seemed too low. So John made the flesh the test: "every spirit that acknowledges that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is from God" (1 John 4:2). A phantom cannot bleed, and a phantom's cross saves no one.

Defending Premise 1

The earliest and best sources present Jesus as a genuine human being — “born of a woman” (Galatians 4:4), sent “in the likeness of sinful flesh” yet without sin (Romans 8:3), “the man Christ Jesus” (1 Timothy 2:5), “made like them, fully human in every way” (Hebrews 2:17).

Precision check: the doctrine is that the eternal Son of God, without ceasing to be God, took to himself a complete human nature — body and rational soul — and was born, lived, and died as a genuine human being. Without ceasing to be God rules out the idea that the deity was traded away for the humanity; complete human nature — body and rational soul rules out a Christ who is human on the outside only; born, lived, and died insists this is history, not appearance. The argument is not that Jesus was a mere man; it is that the one who is God also became truly man. Not that the Father or the divine nature turned into flesh, nor that God suffered as God: the divine nature did not convert into a human one.

Paul assumes it — Galatians 4:4 and Romans 8:3 Presupposed, not argued

The oldest datable Christian documents we possess are the letters of Paul, generally dated to the 50s of the first century — earlier than any of the Gospels. Paul never argues for Christ’s humanity; he everywhere assumes it, which is a stronger kind of evidence, because a thing presupposed offhandedly by a writer making some other point was not up for debate.

“When the set time had fully come, God sent his Son, born of a woman, born under the law.” Born of a woman — the Son entered the world through a mother, in a body. Born under the law — he entered it as a first-century Jew, bound by Torah, located in a real people at a real moment. This is Galatians, one of the letters no serious scholar disputes Paul wrote.

In Romans, Paul says God sent “his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh.” The word likeness has misled readers into hearing a hint of mere appearance — as if Christ only resembled human flesh. But the phrase is “the likeness of sinful flesh,” not “the likeness of flesh.” On a reading widely defended in the commentary tradition, the qualifier bears on “sinful” rather than on “flesh”: Christ took real, full human flesh, but not the sinfulness that marks fallen humanity.

Honest limit: The point does not finally hinge on that grammatical judgment — even interpreters who construe the phrase differently agree on the two things the verse holds together: his flesh was genuine, and he was without sin.

The Pastoral Epistles — corroboration only Corroboration · authorship disputed

Two texts press the point still harder, and they come from the letters to Timothy. “For there is one God and one mediator between God and mankind, the man Christ Jesus” — Paul names Christ’s manhood as essential to his work of standing between God and us. And, in what looks like a snatch of an early hymn, the claim that Christ “appeared in the flesh” — language that reads almost as if aimed in advance at the docetists John would confront.

Honest limit: The letters to Timothy and Titus (the “Pastoral Epistles”) are the Pauline letters whose authorship critical scholars most often question, and a fair reader should know that. But the case for Christ’s humanity does not lean on them. It stands complete on the letters no one disputes — Galatians, Romans, 1 Corinthians, Philippians — with the Pastorals as corroboration, not foundation. And “the man Christ Jesus” is not saying that Jesus is merely a man: the same letters call him the one whose grace was given “before the beginning of time” and who “appeared in the flesh”; his humanity is real without cancelling his deity.

The last Adam — humanity at the center of the gospel Paul’s deliberate case

Where Paul dwells on Christ’s humanity most deliberately is his Adam theology. In 1 Corinthians 15 he sets the two representative men side by side: “For since death came through a man, the resurrection of the dead comes also through a man.” The parallel he draws in Romans 5 turns on exactly this: as one man’s trespass brought condemnation, so “the one man, Jesus Christ” brings the gift of grace. Christ can undo what Adam did only because he stands where Adam stood — as a real human being, the head of a renewed humanity.

Honest limit: Paul genuinely uses Adam–Christ typology, and it is one of his richest ways of asserting Christ’s true humanity. But the typology does not reduce Christ to a merely human second Adam. As N. T. Wright and Gordon Fee argue against James Dunn’s purely Adamic reading, the Philippians hymn describes one who was already “in the form of God” before “taking the form of a servant” and being “born in the likeness of men” — a transition Adam never made. The pre-existence question — Dunn versus Wright, Fee, Bauckham, and Hurtado — is a real and ongoing scholarly debate, not a settled rout, though the weight of recent scholarship has moved toward reading genuine pre-existence in the text.

The Gospels and Hebrews state it outright Whole New Testament

The Gospels show a Jesus who grows (Luke 2:52), tires and sleeps, hungers and thirsts, weeps at a friend’s grave (John 11:35), sweats and bleeds, and dies a real bodily death that a Roman execution squad was expert at confirming. None of this is incidental scenery; it is the texture of a genuine human life.

Hebrews makes the theology explicit: “Since the children have flesh and blood, he too shared in their humanity”; therefore “he had to be made like them, fully human in every way, in order that he might become a merciful and faithful high priest.” The logic is that of representation: a high priest must belong to the people he represents. And the same letter presses it toward comfort: “we do not have a high priest who is unable to empathize with our weaknesses, but we have one who has been tempted in every way, just as we are — yet he did not sin.” His temptations were real; his sinlessness was real; the two are held together, not played against each other.

Honest limit: That Jesus was tempted and did not sin is not in dispute; Scripture states it plainly. But whether he was able to sin — posse non peccare (“able not to sin”) versus non posse peccare (“not able to sin”) — is a genuine intramural debate with serious defenders on each side. What all orthodox parties affirm, and all this page affirms, is that his temptations were real and his sinlessness was actual.

Objection — “The Pastoral Epistles are post-Pauline, so those texts should not count”

Since the Pastoral Epistles are widely thought to be post-Pauline, “the man Christ Jesus” and “appeared in the flesh” should not count.

Reply

Concede the disputed authorship, and notice that nothing in the argument depends on it. Galatians 4:4, Romans 8:3, 1 Corinthians 15, and the humanity of the Philippians hymn are all in the undisputed letters, and Hebrews and the Gospels stand entirely outside the Pastorals question. Remove 1 and 2 Timothy and Titus from the table and the case is unweakened.

The room: That Jesus was a real first-century human being is common ground between confessing Christians and the great majority of secular historians; scholars who deny his deity almost all still affirm his humanity. The live scholarly debate is almost entirely about the other nature — how early, and how high, the confession of his deity runs. The authorship of the Pastoral Epistles is disputed in critical scholarship: many scholars regard them as later, pseudonymous works, while others defend direct or secretarial Pauline authorship.
Defending Premise 2

The same sources give Jesus the worship and the divine name reserved for God alone — so the humanity does not compete with the deity. The church confessed both together against Docetism and Apollinarianism, and sealed it at Chalcedon: “truly God and truly man.”

Precision check: the historic confession is not either God or man, and not a blend of the two into something that is neither; it is the harder, stranger claim that he is fully God and fully man. This premise retracts nothing of the humanity defended above — and this page does not, by itself, prove the deity of Christ: that case is made elsewhere, and it must be, because the two natures stand or fall together.

Two natures, held in one person The logic of the confession

It is worth being precise about the logic, because the humanity is the premise skeptics least expect a Christian to press. The argument is not that Jesus was a mere man; it is that the one who is God also became truly man. So affirming the humanity does nothing to weaken the deity — the two natures are held together in one person, a point the church spent four centuries learning to state without contradiction.

In the fourth century Gregory of Nazianzus put it exactly: “What He was He continued to be; what He was not He took to Himself” — the incarnate Son added humanity and subtracted no deity.

The earliest worship Evidence of kind, not of date

The New Testament that shows Jesus hungry also has him receiving the worship and the divine name reserved for YHWH alone (the case laid out in Philippians 2 and the HANDS essay). The development is in the clarity, not in the substance.

Honest limit: The full case for the deity is made in the companion essays linked above, and is not re-argued here.

What the church ruled out — Docetism Why the flesh was “the test”

Docetism (from the Greek dokein, “to seem”) held that Christ only appeared to have a body and only seemed to suffer — the very error 1 John was written against. Ignatius of Antioch, on his way to martyrdom around AD 110, answered it with vehemence: Christ “was truly born” and “truly…nailed [to the cross] for us in His flesh,” and “suffered truly, even as also He truly raised up Himself, not, as certain unbelievers maintain, that He only seemed to suffer.” If Christ’s suffering was theater, Ignatius saw, then so was our salvation.

What the church ruled out — Apollinarianism Subtler, and more instructive

Apollinarianism was subtler and, for that reason, more instructive. Apollinaris of Laodicea granted Christ a human body but taught that the divine Word simply replaced the human mind or soul — a kind of divine intelligence driving a human body; the church condemned the view at the Council of Constantinople in 381. It sounds pious, and it seems to protect the deity. But it leaves Christ’s humanity a shell. Gregory of Nazianzus replied: “that which He has not assumed He has not healed; but that which is united to His Godhead is also saved.” If the Son did not take a human mind, then the human mind — the very seat of our rebellion — is left unredeemed. To save the whole person, the Son had to become a whole person.

Chalcedon Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant alike

The Council of Chalcedon in 451 drew the boundary that has defined orthodox Christianity across Catholic, Orthodox, and Protestant lines ever since: one and the same Christ, “truly God and truly man,” “consubstantial with us according to the manhood; in all things like unto us, without sin,” made known “in two natures, inconfusedly, unchangeably, indivisibly, inseparably” — that is, without confusion, change, division, or separation. Two full natures, one person — neither absorbed into the other, neither diminished by the other.

Honest limit: Nor does this page resolve every mystery in how one person can be at once infinite and finite, all-knowing as God and growing in wisdom as man; the creeds fenced that mystery rather than dissolving it.

Objection — the “promotion” thesis: a man later made into a god Hardest

If you press the humanity this hard — born, tempted, ignorant of the hour (Mark 13:32), praying in agony, dying — have you not simply described a man? Does not the full weight of the human evidence support the thesis that Jesus was a human being whom later Christians promoted to divinity? This is, in effect, the case Bart Ehrman makes in How Jesus Became God: that Jesus’ earliest followers held an “exaltation” Christology — God raised the man Jesus and adopted him to divine status — yet Ehrman also finds a pre-existent Christ already in material older than Paul, a figure he takes to have been conceived at first as a subordinate divine being, whose elevation to the co-equal God confessed at Nicaea is the later step. A sharper form runs through the ancient heresy of adoptionism — that God adopted the man Jesus as his Son, whether at his baptism or his resurrection — and through James Dunn’s influential argument that a full doctrine of the incarnation emerges clearly only in John’s Gospel. The factual kernel is accurate: the humanity really is everywhere in the sources, and any honest account has to reckon with it rather than explain it away.

Reply

We grant — gladly, since it is the whole point of this page — that the human evidence is pervasive and real. What we do not grant is the inference that humanity competes with deity, so that the more human Jesus is shown to be, the less divine he can be. That inference is precisely what the two-natures confession denies, and it denies it on the strength of the same sources. The disagreement is not really about dates — Ehrman himself finds a pre-existent Christ in material older than Paul’s letters — but about what kind of being the earliest Christians took him to be. And the evidence answers that question with the one thing his developmental thesis cannot easily accommodate: the earliest worship. Marana tha (1 Corinthians 16:22) is Aramaic, pre-Pauline, rooted in the Jerusalem church of the 30s. The point needs care, because a gesture of homage was not itself the line: Israelites could bow before a king as well as before the LORD (1 Chronicles 29:20), and some texts picture homage to an exalted figure. What Jewish monotheism guarded jealously for God was not the posture of homage but the substance of worship—sacrifice, invocation, prayer. And no intermediary figure was ever placed inside the Shema. Wisdom is praised; Enoch is exalted; but no Jewish writer took the creed of divine uniqueness and wrote a second figure into it. Paul did (a reading genuinely contested, not unanimous — James F. McGrath argues that “one Lord” is set alongside, not within, the Shema in 1 Cor 8:6). Against Dunn, “though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor” (2 Corinthians 8:9) reads most naturally — though a metaphor of earthly poverty is possible — as a transition from a prior state. And adoptionism founders on the same texts: Paul’s Son is sent “when the set time had fully come,” not created or adopted en route; he is the one who was “in the form of God” before he took a servant’s form. As for Mark 13:32, the orthodox tradition has long held that the Son’s not knowing the hour belongs to the genuine limits of his human consciousness, not to a deficiency in the divine nature he never ceased to possess. One may find that distinction subtle; it is not ad hoc, for it is the very logic of an incarnation. The correct conclusion from the abundant humanity is not “therefore only a man,” but “therefore truly a man” — which is the very thing Chalcedon affirms alongside, not instead of, his deity. So the developmental objection does not hold.

The room: Bart Ehrman (How Jesus Became God) presses the promotion thesis, and James Dunn argues that a full doctrine of the incarnation emerges clearly only in John’s Gospel; N. T. Wright and Gordon Fee argue against Dunn’s purely Adamic reading, with Bauckham and Hurtado on the same side of the pre-existence question — a real and ongoing scholarly debate, not a settled rout, though the weight of recent scholarship has moved toward reading genuine pre-existence in the text. The live scholarly debate is almost entirely about how early, and how high, the confession of his deity runs; the humanity was never the church’s problem to prove.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: the "promotion" thesis — that the abundant human evidence means Jesus was a man his followers later made into a god (Ehrman; adoptionism). The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the reply below.

"So much human evidence — he was just a man, later called God" Hard

Strongest form

If you press the humanity this hard — born, tempted, ignorant of the hour, dying — you have described a man. The full weight of the human evidence supports the thesis that Jesus was a human being whom later Christians promoted to divinity (Ehrman, How Jesus Became God) — though Ehrman also finds a pre-existent Christ already before Paul, taken as a subordinate divine being whose elevation to co-equal God is the later step.

Reply

Grant the concession first: the human evidence is pervasive and real — that is the whole point of the doctrine. What does not follow is that humanity competes with deity. The same early sources that show Jesus hungry also give him the worship and divine name reserved for YHWH alone. The disagreement is not really about dates — Ehrman himself finds a pre-existent Christ in material older than Paul's letters — but about what kind of being the earliest Christians took him to be; and the earliest evidence answers it: Marana tha (1 Corinthians 16:22), Aramaic prayer addressed to him by the first Judean church. As for Mark 13:32, the orthodox tradition has long held that the Son's not knowing the hour belongs to the genuine limits of his human consciousness, not to a deficiency in the divine nature he never ceased to possess. The right conclusion from the abundant humanity is not "therefore only a man," but "therefore truly a man." So the developmental objection does not hold.

"'Likeness of sinful flesh' means he only seemed human" Common

Strongest form

Romans 8:3 says Christ came in the "likeness" of sinful flesh — likeness, not the thing itself. So Paul is hedging: Jesus only resembled a human being.

Reply

The phrase is "the likeness of sinful flesh," not "the likeness of flesh." On the reading most widely defended in the commentary tradition, the qualifier bears on "sinful," not on the reality of his flesh: Christ took real, full human flesh, but not the sinfulness that marks fallen humanity. And the point does not finally hinge on the grammar — interpreters who construe it differently still agree on the two things the verse holds together: his flesh was genuine, and he was without sin.

"'The last Adam' just makes Jesus a second human archetype" Trickier

Strongest form

Paul's Adam-Christ typology (1 Corinthians 15; Romans 5) shows an exalted human being, a "second Adam," not a pre-existent divine one. "Form of God" in Philippians 2 could just mean bearing God's image, the way Adam did.

Reply

Paul genuinely uses Adam typology to assert Christ's true humanity — but it does not reduce him to a merely human second Adam. As N. T. Wright and Gordon Fee argue against James Dunn's purely Adamic reading, the one who became the last Adam was already "in the form of God" before taking "the form of a servant" — a transition Adam never made. The pre-existence question is a real and ongoing scholarly debate, not a settled rout, though the weight of recent scholarship has moved toward reading genuine pre-existence in the text. The theme does two jobs at once: it establishes real humanity, and it presupposes that the one who became the last Adam was already fully God.

"Fully God and fully man is a flat contradiction" Hard

Strongest form

One person cannot be both infinite and finite, all-knowing and growing in wisdom, unable to die and able to die. Chalcedon is just a form of words papering over a contradiction.

Reply

The confession does not say one nature is both — it says one person subsists in two distinct natures, "without confusion" and "without change," so that no property of one nature cancels the other. That is not shown to be contradictory; it is a mystery. Honesty requires admitting the creeds fenced the mystery of how one person can be at once infinite and finite rather than dissolving it — but "hard to picture" is not the same as "incoherent," and the objection has to show both sets of properties predicated of one nature in the same respect, which is exactly what Chalcedon denies.

"If he was really tempted, he could have sinned — so he wasn't God" Hard

Strongest form

Hebrews says Jesus was "tempted in every way, just as we are." But temptation is only real if failing is possible — so either his temptations were play-acting, or he could have sinned, which God cannot.

Reply

What every orthodox Christian affirms, and all this argument affirms, is that his temptations were real and his sinlessness was actual — the two are held together, not played against each other (Hebrews 4:15). Whether he was able to sin — the old distinction between "able not to sin" and "not able to sin" — is a genuine intramural debate with serious defenders on each side, and this is deliberately not the place it is settled. The objection assumes one answer to that in-house question; it does not touch the shared claim that the temptation and the sinlessness were both real.

The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

On the main claim, the picture is far less contested than the surrounding footnotes might suggest. That Jesus was a real first-century human being is common ground between confessing Christians and the great majority of secular historians; scholars who deny his deity almost all still affirm his humanity. The live scholarly debate is almost entirely about the other nature — how early, and how high, the confession of his deity runs.

A fairness most defenders skip: Two places where thoughtful, orthodox Christians disagree, and on which this argument takes no side: whether Christ was able to sin (his real temptation and actual sinlessness are affirmed; the further question is not settled here), and the relation of Jesus’ brothers to Mary, a Marian question that has long divided Catholic and Orthodox Christians from Protestants — the argument rests only on the plain fact that the earliest sources speak of Jesus’ human family. And the authorship of the Pastoral Epistles is disputed in critical scholarship; the case stands complete without them.

The bounded verdict: The earliest and best sources present Jesus as a genuine human being — born of a woman, living under the law, taking real flesh, tempted, bleeding, dying — and the church has always confessed exactly that, over against every attempt to make him merely seem human. The Incarnation is not God wearing humanity like a garment he might slip off; it is the eternal Son making our nature his own, all the way down to the grave, so that what he assumed he might heal. A Christ who is not one of us cannot represent us, cannot die for us, and cannot sympathize with us. The gospel needs him to be, and joyfully confesses him to be, fully God and fully man.

    SK
    Skeptic
    "You spend all your energy proving Jesus is God. But the earliest evidence is a Galilean who got hungry, wept, and died. Isn't the simplest reading just that he was a man his followers later turned into a god?"
    One strong way to answer — in a 1 Peter 3:15 tone

    "You're right that the human Jesus is everywhere in the sources — and I'd never want to explain that away, because his real humanity is half of what I actually believe. But the argument isn't really about dates — even Ehrman finds a pre-existent Christ in material older than Paul. It's about what kind of being the first Christians took him to be, and the earliest church prayed to him: Marana tha, "our Lord, come." The same sources give him the worship and the divine name reserved for God alone. The right conclusion from all that human evidence isn't 'therefore only a man' — it's 'therefore truly a man,' which is exactly what the church confessed: fully God and fully man, without ceasing to be either. And it has to be both, because a Savior who wasn't truly one of us couldn't stand in for us or die in our place."

    One you weren’t coached on

    NS
    NT student
    "A full doctrine of the incarnation emerges clearly only in John’s Gospel. Paul’s Son is a man God exalted — or, as adoptionists held, adopted — nowhere a prior being who became anything."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    “Though he was rich, yet for your sake he became poor” (2 Corinthians 8:9) reads most naturally — though a metaphor of earthly poverty is possible — as a transition from a prior state. And adoptionism founders on the same texts: Paul’s Son is sent “when the set time had fully come,” not created or adopted en route; he is the one who was “in the form of God” before he took a servant’s form — a transition Adam never made. The pre-existence question is a real and ongoing scholarly debate, not a settled rout, though the weight of recent scholarship has moved toward reading genuine pre-existence in the text. The development is in the clarity, not in the substance: full humanity, assumed by one who was already fully God.
