The Full Humanity of Christ

The deep dive explains the doctrine. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild the two-natures confession from memory, meet its hardest objections, and hold the deity and the humanity together in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

The historic confession about Jesus is not that he is God dressed up as a man, nor a blend of the two into something that is neither. It is the harder claim that the eternal Son of God, without ceasing to be God, took to himself a complete human nature — body and rational soul — and was born, lived, and died as a genuine human being. Fully God and fully man: two full natures in one person.

The evidence is woven through the earliest sources. Paul never argues for Christ's humanity; he assumes it — "born of a woman" (Galatians 4:4), "in the likeness of sinful flesh" yet without sin (Romans 8:3), "the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5). Hebrews states why it is not negotiable: he was "made like them, fully human in every way," so that he could represent us, truly die, and sympathize with us. The church confessed exactly this at Chalcedon (451), against Docetism (Christ only seemed human) and Apollinarianism (the divine Word replacing his human mind).

Honest scope: this does not by itself prove the deity of Christ — that case is made in the companion essays, and the two natures stand or fall together. Two questions it deliberately leaves open, where orthodox Christians disagree: whether Christ was able to sin (his real temptation and actual sinlessness are affirmed; the further question is not settled here), and the relation of Jesus' brothers to Mary. What it establishes is modest and immovable: the earliest sources present a genuinely human Jesus — affirmed alongside, never instead of, his full deity.

Understand it

Not more prose — the load-bearing structure. See the skeleton, then learn to defend each bone.

The argument, formally

Fully God AND fully man — two full natures in one person.

P1

The earliest and best sources present Jesus as a genuine human being — "born of a woman" (Galatians 4:4), sent "in the likeness of sinful flesh" yet without sin (Romans 8:3), "the man Christ Jesus" (1 Timothy 2:5), "made like them, fully human in every way" (Hebrews 2:17).

P2

The same sources give Jesus the worship and the divine name reserved for God alone — so the humanity does not compete with the deity. The church confessed both together against Docetism and Apollinarianism, and sealed it at Chalcedon: "truly God and truly man."

∴ C

Therefore Jesus is fully God AND fully man — two full natures in one person — and only such a Christ can represent us, truly die for us, and sympathize with us.

This does not by itself prove the deity — that case is made elsewhere, and the two natures stand or fall together. Its modest, immovable point: the sources present a genuinely human Jesus, and "fully human" is affirmed alongside, never instead of, "fully God."

Why the humanity was one of the church's first fights.

Among the earliest errors the church faced, teachers denied not that Jesus was God but that he was a man — a bleeding, dying God seemed too low. So John made the flesh the test: "every spirit that acknowledges that Jesus Christ has come in the flesh is from God" (1 John 4:2). A phantom cannot bleed, and a phantom's cross saves no one.

Defending the premises

Why each premise is more plausible than its denial

Support for P1 — the humanity is everywhere in the sources

Paul assumes it, rather than arguing it

Paul never argues for Christ's humanity; he everywhere assumes it — "born of a woman, born under the law" (Galatians 4:4) — which is a stronger kind of evidence, because a thing presupposed offhandedly by a writer making some other point was not up for debate. The case stands on the undisputed letters even if the Pastorals are set aside.

Hebrews and the Gospels state it outright

The Gospels show a Jesus who grows, tires, hungers, weeps (John 11:35), and dies a real bodily death. Hebrews makes the theology explicit: "he had to be made like them, fully human in every way" (2:17).

Support for P2 — the humanity does not compete with the deity

Two natures, one person

Affirming the humanity does nothing to weaken the deity: the one who is God also became truly man, and the two natures are held together in one person. As Gregory of Nazianzus put it, "What He was He continued to be; what He was not He took to Himself."

What the church ruled out

Docetism (Christ only seemed to have a body) and Apollinarianism (the Word replacing his human mind) were both rejected — Apollinarianism condemned at Constantinople (381), the two-natures boundary sealed at Chalcedon (451): "truly God and truly man," in two natures without confusion, change, division, or separation.

The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: the "promotion" thesis — that the abundant human evidence means Jesus was a man his followers later made into a god (Ehrman; adoptionism). The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the reply below.

"So much human evidence — he was just a man, later called God" Hard

Strongest form

If you press the humanity this hard — born, tempted, ignorant of the hour, dying — you have described a man. The full weight of the human evidence supports the thesis that Jesus was a human being whom later Christians promoted to divinity (Ehrman, How Jesus Became God).

Reply

Grant the concession first: the human evidence is pervasive and real — that is the whole point of the doctrine. What does not follow is that humanity competes with deity. The same early sources that show Jesus hungry also give him the worship and divine name reserved for YHWH alone, and our earliest material (the Philippians 2 passage, the reshaped Shema of 1 Corinthians 8:6) already presupposes a divine Christ rather than arriving at one late. The right conclusion from the abundant humanity is not "therefore only a man," but "therefore truly a man."

"'The man Christ Jesus' is from the Pastorals — disputed, so drop it" Trickier

Strongest form

1 and 2 Timothy and Titus are widely thought to be post-Pauline, so "the man Christ Jesus" and "appeared in the flesh" should not count as Paul's own witness.

Reply

Concede the disputed authorship, and notice that nothing in the case depends on it. Galatians 4:4, Romans 8:3, 1 Corinthians 15, and the humanity of the Philippians hymn are all in the undisputed letters, and Hebrews and the Gospels stand entirely outside the Pastorals question. Remove 1 and 2 Timothy and Titus from the table and the case is unweakened.

"'Likeness of sinful flesh' means he only seemed human" Common

Strongest form

Romans 8:3 says Christ came in the "likeness" of sinful flesh — likeness, not the thing itself. So Paul is hedging: Jesus only resembled a human being.

Reply

The phrase is "the likeness of sinful flesh," not "the likeness of flesh." On the reading most widely defended in the commentary tradition, the qualifier bears on "sinful," not on the reality of his flesh: Christ took real, full human flesh, but not the sinfulness that marks fallen humanity. And the point does not finally hinge on the grammar — interpreters who construe it differently still agree on the two things the verse holds together: his flesh was genuine, and he was without sin.

"'The last Adam' just makes Jesus a second human archetype" Trickier

Strongest form

Paul's Adam-Christ typology (1 Corinthians 15; Romans 5) shows an exalted human being, a "second Adam," not a pre-existent divine one. "Form of God" in Philippians 2 could just mean bearing God's image, the way Adam did.

Reply

Paul genuinely uses Adam typology to assert Christ's true humanity — but it does not reduce him to a merely human second Adam. As N. T. Wright and Gordon Fee argue against a purely Adamic reading, the one who became the last Adam was already "in the form of God" before taking "the form of a servant" — a transition Adam never made. The theme does two jobs at once: it establishes real humanity, and it presupposes that the one who became the last Adam was already fully God.

"Fully God and fully man is a flat contradiction" Hard

Strongest form

One person cannot be both infinite and finite, all-knowing and growing in wisdom, unable to die and able to die. Chalcedon is just a form of words papering over a contradiction.

Reply

The confession does not say one nature is both — it says one person subsists in two distinct natures, "without confusion" and "without change," so that no property of one nature cancels the other. That is not shown to be contradictory; it is unusual. Honesty requires admitting the creeds fenced the mystery of how one person can be at once infinite and finite rather than dissolving it — but "hard to picture" is not the same as "incoherent," and no one has produced the contradiction.

"If he was really tempted, he could have sinned — so he wasn't God" Hard

Strongest form

Hebrews says Jesus was "tempted in every way, just as we are." But temptation is only real if failing is possible — so either his temptations were play-acting, or he could have sinned, which God cannot.

Reply

What every orthodox Christian affirms, and all this argument affirms, is that his temptations were real and his sinlessness was actual — the two are held together, not played against each other (Hebrews 4:15). Whether he was able to sin — the old distinction between "able not to sin" and "not able to sin" — is a genuine intramural debate with serious defenders on each side, and this is deliberately not the place it is settled. The objection assumes one answer to that in-house question; it does not touch the shared claim that the temptation and the sinlessness were both real.
