The Conversion of the Sceptics

The deep dive explains the argument. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild it from memory, meet its hardest objections, and stay clear and calm in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

This argument concentrates the case on the witnesses whose biases ran the wrong way: the sceptics. James, the brother of Jesus, did not believe during the ministry — the Gospels candidly report that his family thought Jesus out of his mind (Mark 3; John 7). Saul of Tarsus went further, actively persecuting the church. Neither man had any sympathetic motive to embrace the movement. Yet the very early creed Paul cites in 1 Corinthians 15, whose core (vv. 3–5) most scholars, including sceptics, date to within two to five years of the crucifixion — records that the risen Jesus "appeared to James," and Paul names his own encounter in the same breath.

After the crucifixion James became a leader of the Jerusalem church and was eventually executed (an event noted by the Jewish historian Josephus), while Paul gave his life to the gospel he had tried to crush. The unbelief of Jesus' own family is the kind of embarrassing detail no church would invent, which makes the reversal a fact even hostile scholarship must reckon with.

The honest scope: friendly witnesses can sometimes be explained by grief or expectation, but those explanations strain to reach minds set against the claim. The argument does not stand alone — it sharpens the wider case by showing the appearances reached committed opponents, not just hopeful followers. And this is what we hold to be true: Jesus of Nazareth was bodily raised from the dead, and the wider case — the empty tomb, the failure of the alternatives, the conversions of James and Paul — shows it.

Before you start · key terms

Eight words this argument leans on

Hostile witnessA witness who testifies against his own prior convictions, against his own interests, against everything he had reason to want. His agreement is hard to explain away as wishful thinking.

The argument, formally

The witnesses with nothing to gain and everything to lose.

P1

James, the brother of Jesus, did not believe during the ministry — the family thought him out of his mind (Mark 3; John 7).

P2

After the crucifixion James appears as leader of the Jerusalem church, dying for the faith — and the creed gives the cause: then he appeared to James. The persecutor Paul follows the same arc.

∴ C

Therefore the appearances reached hostile minds — the cases that explanations built on faith, grief, or longing handle poorly.

Friendly witnesses can be explained by psychology; hostile witnesses are the cases the naturalistic theories handle poorly. The argument concentrates the case on the two men whose biases ran the wrong way.

Then the evidential principle — embarrassment plus enmity

The unbelief of Jesus’s own family is doubly secure: it is embarrassing (no church invents a messiah his brothers rejected) and a strong inference from two independent strands (Mark 3:21; John 7:5). That makes the reversal — sceptic to martyred leader of the Jerusalem church — a datum even hostile scholarship must explain.

Defending Premise 1

James, the brother of Jesus, did not believe during the ministry — the family thought him out of his mind (Mark 3; John 7).

Precision check: Mark 3:21 is a statement about the family as a whole, not one that names James (though Mark 6:3 names the brothers, James first, elsewhere). John 7:5 is more sweeping: “For not even his brothers believed in him.” James’s own unbelief is therefore a strong inference from this family tradition rather than a line that singles him out by name — but it is a well-grounded inference.

Criterion of embarrassment Strong · an inference

Mark 3:21 — his relatives “went out to seize him, for they were saying, ‘He is out of his mind’”; John 7:5 — “not even his brothers believed in him.” That the founder’s own family thought him mad, and that one of the movement’s chief leaders had to be won over from outright unbelief, is not the kind of detail a community fabricates to flatter itself. The early church gains nothing and loses face by recording this; it stands because it happened.

Honest limit: the criterion is a tool of historians — a tradition awkward for the people who preserved it is unlikely to have been invented by them — not a proof.

Independent streams Strong · an inference

The unbelief rests on two independent strands about Jesus’ family. It appears in Mark and John independently — two of the strongest attestation positions available.

Honest limit: the Markan strand speaks of “his family” as a whole, and John 7:5 of “his brothers”; neither singles James out by name.

Objection — the texts speak of the family, not of James

Mark 3:21 is a statement about the family as a whole, not one that names James; John 7:5 is more sweeping still. James’s own unbelief is therefore a strong inference from this family tradition rather than a line that singles him out by name.

Reply

Granted — it is a strong inference from the family tradition rather than a named report. But it is a well-grounded inference, and it passes the criterion of embarrassment with ease: the early church revered James, so a memory of his earlier scepticism is the last thing it would have manufactured. Of the individuals the creed names, James is the one whose prior unbelief the Gospel tradition lets us infer.

The room: The unbelief of the family is the kind of detail New Testament historians assess by the criterion of embarrassment. That James existed, was Jesus’ brother, led the Jerusalem church, and died a violent death is close to consensus across the scholarly spectrum, from confessional historians like Richard Bauckham — who has written extensively on James and the relatives of Jesus — to sceptics who grant the same facts while disputing their cause. John Painter has devoted a full-length study to the figure, Just James. The live debate is not over whether James converted but over why.
Defending Premise 2

After the crucifixion James appears as leader of the Jerusalem church, dying for the faith — and the creed gives the cause: then he appeared to James. The persecutor Paul follows the same arc.

Precision check: what this establishes is that a sceptic became a martyr-leader, and that he and the earliest community attributed the change to an appearance of the risen Jesus. That James was sincerely and radically transformed is well supported. That the cause of his transformation was a literal resurrection is a further inference — the best explanation, we hold, of a conversion that grief, ambition, and slow persuasion all fail to capture — defended alongside the empty tomb, the disciples’ experiences, and the conversion of Paul, not a proof standing alone.

James the pillar — and his death Strong · one strand questioned

Paul meets James in Jerusalem (Galatians 1:19) and, on a later visit, names him a “pillar” (Galatians 2:9); Josephus — a non-Christian — records his execution by stoning c. AD 62. From dismissive brother to dying leader. Acts shows James presiding at the Jerusalem council and issuing its decision (Acts 15:13-21). On the Josephus notice (Antiquities 20.200), the overwhelming majority of scholars — including sceptics — regard the passage as authentic, in part because Josephus mentions James only incidentally, to explain why Ananus was deposed, with no Christian agenda to serve.

Honest limit: the vivid Hegesippus martyrdom is later and partly legendary — more legendary in its details and to be cited with care — and even the Josephus passage has been questioned by a minority who suspect the words “who was called Christ” of being a later gloss. The case for James should lean on the convergence, not on any single embellished narrative.

The cause on record Strong · the earliest explanation

The creed Paul recites in 1 Corinthians 15 — a formula most scholars date to within a few years of the crucifixion — states: “Then he appeared to James, then to all the apostles” (1 Corinthians 15:7). This is not a late legend about James; it is early tradition that singles him out by name. A community inventing appearances to lend its message authority had no motive to award one to the founder’s estranged, doubting brother; that it did is better explained by memory than by invention.

Honest limit: this names the cause the earliest tradition gave; that the cause was a literal resurrection is a further inference.

Why grief and trauma fit James badly Cumulative · not a proof

The bereavement-vision hypothesis is a real, documented phenomenon, and we should concede it plainly. But the documented cases are keyed to yearning — the ache of a bereaved person who longs to see the deceased restored. That is the variable James most conspicuously lacks: he is not on record as a devoted follower yearning for his brother’s return; his family is on record as having thought his brother out of his mind. Could James, despite his earlier scepticism, have been so shaken by the trauma of his brother’s public execution that he hallucinated him alive? Trauma and guilt can disturb perception. But it cuts against the grain of what we know: crucifixion was a shameful, covenant-cursed death (Deuteronomy 21:23; cf. Galatians 3:13). For a first-century Jew, a crucified messianic claimant was a refuted messianic claimant. Nothing about the manner of Jesus’ death would have inclined a sceptical brother toward sudden faith; it would have confirmed his doubts. Reginald Fuller — no partisan of the apologetic case — called the disciples’ Easter faith “one of the indisputable facts of history” and said of James that “if there were no record of an appearance to James the Lord’s brother in the New Testament we should have to invent one.”

Honest limit: that is a measure of how hard the conversion is to reduce to grief or wishful thinking; it is not an admission that the case is closed.

Paul: the second hostile witness Strong

Paul began as an active persecutor who, in his own words, “persecuted the church of God violently and tried to destroy it” (Galatians 1:13). James began as a domestic sceptic. The first had every ideological reason to disbelieve; the second had every familiar, this-is-just-my-brother reason. Together the two form a pincer: the outside enemy and the inside sceptic, converging on the same claim from opposite starting points, each immune to the explanation that fits the other.

Honest limit: Paul’s case is developed in its own essay (Why Did Paul Convert?); here he appears only as the second of two hostile witnesses, and the depth belongs to James.

Objection — James was simply talked round, slowly Hardest

Perhaps James was won over slowly — by the disciples’ preaching, by reflection on the scriptures, by the moral force of the early community — and the “appearance” of 1 Corinthians 15:7 is a later, stylized way of marking a gradual change of mind. This is the most serious of the naturalistic readings, because it does not require any anomalous experience at all.

Reply

It is worth saying plainly where the weight lies: for James in particular, the grief or bereavement-vision hypothesis is the weakest of the naturalistic options, precisely because the yearning it depends on is absent. The gradualist reading — and with it the late-interpretation handling of “then he appeared to James” — is the one a fair case must actually beat. Two considerations weigh against it. First, the early creed of 1 Corinthians 15:7 does not describe a process of persuasion; it names a discrete appearance, and it is among the earliest tradition we have, leaving little room for a slow legend to form. Second, gradual persuasion does not easily produce martyrs. People are talked into opinions and talked out of them; the willingness to go on proclaiming under lethal pressure, to the point of a violent death, is the mark of a conviction the holder regards as anchored in something he himself encountered, not merely argued into. The gradualist reading can explain a change of view; it strains to explain a death.

Objection — family loyalty and dynastic ambition

Perhaps James converted not from grief but from opportunity. His brother’s movement survived the crucifixion; James, as a leading member of Jesus’ family, stepped in to lead it — a kind of dynastic succession, with the resurrection claim as the family business. Some sceptical writers have floated versions of this “dynastic succession” reading.

Reply

The reply turns on timing and cost. The standing James came to hold was real — he became a “pillar” of the mother church — but it was standing inside a persecuted sect of a crucified criminal in early-30s Jerusalem, carrying the social shame of being kin to an executed blasphemer and, eventually, a death sentence; it was not the worldly power, wealth, or honour an ambitious man would seek. Dynastic ambition explains why a man might lead a winning movement, not why a sceptic would join a losing and dangerous one and hold to it under lethal pressure to the point of a violent death. And the dynastic reading must still account for the early creed’s specific claim — not that James inherited a role, but that the risen Jesus “appeared to James.” The succession theory explains the leadership while leaving the conversion, which is the thing to be explained, untouched.

The room: Habermas and Licona list the conversion of James and the conversion of Paul among the small set of data conceded by the great majority of critical scholars, precisely because each is multiply rooted and cuts against the grain. N. T. Wright treats the conversion of James, alongside Paul’s, as part of the data any historian of Christian origins must explain, not a pious flourish. Bart Ehrman accepts the Josephus notice. The naturalistic options are still defended by able critics.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: that James was gradually persuaded, and “he appeared to James” marks a slow change of mind. The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the replies below.

"Grief over a brother explains James" Common

Strongest form

A brother dies horribly; guilt and grief do their work; James joins the movement to honour him. Families convert to dead relatives’ causes all the time.

Reply

Grief makes people cherish a memory — not proclaim a cursed blasphemer to be the risen Lord of Israel, in defiance of the theology James held. Honouring a brother does not require dying by stoning for a claim you could quietly soften. And the explanation on record is not grief but encounter: he appeared to James.

"James took over the family business" Trickier

Strongest form

Dynastic succession: the founder dies, the brother inherits the movement — a pattern seen in many sects. Ambition, not appearance.

Reply

There was no business to inherit — a persecuted sect whose founder was freshly executed, offering its leader the best seat at the next stoning. Real dynasts soften the offensive claims to survive; James led a community staking everything on the most offensive claim possible. And succession explains leadership, not conversion — he first had to come to believe his brother was risen.

"Family unbelief was invented to fulfil prophecy" Hard

Strongest form

A prophet has no honour among his relatives — maybe the unbelief was written into the story to echo the proverb, not history.

Reply

The dependence runs the other way: the proverb is quoted because the painful fact was known. Inventing family rejection creates a permanent apologetic liability — his own brothers did not buy it — which opponents could exploit. The criterion of embarrassment exists for exactly this case, and it is reinforced by independent attestation in Mark and John.

"We know almost nothing about James — too thin to argue from" Trickier

Strongest form

A few scattered verses and a disputed Josephus passage. You are building a pillar on a footnote.

Reply

The data points are few but each is well grounded: embarrassing unbelief in two independent Gospels; leadership attested first-hand by Paul, who met him; death attested by Josephus, whose James passage — unlike the longer one — is accepted as authentic by the overwhelming majority of scholars, sceptics included — a minority do question the phrase “who was called Christ,” which is why the case leans on the convergence rather than on Josephus alone. Thin sources with high credibility beat thick sources with low.

"Paul and James just legitimise each other — circular" Hard

Strongest form

The creed naming James comes via Paul; Paul’s status was endorsed by James. Two interested parties vouching for each other prove nothing.

Reply

Galatians shows the opposite of a mutual-admiration pact — Paul confronting Jerusalem, tension over the Gentile mission, independence fiercely asserted. Rivals who agree on a fact are stronger evidence than allies who do: on the appearances, the persecutor and the brother — converted separately, by different events, before they ever met — converge.

"Two converts is a rounding error, not evidence" Common

Strongest form

Billions of conversions across history, in every direction, for every cause. Two more first-century converts move nothing.

Reply

Conversions in general are common; this profile is not: contemporaries of the events, hostile at the time, in a position to check the claims personally — one could interrogate the family, the other was investigating the movement to destroy it — each named as a recipient of an appearance (1 Corinthians 15:7-8), each holding to the claim to a violent death (James per Josephus; Paul by strong early tradition). Evidence weighs by quality of witness, and these are the two best-positioned hostile witnesses in the entire question.

"James was simply talked round, slowly" Hard

Strongest form

Perhaps James was won over slowly — by the disciples’ preaching, by reflection on the scriptures, by the moral force of the early community — and the “appearance” of 1 Corinthians 15:7 is a later, stylized way of marking a gradual change of mind. This is the most serious of the naturalistic readings, because it does not require any anomalous experience at all.

Reply

Two considerations weigh against it. First, the early creed of 1 Corinthians 15:7 does not describe a process of persuasion; it names a discrete appearance, and it is among the earliest tradition we have, leaving little room for a slow legend to form. Second, gradual persuasion does not easily produce martyrs. People are talked into opinions and talked out of them; the willingness to go on proclaiming under lethal pressure, to the point of a violent death, is the mark of a conviction the holder regards as anchored in something he himself encountered, not merely argued into. The gradualist reading can explain a change of view; it strains to explain a death.

The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

The bare facts are not seriously in dispute. That James existed, was Jesus’ brother, led the Jerusalem church, and died a violent death is close to consensus across the scholarly spectrum, from confessional historians to sceptics who grant the same facts while disputing their cause. The live debate is not over whether James converted but over why.

A fairness most defenders skip: The naturalistic options are still defended by able critics, even as their proponents must concede how poorly James fits the grief profile their best theory relies on. The bereavement-vision phenomenon is real and documented, and we should concede it plainly — but the documented cases are keyed to yearning, the variable James most conspicuously lacks. And the sources are real but uneven: the Gospel data on his unbelief and the Pauline data on his leadership are early and strong, and the Josephan notice of his death is solid; but the Hegesippus martyrdom is later and partly legendary, and even the Josephus passage, while accepted by most, has been questioned by a minority who suspect the words “who was called Christ” of being a later gloss.

The bounded verdict: The honest claim is modest and strong at once: the conversion of James is a fact the naturalistic theories handle poorly, and the cumulative case is the better for it. It does not, on its own, prove that Jesus rose; it is one premise of the case, not the whole of it. And this is what we hold to be true: Jesus of Nazareth was bodily raised from the dead, and the wider case shows it.
