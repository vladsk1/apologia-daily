What happened after Jesus was crucified? Andrew Loke sets out what he argues is a complete list of the kinds of explanation for the early Christians’ claim that Jesus appeared to them alive, and for what became of his body — and then examines each rival in turn, starting from the early witness list Paul passes on in 1 Corinthians 15, drawing on history, psychology, memory science, comparative religion, philosophy and theology. His conclusion is that the resurrection is the best explanation — the one left standing — and that calling it a historical certainty is defensible. This club walks the book chapter by chapter, then helps you talk it over, carefully and kindly.

About this study

The sessions follow the book’s own structure — one for each chapter, all nine in order, under the book’s own chapter titles. Dr Loke looked over a preview of this study and gave his permission for it to be published.

We try never to state the book’s claims more strongly than it does, and we keep its hedges and concessions. In places the book goes further than our summary (for instance, it also puts a probability figure on its conclusion); we report the argument and leave the arithmetic to the book. Where Dr Loke hedges (claiming something of at least some of the witnesses, perhaps all), we keep the hedge; where he concedes a point to a critic, we say so; and where he claims a historian’s kind of certainty — not a mathematical proof — while saying his argument does not need even that, we say that too.

Can you walk someone through the case — kindly?

One capstone for the whole book — twelve prompts that walk through the entire case, in two modes. Flashcards: read a prompt, answer it in your head (or out loud to the group), then tap to reveal a model answer — the book’s argument, put in our own words. Self-rate quiz: take the same twelve prompts one at a time, rate how well you answered each, and get an honest score at the end. If you can walk through them graciously, you are better prepared to have the conversation.

01 · The method
How does Loke’s argument work?
Tap to reveal →
By elimination over an exhaustive list. He sorts every possible explanation into categories — for the appearance claims, six naturalistic categories (seven counting the resurrection); for the body, eight (nine counting the resurrection) — then argues that each naturalistic category can reasonably be excluded. Because, he argues, the list covers every kind of answer, a “new” theory has to fall into one of these categories — or a combination of them, which he examines in chapter 7.
02 · The steps
What are the steps from the evidence to “Jesus rose”?
Tap to reveal →
(I) People claimed to have seen the risen Jesus shortly after his crucifixion. (II) They had some kind of experiences. (III) What they experienced was caused by something outside their minds. (IV) That something was the same Jesus who died on the cross. (V) Therefore Jesus rose. On Loke’s account, establishing steps I–IV needs no appeal to the supernatural. Whether it was God who raised him — which Loke argues in chapter 8 — is a further philosophical and theological question; on his view, history can establish the event while leaving its cause to philosophy and theology.
03 · The claims
Why does Loke think the witness list in 1 Corinthians 15 is not a later legend?
Tap to reveal →
It is a tradition Paul says he received and passed on — a very early one, Loke argues — in a letter written around AD 55, and every surviving manuscript contains it. Paul knew Cephas (Peter) and James, and the Corinthians knew of them too; he says most of the five hundred were still living; and some in Corinth openly doubted that the dead are raised. A false list, Loke argues, would have been easy to expose and costly to Paul.
04 · Sincerity
What does Loke think the disciples’ suffering shows?
Tap to reveal →
Not that they were right — Loke grants that people of many faiths suffer for what they believe. It shows, he argues, that at least some of them, perhaps all, were sincere: they were willing to suffer, and a group of people would not willingly suffer and die for what they knew was false. What sets them apart is that they were in a position to know whether they were telling the truth about what they saw — and together with their willingness to suffer, Loke argues, that rules out deliberate invention. Whether a sincere experience could still have been mistaken is the question the next chapters take up. (He notes that how many apostles were actually martyred is debated; his point rests on willingness, not on how many died.)
05 · Only in the mind?
What is the “intramental” hypothesis, and what is Loke’s main argument against it for the appearances to groups?
Tap to reveal →
It is the view that all the experiences of Jesus were caused within the witnesses’ own minds, for example by hallucination. Loke argues that a hallucination has no matching outside stimulus, so without one the members of a group would not agree on the details of what they perceived. He therefore judges it unreasonable to think that three different groups, in different circumstances over a short period, all had such experiences with nothing there. Theories that combine hallucination with other explanations are left for chapter 7.
06 · Was it Jesus, and had he died?
Why does Loke think the witnesses did not mistake someone else for Jesus — and that Jesus had really died?
Tap to reveal →
Loke grants that studies show eyewitnesses can misidentify people, but argues those cases are not parallel. He argues that the disciples knew Jesus too well to mistake someone else for him, across three groups and different settings, and that no impostor could keep up an imitation of Jesus’ behaviour. On the swoon idea, he judges survival unlikely given Roman flogging and crucifixion, while noting Josephus’s report that, of three men taken down and treated, one lived and two still died. Even had Jesus survived, he argues, a half-dead man would have convinced the disciples only that he needed medical help, not that he was the risen Lord. On the naturalistic escape idea (someone else crucified in his place), he thinks it unlikely that enemies and close followers alike missed it at a public execution. Miraculous versions he leaves to chapter 8.
07 · The body
What does Loke conclude about what happened to Jesus’ body — and how much rests on the guard at the tomb?
Tap to reveal →
Loke lists every possible outcome for the body. The escape and swoon theories were treated earlier, so chapter 6 tests six: Jesus was never buried; the body stayed buried; or it was removed by friends, by enemies, by a neutral party such as tomb robbers, or by a non-agent such as an earthquake. He concludes that no naturalistic hypothesis reasonably accounts for what happened to the body. A great deal rests on the guard: he says the guard’s presence, together with other considerations, would rule out all the naturalistic hypotheses about the body — and he replies to the many critical scholars who think Matthew invented the story.
08 · Combinations
What is a “combination hypothesis”, and why does Loke argue that combining natural explanations does not rescue them?
Tap to reveal →
A combination hypothesis joins several natural explanations so that each covers another’s weak point — for example, an unburied body, a few visions, and later embellishment. Loke argues that four of the building blocks (legend, no experience, swoon and escape) are dead ends that add nothing to any mixture. The strongest blend that he thinks is still open to a sceptic (hallucinations for the Twelve and some individuals, mistaken identity for others) nonetheless, he argues, leaves the empty tomb unexplained, rests on unevidenced assumptions, needs an unworkable group hallucination of the Twelve, and would not have survived checking by witnesses and hearers.
09 · Memory
How does Loke answer the objection that memories distort and stories change in the retelling, like a “telephone game”?
Tap to reveal →
He does not claim memory is flawless: he grants, with Redman, that even vivid memories of shocking events can fade, and notes that Ehrman, whose own book argues that memories of Jesus were distorted in the retelling, also grants that most of the time we remember fairly well. Following other scholars, he argues the telephone game is a poor comparison, because the message was retold around a net of communities rather than down a single chain. He adds that it was shocking and a matter of life and death, that living eyewitnesses could be consulted, and that critics could challenge mistakes.
10 · Miracles and rarity
Resurrections are, at the very least, extremely rare. Why does Loke say that rarity does not make the resurrection of Jesus improbable?
Tap to reveal →
Loke argues that what matters is the reason an event is rare. Natural causes act in regular, law-like ways, so if something never happens naturally, that counts against a natural explanation. A miracle, however, is supposed to be the act of a personal agent, who may freely choose to act in a unique way on a special occasion. So rarity, he argues, is not an adequate reason against the resurrection. One has to weigh the specific evidence, whether the alternatives can be excluded, and the religious context.
11 · Who raised him?
Suppose the historical case shows that Jesus rose. How does Loke get from there to “God raised him”?
Tap to reveal →
Loke argues that a natural fluke is wildly improbable given what we know of nature, and contrived, since it would have to coincide by chance with Jesus’ claim to be divine. Even an unknown natural mechanism, he adds, would need a supernatural agent to know it and use it at just that moment in the first century. Other suggested agents he calls ad hoc, because there is no independent reason to think they could raise the dead. He holds that there are independent reasons, argued elsewhere, for a Creator who could. Given those reasons, historical evidence for the resurrection and evidence that Jesus claimed to be divine (set out in an earlier book) would warrant concluding that God raised him.
12 · The takeaway
A sceptical friend gives you sixty seconds. How does Loke’s closing chapter sum up the whole case?
Tap to reveal →
Loke starts from three points he says are widely agreed among scholars, citing Habermas: Jesus died by crucifixion, people soon had experiences they took to be appearances of him risen, and the body was missing — the point he argues at length in chapter 6. He argues that the evidence indicates the tomb was empty; that people claimed to see Jesus alive and really saw something; that its cause lay outside their minds; and that it was the same Jesus. No naturalistic account, he argues, reasonably explains this. So, he concludes, Jesus rose; and since (he argues elsewhere) there are reasons for a Creator and Jesus claimed to be divine, he sees good reasons for thinking the best explanation is that God raised him.
