Did Paul Invent Jesus's Divinity?

The deep dive explains the case. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild from memory why Paul received the divine confession rather than inventing it, meet the "Paul made it all up" objection, and hold the answer together in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

It is one of the most repeated moves in the popular case against Christianity: that the divine Christ is not the Jesus who taught in Galilee but a figure the apostle Paul built — the religion about Jesus that Paul founded, not the religion of Jesus. The answer the evidence returns is no, and it is Paul himself, more than anyone, who rules it out.

Paul tells his readers he received the core confession rather than composing it: "I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received" (1 Corinthians 15:3), a creed datable across the scholarly spectrum to within a few years of the crucifixion. He never argues that Christ is divine; he everywhere presupposes it, even quoting an early hymn (Philippians 2) and an Aramaic prayer that reaches back to the first Aramaic-speaking Judean church (Marana tha, "Our Lord, come!"). And he applies Yahweh's own exclusive texts to Jesus — Isaiah 45's "every knee shall bow" in Philippians 2, Joel's "call on the name of the LORD" in Romans 10 — including Jesus within the identity of the one God of Israel, not importing a second god. Above all, he had been the confession's violent persecutor (Galatians 1:13): you do not invent, at ruinous personal cost, the very belief you had been trying to destroy.

Honest scope: this does not by itself prove the confession true — only that it is early and received, not invented. Whether it is correct is the separate work of the resurrection evidence and of Jesus's own words. And the developed creedal vocabulary (Nicaea's "one essence," Chalcedon's "two natures") did come later — not the deity itself, which is present from the earliest layer we can reach. What this argument establishes is modest and firm: the divine confession stands behind Paul, not with him. And this is what we hold to be true: the one Paul confessed as Lord is the eternal Son, fully God and fully man. This argument does not prove that; it clears the charge against it — the positive case rests on the resurrection and on Jesus's own words.

Received, not invented — Paul is a witness to the divine confession, not its author.

Why "Paul invented it" is the popular skeptic's move.

The claim has a serious scholarly ancestor — the history-of-religions school (Bousset), which derived the divine Kyrios from pagan mystery cults — and a popular descendant in Bart Ehrman's developmental thesis — an early "exaltation" Christology, though he also finds a pre-existent Christ already in material older than Paul; what developed, on his view, was the divine figure's rank, not the date of pre-existence. In the popular retelling that draws on both, Paul stands near the head of the trajectory. The reply concedes the facts (Paul is the earliest writer; he never walked with the earthly Jesus) and refuses only the inference.

Defending Premise 1

Paul received the core confession rather than composing it — “I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received” (1 Corinthians 15:3), the technical idiom for handing on tradition, and a creed datable to within a few years of the crucifixion. He never argues the divinity; he everywhere presupposes it, even quoting an early hymn (Philippians 2) and the Aramaic Marana tha from the first Judean church.

Precision check: grant what is true in the opposing view — a fair reply concedes the facts and refuses only the inference. The oldest datable Christian documents we possess are the letters of Paul, generally dated to the 50s of the first century — earlier than any of the Gospels. Paul did not walk with the earthly Jesus. And his Christology really is very high. None of that is in dispute. The question is whether Paul originated that Christology, or inherited and expressed it. This premise does not, by itself, prove that the confession Paul received is true; that Paul handed on the belief that Jesus is the risen Lord establishes that the claim was made — at the movement’s foundation, by a formerly hostile witness — not that the claim is correct.

“Delivered” and “received” — the technical idiom Paul’s own statement

Around the year 55, in a letter to a quarrelsome church in Corinth, Paul paused to remind his readers of something he insists he did not invent. “For I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received,” he writes, “that Christ died for our sins in accordance with the Scriptures, that he was buried, that he was raised on the third day in accordance with the Scriptures, and that he appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve” (1 Corinthians 15:3–5). The verbs are technical. Paul “delivered” (Greek paredōka) what he “received” (parelabon) — the precise idiom used in Judaism for the transmission of authoritative tradition, the equivalent of the rabbinic masar and qibbel. He is not narrating his own experience; he is handing on something handed to him.

The tradition is old — within about two to five years of the cross Held across the spectrum

Reckoning backward from the fixed points of Paul’s biography — his conversion within a few years of the crucifixion, his visit to Cephas and James in Jerusalem — the formula must predate Paul’s own receipt of it, which pushes its origin into the first half of the 30s, within roughly two to five years of the crucifixion. This is not an apologetic estimate; it is held across the spectrum. The atheist New Testament scholar Gerd Lüdemann judged that “the elements in the tradition are to be dated to the first two years after the crucifixion… not later than three years”; the critical historian James Dunn writes that the formula “was formulated as tradition within months of Jesus’ death.” As N. T. Wright argues, this primitive tradition of the mid-30s reaches behind all our written Gospels to the foundation of the church’s faith.

Honest limit: The full case for the date is laid out in the companion essay on the early creed of 1 Corinthians 15. What matters here is the posture: the confession Paul carries is one he tells us he inherited from those who were witnesses before him.

He assumes the divinity — he never argues it Presupposed, not argued

The received creed of 1 Corinthians 15 is about the resurrection. Turn to the divinity itself, and the same pattern holds, more tellingly still. Across a decade and more of letters written to scattered churches to settle other questions — a collection here, a quarrel there, a word of comfort — Paul never sets out to prove that Christ is divine. He everywhere assumes it, and a thing presupposed offhandedly by a writer making some other point was not up for debate. In Colossians he can call Christ “the image of the invisible God, the firstborn of all creation” — firstborn over creation, not first item within it — the one by whom “all things were created,” and say flatly that “in him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily” — not as a thesis to be defended but as common ground from which to correct a local error. This is Gordon Fee’s central exegetical observation about Paul: the startlingly high Christology is everywhere presupposed, never argued.

Honest limit: Colossians’ own authorship is debated; but the same presupposing-not-arguing pattern runs through the undisputed letters — Philippians, 1 Corinthians, Romans — so the observation does not stand or fall with it.

Behind the letters — the Philippians hymn and Marana tha Bedrock beneath him

In Philippians Paul quotes what most scholars regard as a pre-Pauline hymn — material older than the letter itself — describing one who was “in the form of God” and did not count “equality with God” a thing to be grasped, and who took “the form of a servant, being born in the likeness of men,” and is finally given “the name that is above every name.” Deeper still, Paul preserves an Aramaic prayer left untranslated in his Greek-speaking churches: Marana tha, “Our Lord, come!” That the invocation survives in Aramaic is the point. It carries us back behind Paul’s Greek to the Aramaic-speaking Judean church — the very first community, already calling on the risen Jesus as the Lord who will come. The highest devotion is not a late overlay Paul introduced; it is bedrock beneath him.

Honest limit: The pre-Pauline dating is itself debated — Gordon Fee argues Paul composed the hymn — but the point stands either way: composed or quoted, the conviction it expresses is not one Paul argues into being.

Objection — the developmental thesis: Paul as the engine of the change Hardest

The divine Christ, it is said, is not the Jesus who taught in Galilee but a figure the apostle Paul built. A century ago the history-of-religions school argued that a “high” view of Jesus — pre-existent, divine, hailed as Kyrios (“Lord”) — could not have arisen among monotheistic Jews in Palestine; it must have drifted upward later. Its popular descendant today is the developmental thesis of Bart Ehrman, who argues that the earliest followers held an “exaltation” Christology — God raised the man Jesus and adopted him to divine status — yet who also finds a pre-existent Christ already in material older than Paul, a figure he takes to have been conceived at first as a subordinate divine being — God’s chief angel, or a divinity of a lesser order — whose elevation to the co-equal God confessed at Nicaea in 325 is the later step, reaching its New Testament height in the latest Gospel, John. On this view what developed was the divine figure’s rank, not the date of pre-existence itself. In the popular retelling that draws on both, Paul stands near the head of the trajectory — the earliest and most influential engine of the change.

Reply

What the evidence closes off is the specific charge that Paul invented the divinity. The disagreement is not really about dates — Ehrman himself finds a pre-existent Christ in material older than Paul’s letters — but about what kind of being the earliest Christians took Jesus to be. Bauckham sharpens the point: the earliest Christians did not slowly climb a ladder from a low Christology to a high one; they expressed the highest possible claim — inclusion in the divine identity — from the start, using the very resources of Jewish monotheism. It is a widely influential framing, though the divine-identity category is itself debated (Larry Hurtado read the same data as a pattern of devotion; James F. McGrath disputes the category). The words for it were refined over centuries; the reality is bedrock, and it is there in the earliest layer we can reach. Paul stands inside that layer, not above it.

Concession: The developed creedal vocabulary came later: the fourth- and fifth-century language of “one essence” and “two natures” is a real achievement, the church’s articulation of what was already in the texts, not a first-century phrase. Not saying: not that the deity of Christ was a later human invention.

Objection — “Paul says his gospel was not man’s gospel” (Galatians 1:11–12)

Paul himself insists the gospel he preached was “not man’s gospel” but came “through a revelation of Jesus Christ” (Galatians 1:11–12) — the text the inventor thesis leans on hardest.

Reply

What Paul says came by revelation is his encounter with the risen Son and his commission to the Gentiles (Galatians 1:15–16), not a creed of his own devising: the confession itself he says he “received” (1 Corinthians 15:3), and the message he preached was set before the leading men of the Jerusalem church, those who had known Jesus, and was endorsed by them rather than corrected (Galatians 2:2, 6, 9).

The room: Lüdemann (an atheist New Testament scholar, cited for the dating only) and Dunn (a mainstream critical historian, not an apologist) both date the formula to within months or a few years of the crucifixion; N. T. Wright places it in the mid-30s. Gordon Fee, cited on the exegesis of Paul’s Greek only, observes that Paul presupposes rather than argues; he also argues Paul composed the Philippians hymn. Bart Ehrman presses the developmental thesis, while himself finding a pre-existent Christ in material older than Paul’s letters.
Defending Premise 2

When Paul describes the exalted Jesus he applies Yahweh’s own exclusive texts to him (Isaiah 45 in Philippians 2; Joel 2:32 in Romans 10) — including Jesus within the identity of the one God, not importing a second god from paganism. And he had been the confession’s persecutor (Galatians 1:13): you do not invent, at ruinous cost, the belief you were destroying.

Precision check: Paul does not reach outward to the mystery cults; he reaches into the Old Testament’s most jealously monotheistic texts. This premise is a claim about what the evidence closes off — the specific charge that Paul invented the divinity — not a proof that the confession is true: whether it is correct is the work of the resurrection evidence and of Jesus’s own words, argued elsewhere in this Library.

Yahweh’s own words — Isaiah 45 and Joel 2:32 The direction of borrowing

When Paul reaches for language to describe the exalted Jesus, he does not reach outward to the mystery cults; he reaches into the Old Testament’s most jealously monotheistic texts — and applies to Jesus the words reserved for Yahweh alone. The climax of the Philippians hymn, “every knee should bow… every tongue confess,” is lifted from Isaiah 45, where the LORD swears that to him alone every knee shall bow — one of the most fiercely monotheistic passages in all of Scripture — and Paul applies it to Jesus. In Romans 10, having written that “if you confess with your mouth that Jesus is Lord… you will be saved,” he clinches the point by quoting Joel — “everyone who calls on the name of the Lord will be saved” — a passage in which the Lord one calls upon is Yahweh himself.

Inside the identity of the one God of Israel Widely influential · category debated

This is what the British scholar Richard Bauckham calls including Jesus within the unique identity of the one God of Israel: Jesus is placed on the Creator’s side of the line that Jewish monotheism drew between God and everything else.

Honest limit: It is a widely influential framing, though the divine-identity category is itself debated (Larry Hurtado read the same data as a pattern of devotion; James F. McGrath disputes the category).

1 Corinthians 8:6 — Israel’s confession, its two divine titles divided Influential · contested

In 1 Corinthians 8:6 Paul takes Israel’s own confession that the LORD is one and divides its two divine titles, so that the “one God” is the Father and the “one Lord” is Jesus Christ, “through whom are all things.” Read as folded into the creed, it is not one more step up a ladder. Wisdom is praised; Enoch is exalted; but no Jewish writer we know of wrote a second figure into the creed of divine uniqueness itself (Philo’s ‘second god,’ the Logos, is the nearest case, and it is never placed inside that confession). Paul did. And whatever room Judaism had for exalted intermediaries, the angels among them characteristically refuse worship and redirect it to God, while the earliest Christians were praying to Jesus and singing hymns about him within the first two decades.

Honest limit: That reading of the verse is influential, arguably dominant, and genuinely contested: James F. McGrath argues that “one Lord” is set alongside the creed rather than folded into it.

The timeline is too fast Hengel’s argument

As Martin Hengel argued, more happened in Christology in the less than two decades between the crucifixion and Paul’s letters than in the seven centuries of doctrinal development that followed. The timeline is too fast, and the vocabulary too Jewish, for a slow drift out of paganism.

Exhibit A is Paul himself The persecutor

There is one witness the inventor thesis has to explain, and it is the supposed inventor. Before he was the apostle Paul, he was Saul of Tarsus, and he was the church’s enemy. “I persecuted the church of God violently and tried to destroy it,” he reminds the Galatians; to the Philippians he lists his credentials with a kind of bitter pride: “as to zeal, a persecutor of the church” (Galatians 1:13; Philippians 3:6). This is not a hostile source describing Paul; it is Paul describing himself, in letters whose authenticity virtually all critical scholars accept. And Saul was no confused bystander. He grasped the claim that a crucified man was Israel’s Messiah, and he found it a scandal — for a hanged man was under God’s curse (Deuteronomy 21:23), and a crucified Messiah was a contradiction in terms to a first-century Pharisee.

Then something reversed him. A movement’s enemies do not usually defect to it, at ruinous personal cost, on the strength of nothing. And the reversal is fatal to the thesis that Paul is the inventor, because it puts the causation backwards. You do not invent, at the cost of your standing and your safety and your inherited convictions, the very belief you had been trying to stamp out — a belief whose divine shape, as the Aramaic Marana tha and the hymn behind Philippians show, already reached back behind him. Whatever happened on the Damascus road, Paul did not build the confession of the risen, divine Christ. He joined it — and three years later stayed fifteen days with Cephas, the first name in the creed (Galatians 1:18).

Objection — “Kyrios is borrowed from the pagan cults”

A “high” view of Jesus — pre-existent, divine, hailed as Kyrios (“Lord”) — could not have arisen among monotheistic Jews in Palestine; it must have drifted upward later, as the church moved out into the Greek world and absorbed the vocabulary of the pagan cults that hailed their gods as Kyrios. (The classic statement is Wilhelm Bousset’s.)

Reply

Here the derivation-from-paganism thesis meets its hardest problem, and it is a problem of direction: a man importing a second god from the mystery religions does not reach for the chapter of Isaiah that most insists there is no god beside the LORD. The matrix is Jewish monotheism, not Hellenistic syncretism — which is why the older derivation-from-paganism model has largely collapsed under closer study.

The room: Richard Bauckham (divine identity) is widely influential, though Larry Hurtado reads the same data as a pattern of devotion and James F. McGrath disputes the category and reads 1 Corinthians 8:6 as setting “one Lord” alongside the creed. Martin Hengel supplies the timeline argument; Wilhelm Bousset is the classic statement of the history-of-religions derivation, and Bart Ehrman its popular descendant in the developmental thesis. Paul did not invent Jesus’s divinity. He is the single most important early witness to it, but a witness is precisely what he is.
The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: the developmental thesis — that Paul, our earliest and most influential writer, is the engine that turned a Galilean preacher into a divine Lord (the popular retelling that draws on Ehrman and the older history-of-religions school). The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the reply below.

"Paul is the earliest writer — so he's the one who invented the divine Jesus" Hard

Strongest form

Paul's letters are the oldest Christian documents, decades before the Gospels, and his Christology is sky-high. The simplest reading is that a rising belief in Jesus's divinity crested in Paul — he is the inventor, or at least the engine (the popular retelling of the developmental thesis).

Reply

Grant the facts and refuse the inference. Paul is the earliest writer — but he tells us he is quoting, not composing: he "received" the confession (1 Corinthians 15:3), and the material he quotes reaches behind him — the Aramaic Marana tha, a prayer of the first Judean church, and the Philippians hymn (composed or quoted, it is not argued into being). The earliest reachable layer already presupposes, on Bauckham's divine-identity reading (widely influential, though the category is itself debated — Hurtado read the same data as a pattern of devotion; James F. McGrath disputes the category), a Christ included in God's identity and worship. The disagreement is not really about dates — Ehrman himself finds a pre-existent Christ in material older than Paul's letters — but about what kind of being the earliest Christians took Jesus to be. And whatever room Judaism had for exalted intermediaries, the angels among them characteristically refuse worship and redirect it to God, while the earliest Christians were praying to Jesus and singing hymns about him within the first two decades.

"The divine 'Lord' was borrowed from pagan mystery religions" Common

Strongest form

Calling Jesus Kyrios ("Lord") and worshipping him looks like the dying-and-rising lords of the Hellenistic mystery cults. Paul, at home in the Greek world, imported that pattern into a Jewish sect (Bousset, Kyrios Christos).

Reply

The direction is wrong. When Paul describes the exalted Jesus he reaches into the Old Testament's most fiercely monotheistic texts — Isaiah 45, Joel 2:32 — and applies to Jesus the words reserved for Yahweh alone. A monotheist importing a pagan god does not reach for the chapter that most insists there is no god beside the LORD. The matrix is Jewish monotheism, not paganism — which is why the derivation-from-paganism model has largely collapsed (Hengel).

"The creed Paul 'received' is about the resurrection, not the divinity" Trickier

Strongest form

1 Corinthians 15:3–5 confesses that Christ died, was buried, and was raised — it says nothing about him being divine. So "Paul received it" establishes an early resurrection belief, not an early divine confession. The deity could still be Paul's own addition.

Reply

Fair — and the argument does not rest the divinity on that creed. The received creed shows Paul's posture (he quotes rather than invents) and that the movement's core is pre-Pauline. The divine content is carried separately, and it is also pre-Pauline: the Aramaic Marana tha (prayer to the risen Lord, from the first Judean church) and the Philippians hymn, plus Paul's habit of applying Yahweh's texts to Jesus. The deity is early on independent grounds.

"Your best 'fullness of deity' verse is from Colossians — a disputed letter" Trickier

Strongest form

"In him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily" is your clearest presupposed-deity text, but Colossians's Pauline authorship is contested — so it may be later and deutero-Pauline, which is exactly the developmental point.

Reply

Concede the authorship debate; the case does not stand or fall on Colossians. The same presuppose-not-argue pattern runs through the undisputed letters — Philippians (the hymn), 1 Corinthians (the reshaped Shema of 8:6 — influential, arguably dominant, and genuinely contested (McGrath); Marana tha), Romans (the Joel citation). Remove Colossians and the divine confession is still presupposed across the letters no one disputes Paul wrote.

"Even if Paul received it, that doesn't make it true" Hard

Strongest form

Suppose the divine confession really is pre-Pauline. All that shows is that people believed it early — and early belief can still be error. You have not shown Jesus is actually divine, only that the claim was made soon.

Reply

Correct, and the argument says so plainly. It establishes that Paul received rather than invented the confession — that it is early and pre-Pauline — not that it is true. Whether it is true is a separate question, answered on separate grounds: the resurrection evidence and Jesus's own claims, each treated in its own track. What this argument closes off is only the specific charge that Paul was the inventor.

"The 'pre-Pauline hymn' is speculative — maybe Paul wrote it" Trickier

Strongest form

You lean on the Philippians hymn being older than Paul's letters. But the pre-Pauline dating is a scholarly reconstruction, and Gordon Fee argues Paul composed it himself — in which case it is not evidence of anything earlier.

Reply

Granted, and the argument is built to survive it. Whether Paul quoted the hymn or composed it, the point holds: composed or received, the exalted-Christ conviction it expresses is not one Paul argues into being — he assumes it. And the load-bearing pre-Pauline evidence is the Aramaic Marana tha, which carries us back behind Paul's Greek to the Aramaic-speaking Judean church — not the hymn's disputed dating.

The honest state of the debate

Where the argument really stands — and a fairness most defenders skip

The facts the inventor thesis starts from are not in dispute: the oldest datable Christian documents we possess are the letters of Paul, generally dated to the 50s — earlier than any of the Gospels; Paul did not walk with the earthly Jesus; and his Christology really is very high. The question is whether Paul originated that Christology, or inherited and expressed it. Several supports are debated and are carried with their limits: the pre-Pauline dating of the Philippians hymn (Gordon Fee argues Paul composed it), the authorship of Colossians, the divine-identity category (Hurtado read the same data as a pattern of devotion; James F. McGrath disputes it), and the reading of 1 Corinthians 8:6, which is influential and genuinely contested.

A fairness most defenders skip: The disagreement with the developmental thesis is not really about dates — Ehrman himself finds a pre-existent Christ in material older than Paul’s letters — but about what kind of being the earliest Christians took Jesus to be: on his view what developed was the divine figure’s rank, not the date of pre-existence itself. And the dating witnesses are cited for the dating only: Lüdemann affirms the early date while rejecting a bodily resurrection; Dunn wrote as a mainstream critical historian, not an apologist.

The bounded verdict: So the honest answer to the question is no. Paul did not invent Jesus’s divinity. He is the single most important early witness to it — but a witness is precisely what he is: a man who received a confession, assumed it rather than argued it, clothed it in Israel’s own language for God, and embraced it only after it had overturned everything he believed. And this is what we hold to be true: the one Paul confessed as Lord is the eternal Son, fully God and fully man. This argument does not prove that; it clears the charge against it — the positive case rests on the resurrection and on Jesus’s own words.

    One you weren’t coached on

    RS
    Religious studies student
    "Those other Pauline lines don’t make Jesus God. “Sin against Christ,” “test Christ,” a prayer-wish addressed to the Father and Jesus together — on the agent reading, that is simply the language of God’s agent, the one who acts for God, not God himself."
    How the argument answers it — ported from the deep dive

    On its own, “sin against Christ” (1 Corinthians 8:12) proves little — but it belongs to a pattern. Two chapters later, retelling Israel’s rebellion in the wilderness, Paul warns the Corinthians not to “test Christ, as some of them did” (10:9; the earliest manuscript reads “Christ,” some read “the Lord”), and asks, “Are we trying to provoke the Lord to jealousy?” (10:22) — the Lord whose cup and table they share (10:21), in the language of Deuteronomy 32, where it is the LORD whom Israel provokes to jealousy with foreign gods. And in his earliest surviving letter he asks the Father and Jesus together, with a single verb, to act: “May our God and Father Himself, and our Lord Jesus, direct our way to you” (1 Thessalonians 3:11). On the agent reading these would be the language of God’s agent; but the jealousy Israel provoked was the LORD’s alone, and Jewish piety characteristically did not address prayer, or prayer-wishes like this one, to an agent. And whatever room Judaism had for exalted intermediaries, the angels among them characteristically refuse worship and redirect it to God, while the earliest Christians were praying to Jesus and singing hymns about him within the first two decades. So these lines do not fit the agent reading: as Bauckham puts it, the earliest Christians expressed the highest possible claim — inclusion in the divine identity — from the start, using the very resources of Jewish monotheism. The words for it were refined over centuries; the reality is bedrock, and it is there in the earliest layer we can reach. Paul stands inside that layer, not above it.
