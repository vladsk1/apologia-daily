Did Paul Invent Jesus's Divinity?

The deep dive explains the case. This track makes you able to wield it — rebuild from memory why Paul received the divine confession rather than inventing it, meet the "Paul made it all up" objection, and hold the answer together in a real conversation.

The argument in brief

It is one of the most repeated moves in the popular case against Christianity: that the divine Christ is not the Jesus who taught in Galilee but a figure the apostle Paul built — the religion about Jesus that Paul founded, not the religion of Jesus. The answer the evidence returns is no, and it is Paul himself, more than anyone, who rules it out.

Paul tells his readers he received the core confession rather than composing it: "I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received" (1 Corinthians 15:3), a creed datable across the scholarly spectrum to within a few years of the crucifixion. He never argues that Christ is divine; he everywhere presupposes it, even quoting an early hymn (Philippians 2) and an Aramaic prayer that reaches back to the first Aramaic-speaking Judean church (Marana tha, "Our Lord, come!"). And he applies Yahweh's own exclusive texts to Jesus — Isaiah 45's "every knee shall bow" in Philippians 2, Joel's "call on the name of the LORD" in Romans 10 — including Jesus within the identity of the one God of Israel, not importing a second god. Above all, he had been the confession's violent persecutor (Galatians 1:13): you do not invent, at ruinous personal cost, the very belief you had been trying to destroy.

Honest scope: this does not by itself prove the confession true — only that it is early and received, not invented. Whether it is correct is the separate work of the resurrection evidence and of Jesus's own words. And the developed creedal vocabulary (Nicaea's "one essence," Chalcedon's "two natures") did come later — not the deity itself, which is present from the earliest layer we can reach. What this argument establishes is modest and immovable: the divine confession stands behind Paul, not with him.

The argument, formally

Received, not invented — Paul is a witness to the divine confession, not its author.

P1

Paul received the core confession rather than composing it — "I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received" (1 Corinthians 15:3), the technical idiom for handing on tradition, and a creed datable to within a few years of the crucifixion. He never argues the divinity; he everywhere presupposes it, even quoting an early hymn (Philippians 2) and the Aramaic Marana tha from the first Judean church.

P2

When Paul describes the exalted Jesus he applies Yahweh's own exclusive texts to him (Isaiah 45 in Philippians 2; Joel 2:32 in Romans 10) — including Jesus within the identity of the one God, not importing a second god from paganism. And he had been the confession's persecutor (Galatians 1:13): you do not invent, at ruinous cost, the belief you were destroying.

∴ C

Therefore Paul did not invent Jesus's divinity — he received it. The divine confession stands behind Paul, not with him.

This does not by itself prove the confession true — that is the separate work of the resurrection evidence and Jesus's own words. Its narrower, firmer point: the divinity is early and received, not a late Pauline invention. The developed creedal vocabulary came later; the reality did not.

Why "Paul invented it" is the popular skeptic's move.

The claim has a serious scholarly ancestor — the history-of-religions school (Bousset), which derived the divine Kyrios from pagan mystery cults — and a popular descendant in Bart Ehrman's developmental thesis: an exalted human early, full divinity late. On either version Paul, our earliest writer, is cast as the engine of the change. The reply concedes the facts (Paul is the earliest writer; he never walked with the earthly Jesus) and refuses only the inference.

Defending the premises

Why each premise is more plausible than its denial

Support for P1 — Paul received the confession and presupposes the divinity

He says he received it, not invented it

Around AD 55 Paul reminds Corinth of something he insists he did not invent: "I delivered to you as of first importance what I also received" (1 Corinthians 15:3). He "delivered" (paredōka) what he "received" (parelabon) — the precise idiom for handing on authoritative tradition. Reckoning back from his conversion and early Jerusalem visit, the creed predates him, within roughly two to five years of the cross (Lüdemann: "not later than three years"; Dunn: "within months").

He assumes the divinity, never argues it

Across fifteen years of letters he presupposes Christ's deity rather than defending it — "in him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily" (Colossians) is stated as common ground, not a thesis. The pattern reaches behind his letters: the Philippians hymn (widely regarded as pre-Pauline; Fee argues Paul composed it — either way not argued into being) and the untranslated Aramaic Marana tha, a prayer of the first Judean church.

Support for P2 — Yahweh's own texts on Jesus, and Paul the persecutor

He puts Jesus inside Israel's God

Paul reaches not to the mystery cults but to the Old Testament's most monotheistic texts, applying to Jesus what is reserved for Yahweh: Isaiah 45's "every knee shall bow" in Philippians 2, Joel's "call on the name of the LORD" in Romans 10. This is Bauckham's divine identity — Jesus within the one God, not a second god. A pagan import would not reach for the chapter of Isaiah that most denies there is any god but the LORD (Hengel: more happened in Christology in the few years before Paul than in the seven centuries after).

Exhibit A: the persecutor

The supposed inventor had been the confession's enemy: "I persecuted the church of God violently and tried to destroy it" (Galatians 1:13). A crucified Messiah was a scandal — a hanged man was under God's curse (Deuteronomy 21:23). A movement's enemies do not defect to it, at ruinous cost, on the strength of nothing. Paul did not build the confession; he joined it — a confession whose divine shape, as the Aramaic Marana tha and the hymn behind Philippians show, already reached back behind him.

The hard part — objections, answered

The objections you will actually meet

Each of these gets its strongest form first, then the reply — and the hardest objection of all is flagged above.

The one that matters most: the developmental thesis — that Paul, our earliest and most influential writer, is the engine that turned a Galilean preacher into a divine Lord (Ehrman; the older history-of-religions school). The deep dive answers it at length — take it with you, not just the reply below.

"Paul is the earliest writer — so he's the one who invented the divine Jesus" Hard

Strongest form

Paul's letters are the oldest Christian documents, decades before the Gospels, and his Christology is sky-high. The simplest reading is that a rising belief in Jesus's divinity crested in Paul — he is the inventor, or at least the engine (Ehrman's developmental thesis).

Reply

Grant the facts and refuse the inference. Paul is the earliest writer — but he tells us he is quoting, not composing: he "received" the confession (1 Corinthians 15:3), and the material he quotes reaches behind him — the Aramaic Marana tha, a prayer of the first Judean church, and the Philippians hymn (composed or quoted, it is not argued into being). The earliest reachable layer already presupposes, on the divine-identity reading of Bauckham and Hurtado, a Christ included in God's identity and worship — so the burden is the harder one for the developmental thesis: it must explain why the deity is there at the bottom, not arriving late.

"The divine 'Lord' was borrowed from pagan mystery religions" Common

Strongest form

Calling Jesus Kyrios ("Lord") and worshipping him looks like the dying-and-rising lords of the Hellenistic mystery cults. Paul, at home in the Greek world, imported that pattern into a Jewish sect (Bousset, Kyrios Christos).

Reply

The direction is wrong. When Paul describes the exalted Jesus he reaches into the Old Testament's most fiercely monotheistic texts — Isaiah 45, Joel 2:32 — and applies to Jesus the words reserved for Yahweh alone. A monotheist importing a pagan god does not reach for the chapter that most insists there is no god beside the LORD. The matrix is Jewish monotheism, not paganism — which is why the derivation-from-paganism model has largely collapsed (Hengel).

"The creed Paul 'received' is about the resurrection, not the divinity" Trickier

Strongest form

1 Corinthians 15:3–5 confesses that Christ died, was buried, and was raised — it says nothing about him being divine. So "Paul received it" establishes an early resurrection belief, not an early divine confession. The deity could still be Paul's own addition.

Reply

Fair — and the argument does not rest the divinity on that creed. The received creed shows Paul's posture (he quotes rather than invents) and that the movement's core is pre-Pauline. The divine content is carried separately, and it is also pre-Pauline: the Aramaic Marana tha (prayer to the risen Lord, from the first Judean church) and the Philippians hymn, plus Paul's habit of applying Yahweh's texts to Jesus. The deity is early on independent grounds.

"Your best 'fullness of deity' verse is from Colossians — a disputed letter" Trickier

Strongest form

"In him the whole fullness of deity dwells bodily" is your clearest presupposed-deity text, but Colossians's Pauline authorship is contested — so it may be later and deutero-Pauline, which is exactly the developmental point.

Reply

Concede the authorship debate; the case does not stand or fall on Colossians. The same presuppose-not-argue pattern runs through the undisputed letters — Philippians (the hymn), 1 Corinthians (the reshaped Shema of 8:6; Marana tha), Romans (the Joel citation). Remove Colossians and the divine confession is still presupposed across the letters no one disputes Paul wrote.

"Even if Paul received it, that doesn't make it true" Hard

Strongest form

Suppose the divine confession really is pre-Pauline. All that shows is that people believed it early — and early belief can still be error. You have not shown Jesus is actually divine, only that the claim was made soon.

Reply

Correct, and the argument says so plainly. It establishes that Paul received rather than invented the confession — that it is early and pre-Pauline — not that it is true. Whether it is true is a separate question, answered on separate grounds: the resurrection evidence and Jesus's own claims, each treated in its own track. What this argument closes off is only the specific charge that Paul was the inventor.

"The 'pre-Pauline hymn' is speculative — maybe Paul wrote it" Trickier

Strongest form

You lean on the Philippians hymn being older than Paul's letters. But the pre-Pauline dating is a scholarly reconstruction, and Gordon Fee argues Paul composed it himself — in which case it is not evidence of anything earlier.

Reply

Granted, and the argument is built to survive it. Whether Paul quoted the hymn or composed it, the point holds: composed or received, the exalted-Christ conviction it expresses is not one Paul argues into being — he assumes it. And the load-bearing pre-Pauline evidence is the Aramaic Marana tha, whose early, first-community origin is not seriously contested — not the hymn's disputed dating.

    Skeptic
    "Christianity isn't really the religion of Jesus — it's the religion Paul built about him. A Galilean preacher got turned into a divine, pre-existent Lord by an educated Pharisee who never even met him. Paul invented the whole thing."
    One strong way to answer — in a 1 Peter 3:15 tone

    "I get why it looks that way — Paul is our earliest writer and his Christology is very high. And here's the kicker: Paul had been out arresting Christians for this exact belief. You don't invent, at the cost of everything, the thing you were trying to destroy. So no — he didn't invent the divine Jesus; he received it, and it changed him. Whether it's true is a further question — that's the resurrection and Jesus's own claims — but 'Paul made it up' doesn't survive contact with Paul."
